# REP Ref. Contracts

Reference note: contract law as applied to real estate purchase agreements, options, land contracts, and leases. Authored expository text (status: draft — needs licensed review). Covers Pearson VUE national outline IV.A–IV.D and PSI national outline domain V. Where a rule varies by state, the state portion of the exam governs.

## § 1 What a contract is and how contracts are classified

### § 1.1 Definition

A contract is a legally enforceable agreement between two or more competent parties to do, or to refrain from doing, some lawful act in exchange for consideration. "Enforceable" is the operative word: many promises are made every day, but only those that satisfy the essential elements described in § 2 give a party the right to ask a court for a remedy when the other side fails to perform. In real estate, the most common contracts a licensee encounters are the purchase agreement (sales contract), the listing agreement, the buyer representation agreement, the lease, the option, and the land contract. Each is analyzed with the same general rules.

### § 1.2 Express versus implied

An **express** contract is one whose terms are stated by the parties, orally or in writing. A written purchase agreement is an express contract; so is a spoken agreement to buy a lawnmower for fifty dollars. An **implied** contract (sometimes called implied-in-fact) arises from the conduct of the parties rather than from stated words. When a diner orders a meal at a restaurant, nothing is said about paying, yet an implied promise to pay exists. In real estate, implied contracts matter mostly in the agency context (see REP Ref. Agency), because a licensee's conduct can create an agency relationship the licensee never intended. Purchase contracts for real property are almost always express and, because of the Statute of Frauds (§ 2.6), almost always written.

### § 1.3 Bilateral versus unilateral

A **bilateral** contract is an exchange of promises: each party promises to perform, and each promise is consideration for the other. A purchase agreement is bilateral — the seller promises to convey and the buyer promises to pay. A **unilateral** contract is a promise in exchange for an act: only one party makes a promise, and the other party is not bound to do anything but becomes entitled to performance if he or she does the requested act. An option contract (§ 9) is the classic real estate example: the optionor promises to sell at a set price if the optionee chooses to buy; the optionee promises nothing. An open listing (see REP Ref. Brokerage Practice) is also unilateral — the seller promises a commission to whichever broker produces a buyer, but no broker promises to try.

Candidates often confuse "unilateral" with "one party signs." The test is who is obligated, not who signs. Both parties sign an option, but only the optionor is bound.

### § 1.4 Executed versus executory

An **executory** contract is one in which something remains to be performed by at least one party. A signed purchase agreement awaiting closing is executory. An **executed** contract is one in which all parties have fully performed. After closing, when the deed has been delivered and the price paid, the purchase agreement is executed. The word "executed" is also used loosely to mean "signed," which causes confusion: a contract that has merely been signed is executed in the everyday sense but is executory in the legal sense until performance is complete. Exam items generally use the legal sense.

### § 1.5 Valid, void, voidable, and unenforceable

These four terms describe a contract's legal status.

- **Valid**: all essential elements are present and the agreement is binding and enforceable on both parties.
- **Void**: the agreement has no legal effect from the start because an essential element is missing or the object is illegal. A contract to sell a property the "seller" does not own and cannot acquire, or a contract whose purpose is a crime, is void. Neither party can enforce it; there is nothing to enforce.
- **Voidable**: the contract is valid and binding on one party but may be rescinded (cancelled) at the election of the other. Contracts entered into by minors, or induced by fraud, misrepresentation, duress, or undue influence, are typically voidable by the injured or protected party. Until that party elects to void it, the contract remains in force, and the protected party may instead choose to affirm (ratify) it.
- **Unenforceable**: the agreement may appear valid but a court will not compel performance, usually because of a procedural defect such as the absence of a required writing under the Statute of Frauds, or the running of the statute of limitations. The parties may still voluntarily perform, and if they do, the performance stands.

Example: Jordan, age 17, signs a contract to buy a condominium. The contract is voidable by Jordan (the minor), not void, and not voidable by the adult seller. If Jordan turns 18 and continues to make payments, Jordan has ratified the contract and it becomes fully binding.

## § 2 Essential elements of a valid contract

### § 2.1 Overview

A valid contract requires: (1) competent parties; (2) mutual assent, shown by an offer and an acceptance; (3) consideration; (4) a lawful object; and, for most real estate contracts, (5) a writing signed by the party to be charged. Some texts fold "reality of consent" (freedom from fraud, mistake, duress, and undue influence) into mutual assent; others list it separately. This note treats it as part of mutual assent (§ 2.3).

### § 2.2 Competent parties (capacity)

Each party must have the legal capacity to contract. Capacity is generally lacking for minors (persons under the age of majority set by state law), persons adjudicated mentally incompetent, and persons so intoxicated that they cannot understand the transaction. Corporations, partnerships, LLCs, and trusts contract through authorized officers, partners, managers, or trustees; a licensee dealing with an entity should confirm the signer's authority. A contract with a minor is voidable by the minor (§ 1.5). A contract with a person previously declared incompetent by a court is generally void.

### § 2.3 Mutual assent — offer and acceptance

Mutual assent (sometimes called "meeting of the minds") exists when one party makes a definite offer and the other party accepts it without changing its terms. An **offer** is a promise, communicated to the offeree, to do something on stated terms. An **acceptance** is an unconditional agreement to those terms, communicated back to the offeror. Consent must be genuine: assent obtained through fraud, intentional or negligent misrepresentation, duress (threats), undue influence (abuse of a position of trust), or a mutual mistake about a material fact is defective, and the resulting contract is typically voidable by the victim. The mechanics of offers and acceptances are detailed in § 3.

### § 2.4 Consideration

Consideration is what each party gives up in exchange for the other's promise: money, property, a promise, a service, or forbearance from doing something one has a right to do. It need not be money and need not be equal in value — courts generally do not weigh the adequacy of consideration in the absence of fraud. In a purchase agreement, the buyer's promise to pay the price is consideration for the seller's promise to convey, and vice versa. Earnest money is *not* the consideration for a purchase agreement (§ 5.1), although it is often mistaken for it. "Love and affection" (called "good" consideration) supports a gift deed but does not create an enforceable contract; contracts require "valuable" consideration.

### § 2.5 Lawful object

The purpose of the contract must be legal. A contract whose object is illegal — for example, an agreement between competing brokers to fix commission rates, which the cached Sherman Act prohibits, or an agreement to violate zoning law — is void. If part of a contract is illegal and can be separated from the rest, courts may enforce the lawful part (severability).

### § 2.6 A writing where required — the Statute of Frauds

Every state has a statute, descended from a 17th-century English law, requiring certain classes of contracts to be in writing and signed by the party against whom enforcement is sought. Contracts for the sale of real property, and (in most states) leases longer than one year, fall within it. An oral agreement to sell land is therefore generally unenforceable (§ 1.5), though not void: if both parties choose to perform, the conveyance is valid. The writing need not be a single formal document; a signed memorandum that identifies the parties, the property, the price, and the essential terms usually suffices. Exact thresholds and exceptions (for example, partial performance) vary by state; the state portion governs.

The Statute of Frauds does not usually require the *listing agreement* to be in writing as a matter of general contract law, but many state license laws independently require written listing and buyer agreements. Again, the state portion governs.

### § 2.7 Electronic signatures

Federal law provides that a signature, contract, or other record relating to a transaction may not be denied legal effect solely because it is in electronic form, and that if a law requires a signature, an electronic signature satisfies the requirement. The governing federal text is the cached statute 15 U.S.C. §§ 7001–7006 (E-SIGN). Items on e-signatures should cite that statute directly, not this note. Most states have also adopted a uniform electronic transactions act with similar effect; the state portion governs any state-specific rule.

## § 3 Offer mechanics

### § 3.1 Requirements of an offer

An offer must be definite enough that a court could enforce it, must show a present intent to be bound, and must be communicated to the offeree. An advertisement or listing is ordinarily an invitation to make offers, not an offer itself; the buyer who submits a purchase agreement is the offeror, and the seller becomes the offeree.

### § 3.2 Acceptance and the mirror-image rule

To form a contract, the acceptance must match the offer exactly. Any change to price, closing date, personal property included, contingencies, or other terms is not an acceptance; it is a **counteroffer**, which operates as a rejection of the original offer and creates a new offer running the other way. Once a counteroffer is made, the original offer is dead and cannot later be accepted unless the original offeror renews it.

Example: Priya offers $400,000 with closing in 45 days. The seller, Marcus, signs the contract but changes the closing date to 30 days and initials the change. There is no contract. Marcus has rejected Priya's offer and made a counteroffer. Priya may accept, reject, or counter. If Priya says nothing and Marcus later decides 45 days is fine, he cannot simply "accept" Priya's original offer; he must make a new offer that Priya may accept.

### § 3.3 Communication of acceptance

An acceptance is generally effective only when it is communicated to the offeror (or the offeror's agent). The contract is formed at that moment, not when the offeree signs. Real estate forms commonly specify how and when notice is effective — for example, on delivery to the listing licensee, or on electronic transmission — and the form controls. An offeree's silence is not acceptance.

### § 3.4 Revocation

The offeror may withdraw (revoke) an offer at any time before it has been accepted and the acceptance communicated, even if the offer stated it would remain open for a specified period. The exception is an option (§ 9), where the offeree has paid consideration to keep the offer open. A revocation is effective when it is communicated to the offeree.

Example: Tuesday morning a buyer submits an offer stating "this offer expires Thursday at 5 p.m." Tuesday evening the buyer's agent tells the listing agent the buyer has withdrawn. The offer is revoked; the seller cannot accept it on Wednesday even though the stated deadline has not passed.

### § 3.5 Lapse and other terminations of an offer

An offer terminates without acceptance by: (a) its own expiration date passing; (b) lapse of a reasonable time where no date is stated; (c) rejection by the offeree; (d) counteroffer (§ 3.2); (e) revocation (§ 3.4); (f) death or incapacity of either party before acceptance; or (g) destruction of the subject property or supervening illegality before acceptance.

### § 3.6 Multiple offers and counteroffers — practice guidance

A seller who receives several offers may accept one, reject all, or counter one or more. A seller should be cautioned against signing counteroffers to more than one buyer at the same time on identical terms: if two buyers accept, the seller may have contracted to sell the same property twice and will be in breach with one of them. Where forms permit, a multiple-counteroffer procedure typically makes each counter conditional on the seller's later written confirmation after a buyer accepts, so that only one binding contract can result. A listing licensee has a duty to present all offers to the seller promptly (until closing, unless the seller has waived it in writing, in many states), and generally may not disclose the terms of one buyer's offer to another buyer without the seller's authorization. Whether the existence of other offers may be disclosed at all is a matter of state law and brokerage policy; the state portion governs. A buyer's licensee should explain that a seller is under no obligation to accept the highest offer or any offer, and should help the buyer decide on price, terms, and contingencies with that in mind.

## § 4 The purchase agreement

### § 4.1 Function and typical contents

The purchase agreement (also called a sales contract, purchase and sale agreement, or offer to purchase) is the bilateral, executory contract that governs the transaction from acceptance to closing. It typically identifies the parties and the property; states the price and how it will be paid (cash, financing terms); lists personal property included or excluded and fixtures excluded; sets the earnest money amount and holder; sets a closing date and possession date; allocates closing costs and prorations; specifies the type of deed and the condition of title to be delivered; contains contingencies; states the remedy for default; and addresses risk of loss between contract and closing. Because it is the document that determines everyone's rights until closing, licensees are expected to know how to fill in a form contract but not to draft one — drafting original contract language for others is generally the practice of law (see REP Ref. Brokerage Practice).

### § 4.2 Equitable title

Once a purchase agreement is signed by both parties, the buyer holds **equitable title**: an interest in the property that the buyer can enforce in equity even though legal title remains with the seller until the deed is delivered at closing. This is why the buyer typically has an insurable interest before closing and why a seller who tries to sell to someone else after signing can be compelled to convey to the first buyer (§ 7.2).

### § 4.3 Time is of the essence

A "time is of the essence" clause makes stated deadlines strict: a party who misses a deadline is in breach and the other party may pursue remedies or, if the contract so provides, treat the contract as terminated. Without such a clause, courts generally allow performance within a reasonable time after a stated date. Licensees should treat all contract dates as firm regardless, and should obtain written extensions (amendments, § 11.2) when a deadline cannot be met.

### § 4.4 Assignment and novation

Unless the contract prohibits it, a party may **assign** its rights under a purchase agreement to a third party. The assignor typically remains secondarily liable for performance unless released. Many forms prohibit assignment without the seller's consent, and contracts that depend on the buyer's personal creditworthiness (owner financing, for example) are often not assignable. **Novation** is different: it is a new contract substituted for the old one, with the consent of all parties, that releases the original party entirely. Assignment transfers rights; novation replaces a party and extinguishes the original obligation.

Example: Investor A signs a contract to buy a duplex, then assigns the contract to Investor B for a $5,000 fee. Absent a release, if B fails to close, the seller can still look to A. If instead the seller, A, and B sign an agreement substituting B for A and releasing A, that is a novation, and A is off the hook.

## § 5 Earnest money

### § 5.1 What it is and what it is not

Earnest money (a good-faith deposit) is a sum the buyer delivers with an offer to show serious intent. It is not required to form a contract and is not the consideration for the contract (the mutual promises are; § 2.4). Its amount is negotiable. If the sale closes, it is credited to the buyer's funds at closing. If the buyer defaults, the contract often provides that the seller may keep it as liquidated damages (§ 7.4). If the buyer properly terminates under a contingency, it is returned to the buyer.

### § 5.2 Handling in general terms

Earnest money belongs to neither the buyer nor the seller alone while the contract is pending; it is held in trust by a neutral party — commonly the listing broker, a title or escrow company, or an attorney — under the terms of the contract. A broker who holds earnest money must deposit it in a trust (escrow) account within the time state law prescribes, may not mix it with the brokerage's own operating funds (commingling), and may not use it for any purpose other than as the contract and the parties direct (using it otherwise is conversion). When buyer and seller disagree about who is entitled to the deposit after a failed transaction, a broker generally may not simply decide; typical state procedures involve holding the funds pending a written agreement of the parties, mediation, a court order, or a statutory interpleader process. Deposit deadlines, procedures, and penalties vary by state; the state portion governs. Trust account concepts are treated in more depth in REP Ref. Brokerage Practice.

## § 6 Contingencies

### § 6.1 Concept

A contingency (condition) is a term making one party's duty to perform depend on the occurrence of a stated event. If the event does not occur, the party protected by the contingency may terminate without being in breach, and the earnest money is usually returned. A contingency should state what must happen, by when, who must give notice, what form the notice takes, and what happens if the deadline passes without notice (some contingencies are deemed waived if the buyer says nothing; others are deemed to have failed). The party protected by a contingency may generally waive it. Contracts subject to unresolved contingencies are sometimes described as "pending" or "under contract with contingencies."

### § 6.2 Financing contingency

Makes the buyer's obligation contingent on obtaining a loan of a described type, amount, rate, and term by a stated date. If the buyer applies diligently and is denied, the buyer may terminate and recover the deposit. A buyer who fails to apply, or who applies for a different loan than described, has generally not satisfied the good-faith requirement and may lose the deposit. A cash buyer needs no financing contingency.

### § 6.3 Inspection (due diligence) contingency

Gives the buyer a period to have the property inspected and, depending on the form, either to terminate for any reason, to terminate only for defects above a stated threshold, or to request repairs which the seller may accept or refuse. A request for repairs is often treated as a proposal that the seller may accept, reject, or counter; the buyer must then decide whether to proceed. Inspection contingencies protect buyers from taking a property "as is" without knowledge.

### § 6.4 Appraisal contingency

Allows the buyer to terminate (or renegotiate) if the lender's appraisal comes in below the purchase price, because the lender will base the loan on the lower of price or appraised value and the buyer would otherwise have to bring the difference in cash. Some forms fold this into the financing contingency; others treat it separately so that a buyer can waive one and keep the other.

Example: Price $350,000, loan 80% of value. Appraisal comes in at $340,000. The lender will lend 80% × $340,000 = $272,000, not $280,000. Without an appraisal contingency, the buyer must find an additional $8,000 or be in default. With one, the buyer may terminate or ask the seller to reduce the price.

### § 6.5 Sale of buyer's home contingency

Makes the purchase contingent on the buyer closing the sale of another property by a stated date. Sellers accepting such a contingency often insist on an escape (kick-out) clause: the seller may continue to market the property and, if another acceptable offer arrives, give the first buyer a short period to remove the contingency or step aside.

### § 6.6 Other common conditions

Contracts may also be contingent on the buyer's review of condominium or association documents, on a satisfactory survey, on the seller delivering marketable title, on a satisfactory well or septic test, or on the buyer's attorney's approval. Each works the same way: it is a condition, not a promise, and failure of the condition excuses performance rather than causing a breach.

## § 7 Breach and remedies

### § 7.1 Breach defined

A breach is a failure, without legal excuse, to perform a contractual duty when due. A **material** breach goes to the heart of the bargain (refusing to close, failing to deliver title) and excuses the other party's performance. A **minor** breach (a day's delay where time is not of the essence) entitles the injured party to damages but not to walk away. An **anticipatory** breach occurs when a party announces in advance that it will not perform; the other party may treat that as an immediate breach.

### § 7.2 Specific performance

A court order compelling the breaching party to perform the contract as written. Because every parcel of land is considered unique, money damages are often inadequate for a buyer, and specific performance is a traditional remedy for a buyer against a defaulting seller. Sellers may also seek it against buyers in some states, though courts are less inclined to compel a buyer to pay because money damages are usually adequate. Specific performance is an equitable remedy and is discretionary.

### § 7.3 Rescission

Cancellation of the contract and restoration of the parties to their pre-contract positions (each returns what it received). Rescission is available for material breach, fraud, misrepresentation, mutual mistake, and similar defects in formation. A **mutual rescission** is simply an agreement by both parties to cancel (§ 8.2).

### § 7.4 Liquidated damages

A sum agreed in advance as the measure of damages if a party defaults, most commonly the buyer's earnest money. Courts enforce liquidated damages clauses when actual damages would be hard to estimate at the time of contracting and the amount is a reasonable forecast rather than a penalty. Many forms make forfeiture of the deposit the seller's *sole* remedy for buyer default; others let the seller elect between keeping the deposit and suing for actual damages or specific performance. The form controls.

### § 7.5 Compensatory (actual) damages

Money to put the injured party in the position it would have occupied had the contract been performed. For a seller whose buyer defaults, the measure is often the difference between the contract price and the price obtained on resale, plus carrying costs. For a buyer, it may include lost bargain, inspection and appraisal fees, and other out-of-pocket costs. Punitive damages are generally unavailable for simple breach of contract; they may be available for fraud.

### § 7.6 Mitigation

The injured party must take reasonable steps to reduce its loss. A seller whose buyer defaults must make reasonable efforts to resell rather than let the property sit and then claim the full price as damages. A landlord whose tenant abandons generally must try to re-let (the rule varies by state; the state portion governs).

## § 8 Termination and discharge of contracts

### § 8.1 Performance

The normal end of a contract: both sides do what they promised, and the contract becomes executed (§ 1.4). Substantial performance — performance with only trivial deviations — usually discharges the performing party, subject to an offset for the deficiency.

### § 8.2 Agreement of the parties

The parties may cancel by mutual rescission, replace the contract by novation (§ 4.4), or agree to an accord and satisfaction (acceptance of a different performance in full settlement). Termination by agreement should be documented in writing, with express instructions for release of any earnest money.

### § 8.3 Impossibility and related doctrines

A contract is discharged if performance becomes objectively impossible through no fault of the party — the building is destroyed by fire before closing (subject to the risk-of-loss clause), the seller dies before signing a deed that only the seller can sign, or a change in law makes the promised use illegal. Mere difficulty or increased expense is not impossibility. Some jurisdictions also recognize frustration of purpose or commercial impracticability; treatment varies by state.

### § 8.4 Operation of law

Contracts may be discharged by legal rules operating independently of the parties' wishes: the running of the statute of limitations (making the contract unenforceable), bankruptcy of a party (which may permit rejection of executory contracts), a material alteration of the writing by one party without the other's consent, or a merger of the contract into a later instrument (the terms of a purchase agreement generally merge into the deed at closing except where the contract says they survive).

### § 8.5 Breach

Material breach by one party discharges the other party's duty to perform and gives rise to the remedies in § 7. A party who is itself in material breach cannot demand performance from the other side.

## § 9 Options and rights of first refusal

### § 9.1 Option contract

An option is a unilateral contract (§ 1.3) in which the owner (optionor) gives another (optionee), for consideration, the right to buy (or lease) the property at a fixed price within a fixed period. The optionee is never obligated to buy; the optionor is obligated to sell if the optionee exercises within the term. Because the optionee has paid for the right, the optionor cannot revoke during the option period, unlike an ordinary offer (§ 3.4). The option fee is generally kept by the optionor whether or not the option is exercised, unless the contract provides that it applies to the price on exercise. Exercise must strictly follow the option's terms (written notice, within the period). On exercise, the option ripens into a bilateral purchase contract. Options are commonly recorded to protect the optionee against a sale to a third party. An option is an interest in real estate that is generally assignable unless the agreement says otherwise.

Example: Dana pays $10,000 for a two-year option to buy a lot at $250,000. Eighteen months later the lot is worth $320,000; Dana exercises and buys at $250,000. Had values fallen, Dana could simply let the option expire and lose only the $10,000.

### § 9.2 Right of first refusal

A right of first refusal (preemptive right) obligates the owner, before selling to a third party, to offer the property to the holder on the same terms the third party has offered. Unlike an option, it fixes no price and gives the holder no power to compel a sale; it is triggered only when the owner decides to sell. It commonly appears in leases and in condominium or cooperative documents.

### § 9.3 Lease with option

A lease that grants the tenant an option to purchase during or at the end of the term. Sometimes part of the rent is credited toward the price on exercise; if so the contract should say so expressly. The lease and the option are analyzed separately: the lease is bilateral, the option unilateral.

## § 10 Land contracts (contracts for deed)

### § 10.1 Structure

A land contract — also called a contract for deed, installment contract, or agreement for deed — is a financing arrangement in which the buyer (vendee) takes possession and pays the price in installments to the seller (vendor), who retains legal title until the price (or an agreed portion) is paid, at which point the vendor delivers a deed. The vendee holds equitable title during the term, is usually responsible for taxes, insurance, and maintenance, and may lose the property on default under procedures that vary widely by state (some states treat default like mortgage foreclosure; others permit forfeiture of payments after notice). Vendees should record the contract or a memorandum of it to protect against the vendor's creditors and later purchasers. Because the vendor keeps title, a vendor who encumbers or loses the property (for example, through the vendor's own mortgage default) can leave the vendee unable to obtain clear title even after full payment; that is the principal risk to buyers.

### § 10.2 Contrast with a purchase agreement

A purchase agreement is a short-lived executory contract that ends at closing when title passes. A land contract is a long-term executory contract in which possession passes at the start and title passes at the end. Both give the buyer equitable title; only the land contract is also a financing device.

## § 11 Addenda and amendments

### § 11.1 Addendum

An addendum is a document attached to and made part of the contract at the time it is formed (or at the time of a counteroffer), adding or modifying terms. Examples: a lead-based paint disclosure addendum for pre-1978 housing (federal requirements are in the cached lead paint statute), a financing addendum, a sale-of-buyer's-home addendum, or an addendum listing personal property included. An addendum is part of the original bargain, not a later change.

### § 11.2 Amendment

An amendment is a change agreed to by both parties *after* the contract has been formed — extending the closing date, reducing the price after inspection, changing the earnest money holder. An amendment requires the same mutual assent as the original contract and, under the Statute of Frauds, should be in writing and signed by both parties. Unilateral changes to a signed contract are ineffective (and may be a breach or alteration under § 8.4). The distinction candidates should remember: addenda are attached at formation; amendments modify an existing contract.

## § 12 Leases as contracts

### § 12.1 Nature of a lease

A lease is both a contract and a conveyance: it transfers to the tenant (lessee) a possessory interest in real property — a leasehold estate — for a term, in exchange for rent, and it sets out the parties' mutual promises. The landlord (lessor) retains a reversionary interest (the right to possession when the lease ends). Leases are analyzed under the same elements as any contract (§ 2), and leases longer than one year generally must be in writing under the Statute of Frauds (§ 2.6). Statutory landlord–tenant rules — security deposit limits, habitability, notice periods, eviction procedure — vary by state; the state portion governs.

### § 12.2 Leasehold estates

- **Estate for years**: a lease for any fixed term (a week, one year, 99 years) with a definite beginning and end. It ends automatically at the end of the term without notice.
- **Periodic estate (estate from period to period)**: a lease that renews automatically for successive periods (month to month is the most common) until either party gives the notice required by the lease or state law. Arises expressly or when a tenant holds over after an estate for years and the landlord accepts rent.
- **Estate at will**: possession with the owner's consent for no fixed term, terminable by either party at any time (many states now require notice). Rare in practice.
- **Estate at sufferance**: the position of a tenant who remains after the lease has ended without the landlord's consent. The tenant is not a trespasser (entry was lawful) but has no right to remain; the landlord may evict.

### § 12.3 Types of leases by rent structure

- **Gross lease**: the tenant pays a fixed rent and the landlord pays the operating expenses (taxes, insurance, maintenance). Typical of residential leases.
- **Net lease**: the tenant pays base rent plus some or all operating expenses. In a "triple net" (NNN) lease the tenant pays taxes, insurance, and maintenance in addition to rent. Common in commercial and single-tenant retail leases.
- **Percentage lease**: rent is a base amount plus a percentage of the tenant's gross sales above a stated breakpoint. Used for retail tenants in shopping centers.
- **Graduated (step-up) lease**: rent increases at scheduled intervals by fixed amounts or percentages.
- **Index (escalation) lease**: rent adjusts according to a published index such as a consumer price index.
- **Ground lease**: a long-term lease of land only, on which the tenant builds; improvements usually revert to the landowner at the end of the term.

Example (percentage lease): A boutique pays $3,000 per month base rent plus 5% of monthly gross sales above $60,000. In a month with $90,000 in sales, rent is $3,000 + 5% × ($90,000 − $60,000) = $3,000 + $1,500 = $4,500.

### § 12.4 Assignment versus sublease

When a tenant transfers the *entire* remaining term to another, that is an **assignment**; the assignee becomes the landlord's tenant, and the original tenant remains liable on the lease unless released. When a tenant transfers *less* than the entire interest — a portion of the space, or the whole space for part of the remaining term — that is a **sublease**; the subtenant is the original tenant's tenant, pays rent to the original tenant, and has no direct relationship with the landlord. The original tenant remains fully liable to the landlord in both cases absent a release. Most leases require the landlord's written consent to either.

### § 12.5 Termination of leases

A lease ends by expiration of the term, by proper notice in a periodic tenancy, by agreement (surrender and acceptance), by breach and eviction (actual eviction), by the landlord's interference with possession so severe that the tenant is justified in leaving (constructive eviction), by destruction of the premises (depending on the lease and state law), or by operation of law such as condemnation. The sale of the property does not terminate a lease; the new owner takes subject to existing tenancies. Death of either party generally does not terminate an estate for years.

## § 13 Common confusions summarized

### § 13.1 Quick distinctions

- *Void vs. voidable*: void never had effect; voidable is valid until the protected party cancels.
- *Unenforceable vs. void*: unenforceable is a valid agreement a court will not enforce (usually a missing writing); performance by the parties still stands.
- *Counteroffer vs. acceptance*: any change is a counteroffer and kills the original offer.
- *Revocation vs. rejection*: the offeror revokes; the offeree rejects.
- *Option vs. right of first refusal*: option fixes a price and can force a sale; a right of first refusal only matches a third-party offer.
- *Assignment vs. novation*: assignment transfers rights and leaves the assignor secondarily liable; novation substitutes a party and releases the original.
- *Assignment vs. sublease*: assignment transfers the whole remaining term; sublease transfers less.
- *Addendum vs. amendment*: addendum at formation; amendment after.
- *Earnest money vs. consideration*: the mutual promises are the consideration; earnest money is evidence of good faith and a source of liquidated damages.
- *Executed (signed) vs. executed (performed)*: exam usage is "fully performed."
