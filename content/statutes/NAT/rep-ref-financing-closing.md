# REP Ref. Financing & Closing

Authored reference note for the national portion of the salesperson and broker examinations. Coverage: Pearson VUE VII.A, VII.B, VII.D; PSI IV.A, IV.B, and the closing and foreclosure parts of PSI IX. Federal disclosure and anti-discrimination rules are not restated: items on RESPA, TILA/Reg Z, TRID timing, ECOA and HOEPA must cite the cached statute text. Status: draft, authored 2026-09-08, needs licensed review. Figures marked "as of 2026; verify" change periodically and must be checked against the current HUD, VA, USDA and FHFA publications before an item relies on them.

## § 1 Financing instruments and security theories

### § 1.1 The promissory note

The promissory note is the borrower's written, unconditional promise to repay a definite sum with interest on stated terms. It is the evidence of the debt. It names the maker (borrower) and payee (lender), the principal, the rate, the payment schedule, the maturity date, and the consequences of late payment or default. A note is a negotiable instrument the lender can sell or assign, which is what makes the secondary market (§ 7) possible. Only the borrower signs it, and it is not recorded.

### § 1.2 Security instruments: mortgage and deed of trust

A note alone is unsecured. To give the lender a claim on the property, the borrower also signs a security instrument pledging the real estate as collateral. A mortgage is a two-party instrument between borrower (mortgagor) and lender (mortgagee) that creates a lien. A deed of trust (trust deed) is a three-party instrument in which the borrower (trustor) conveys bare legal title to a neutral third party (trustee) who holds it for the lender (beneficiary) until the debt is paid. Which instrument is customary varies by state; the state portion governs. Either instrument is recorded in the county where the land lies so the lender's interest is public and takes its place in lien priority (§ 3.9).

### § 1.3 Lien theory, title theory and intermediate theory

In a lien-theory state the borrower keeps legal and equitable title; the lender holds only a lien and must foreclose, usually through the courts, to reach the property. In a title-theory state the borrower conveys legal title to the lender or a trustee for the life of the loan and keeps equitable title and possession; the defeasance clause (§ 2.4) obliges the lender to return title when the debt is paid. An intermediate-theory state follows lien theory until default, when title shifts to the lender. The tested consequence is procedural: title-theory and deed-of-trust arrangements tend to permit faster non-judicial foreclosure, while lien-theory mortgages tend to require judicial foreclosure. The applicable theory varies by state; the state portion governs.

### § 1.4 Naming the parties

The party who gives the instrument ends in "-or"; the party who receives it ends in "-ee". The mortgagor is the borrower who gives the mortgage; the mortgagee is the lender who receives it. Under a deed of trust the trustor is the borrower, the trustee is the title holder, and the beneficiary is the lender. Under a contract for deed (§ 5.7) the vendor sells and the vendee buys. Candidates reverse mortgagor and mortgagee because the borrower "gets a mortgage" in everyday speech; on the examination the borrower gives the mortgage. Pledging property as security while keeping possession of it is called hypothecation.

## § 2 Common clauses

### § 2.1 Acceleration clause

The acceleration clause lets the lender declare the entire unpaid balance due at once upon a defined default: missed payments, unpaid taxes, lapsed insurance, or an unauthorized transfer. Without it the lender could sue only for each installment as it fell due. Acceleration is the step that precedes foreclosure.

### § 2.2 Alienation clause (due-on-sale)

The alienation or due-on-sale clause lets the lender accelerate if the borrower transfers title without consent, so a buyer cannot assume a below-market loan without re-underwriting. A loan without an enforceable alienation clause is freely assumable. The Garn–St Germain Act of 1982 made these clauses generally enforceable but exempts transfers such as to a spouse or child, on the death of a joint tenant, or into the borrower's living trust. FHA and VA loans are assumable with approval of the new borrower (§ 6.2, § 6.3). A buyer who assumes a loan becomes liable and the seller stays secondarily liable unless released (novation); a buyer who takes title "subject to" the loan makes the payments but is not personally liable on the note.

### § 2.3 Prepayment clause and penalty

The prepayment clause states whether and on what terms the borrower may pay early. A prepayment penalty is a fee for early payoff, usually a percentage of the balance or a stated number of months' interest (computation in REP Ref. Real Estate Math § 5.10). Federal rules restrict penalties on most residential loans (see cached TILA/Reg Z); FHA, VA and USDA loans have none. An "or more" clause permits extra principal payments without penalty.

### § 2.4 Defeasance clause

The defeasance clause defeats the lender's interest when the debt is fully paid: the lender must release the lien or cause the trustee to reconvey title. It guarantees the borrower clear title at payoff and is the hallmark of a title-theory instrument, though release provisions appear in all modern security instruments.

### § 2.5 Subordination clause

Priority is normally set by recording date (§ 3.9). A subordination clause or agreement lets a lien holder voluntarily accept a lower priority than a later lien. Typical uses: a seller carrying a purchase-money loan on land subordinates to a future construction loan so the buyer can build; a second lender agrees to stay junior when the first loan is refinanced.

### § 2.6 Release, satisfaction and reconveyance

When a mortgage is paid in full the lender executes a satisfaction (release) of mortgage, which is recorded to clear the lien. Under a deed of trust the beneficiary directs the trustee to execute a deed of reconveyance returning legal title to the trustor. A partial release clause (§ 5.2) releases individual parcels from a blanket loan as portions of the debt are paid.

### § 2.7 Other frequently tested provisions

Covenants require the borrower to pay taxes and assessments, keep the improvements insured with the lender as loss payee, maintain the property and avoid waste. An assignment-of-rents clause lets the lender collect rents from an income property after default. An escalation clause raises the rate on stated events (distinct from ARM index adjustments). An exculpatory or non-recourse clause limits the lender to the collateral so no deficiency judgment can be taken against the borrower personally. A power-of-sale clause authorizes non-judicial foreclosure where state law permits (§ 3.3).

## § 3 Default, foreclosure and alternatives

### § 3.1 Default and pre-foreclosure alternatives

Default is any breach of the note or security instrument; nonpayment is the usual one. Alternatives to foreclosure include forbearance (temporary suspension or reduction of payments), a repayment plan, a loan modification (permanent change of rate, term or balance), a deed in lieu (§ 3.7) and a short sale (§ 3.8). Servicer loss-mitigation duties are RESPA/Reg X rules; cite the cached RESPA text.

### § 3.2 Judicial foreclosure

Judicial foreclosure is a lawsuit: the lender files a complaint, everyone with a recorded interest is named and served, the court enters judgment for the amount owed, and a sheriff or court officer sells the property at public auction, often subject to court confirmation. Proceeds pay costs of sale, then liens in priority, with any surplus to the borrower. It is the required or default method in lien-theory states and is slower and costlier than non-judicial foreclosure. Whether it is required varies by state; the state portion governs.

### § 3.3 Non-judicial foreclosure (power of sale)

Where the instrument contains a power-of-sale clause and state law allows, the trustee (deed of trust) or mortgagee may sell without a court proceeding after statutory notices: typically a recorded notice of default, a reinstatement period during which the borrower can cure the arrears, then a published and posted notice of sale, then public auction. The trustee delivers a trustee's deed to the high bidder. It is faster and cheaper, but some states bar or limit deficiency judgments after a power-of-sale foreclosure. Availability and notice periods vary by state; the state portion governs.

### § 3.4 Strict foreclosure

The court sets a deadline for the borrower to pay in full; if it passes, title vests in the lender with no sale. Because the lender keeps any equity, most states have abolished or limited it; a few New England states retain it. It is tested chiefly as a definition to distinguish from the sale-based methods.

### § 3.5 Deficiency judgments

If the sale brings less than the debt plus costs, the shortfall is a deficiency, and a deficiency judgment is a personal judgment against the borrower for it. Many states restrict deficiencies after non-judicial sales, on purchase-money loans for owner-occupied homes, or by measuring the deficiency against fair market value rather than the auction price. A non-recourse clause (§ 2.7) waives it by contract. Availability varies by state; the state portion governs.

### § 3.6 Redemption

The equitable right of redemption exists everywhere: before the sale the borrower (or a junior lien holder) may stop the foreclosure by paying the full accelerated debt plus costs. Reinstatement, where allowed, is narrower: paying only the arrears and fees within a statutory window. The statutory right of redemption, which exists only where a statute creates it, lets the borrower reclaim the property for a fixed period after the sale by paying the sale price plus interest and costs; where it exists the purchaser gets a certificate of sale and receives a deed only when the period ends. Whether a post-sale period exists, and its length, varies by state; the state portion governs.

### § 3.7 Deed in lieu of foreclosure

A voluntary conveyance from borrower to lender in exchange for cancellation of the debt. It avoids the cost and publicity of foreclosure, but because there is no sale, junior liens are not extinguished; the lender takes title subject to them and therefore orders a title search first. The borrower may have cancellation-of-debt income; refer tax questions to a tax professional.

### § 3.8 Short sale

A sale for less than the total secured debt, with the lender's written consent to accept the net proceeds in full or partial satisfaction. The lender must approve the price and settlement statement, and its approval letter states whether the deficiency is waived or reserved. Short sales take longer because of lender review; a listing licensee must disclose that the sale is subject to lender approval, must not misrepresent the property's status, and presents offers to the seller, who remains the owner.

### § 3.9 Lien priority and distribution of proceeds

Priority is generally "first in time, first in right," fixed by recording date, except that real property tax and special assessment liens come first regardless of date, and in some states mechanics' liens relate back to the start of work. A subordination agreement changes priority by contract. Foreclosure proceeds pay (1) costs of sale, (2) liens in order of priority, (3) the remainder to the borrower. Foreclosure of a senior lien wipes unpaid junior liens off the title, which is why junior lenders get notice and may bid or redeem; foreclosure of a junior lien leaves a senior lien in place and the purchaser takes subject to it.

## § 4 Loan types by repayment structure

### § 4.1 Fixed-rate, fully amortizing loan

The rate never changes and a level payment pays the month's interest first, with the remainder reducing principal, so the interest share falls and the principal share rises until the balance reaches zero at maturity. Common terms are 30, 20 and 15 years. A loan whose payment also collects one-twelfth of annual taxes and insurance into escrow is a budget mortgage; the total is PITI (§ 8.4). Arithmetic is in REP Ref. Real Estate Math § 5.5–§ 5.8.

### § 4.2 Adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM)

The rate resets periodically by formula: fully indexed rate = index + margin. The index is a published market rate outside the lender's control (today commonly a SOFR average or the one-year constant-maturity Treasury yield; LIBOR was retired in 2023). The margin is the lender's fixed spread, constant for the loan's life. The adjustment period states how often the rate changes: a 5/1 or 5/6 ARM is fixed for five years, then adjusts yearly or every six months. Caps limit changes: an initial-adjustment cap, a periodic cap and a lifetime cap, written for example 2/2/5 or 5/2/5. A payment cap instead of a rate cap can leave the payment below the interest due, and the shortfall is added to principal: negative amortization. A discounted introductory ("teaser") rate produces payment shock at the first adjustment.

### § 4.3 Term loan, partially amortized loan and balloon payment

A term (straight) loan has interest-only payments with the whole principal due at maturity. A partially amortized loan has payments computed on a long schedule (say 30 years) but a shorter maturity (say 7 years), so a balloon payment of the remaining balance falls due at maturity, exposing the borrower to refinancing risk. Federal ability-to-repay rules limit balloons in the qualified-mortgage market (see cached TILA/Reg Z).

### § 4.4 Interest-only period loans

A modern interest-only loan allows interest-only payments for an initial period (often 5 to 10 years), then re-amortizes the full balance over the remaining term, producing a sharp payment increase; until then equity grows only through appreciation.

### § 4.5 Graduated-payment and growing-equity mortgages; buydowns

A graduated-payment mortgage starts with payments below the fully amortizing amount and raises them on a schedule for several years; early payments may not cover interest, causing negative amortization. A growing-equity mortgage also steps payments up, but starts at the fully amortizing level and applies every increase to principal, retiring the loan early. A buydown is a lump sum paid at closing (often by a seller or builder) to reduce the borrower's rate temporarily (a 2-1 buydown lowers it 2 points in year one and 1 point in year two) or permanently.

### § 4.6 Reverse mortgage

A reverse mortgage lets an older owner convert equity to cash without selling or making monthly payments; the balance grows as advances and interest accrue and becomes due when the last borrower dies, sells, or stops occupying the home as a principal residence (generally for 12 months). The dominant product is the FHA-insured Home Equity Conversion Mortgage (HECM): borrowers must be 62 or older, own the home outright or nearly so, occupy it, keep taxes and insurance current, and complete HUD-approved counseling. HECMs are non-recourse: neither borrower nor heirs owe more than the home's value at repayment. Proceeds may be a lump sum, tenure or term payments, a line of credit, or a combination. Reverse mortgages are outside the TRID forms and still use the GFE/HUD-1.

## § 5 Loan types by purpose or collateral

### § 5.1 Package mortgage

Secured by real property plus personal property sold with it (appliances, furniture, equipment), so the personalty is financed on the same terms; common in furnished condominium and hospitality sales.

### § 5.2 Blanket mortgage

Covers more than one parcel under one loan, as when a developer finances a subdivision. It includes a partial release clause so that when a lot is sold and a stated amount is paid, that lot is released while the rest stay encumbered. Without it, a lot buyer would take title subject to a lien on the whole tract.

### § 5.3 Wraparound mortgage

A new junior loan whose face amount includes the balance of an existing senior loan plus new money. The borrower makes one payment to the wrap lender (often the seller), who keeps paying the underlying loan. It works only when the senior loan has no enforceable alienation clause or the senior lender consents. The buyer's risk is that the wrap lender stops paying the underlying loan, so payments are often routed through a collection escrow.

### § 5.4 Open-end mortgage

Lets the borrower draw additional funds later, up to a stated maximum, secured by the same instrument without a new closing; the ancestor of the HELOC (§ 5.8). Whether later advances keep the original priority against intervening liens depends on whether the advance was obligatory or optional; varies by state, the state portion governs.

### § 5.5 Construction loan

Interim financing disbursed in draws as work is completed and inspected, charging interest only on funds advanced, and repaid from a permanent "take-out" loan when the building is finished; a construction-to-permanent loan combines both in one closing. Because the collateral is unfinished and mechanics' lien exposure is high, rates and fees are higher and lenders often require a take-out commitment before funding.

### § 5.6 Bridge (swing, gap) loan

Short-term financing that lets a buyer close on a new home before the current one sells, usually secured by the current home's equity and repaid from its sale proceeds; higher rate, term measured in months. "Gap" financing also bridges a construction payoff and permanent financing.

### § 5.7 Purchase-money mortgage, seller financing and contract for deed

Broadly, a purchase-money mortgage is any loan used to acquire the property that secures it (some anti-deficiency statutes use this meaning). In examination usage it usually means seller financing: the seller takes back a note and security instrument for part of the price and the buyer receives a deed at closing; the seller is the mortgagee and may hold a first or a second lien. A contract for deed (land contract, installment contract, bond for deed, agreement of sale) is different in kind: the seller (vendor) keeps legal title until the price is paid, often years later, while the buyer (vendee) gets possession and equitable title immediately. The buyer's protection depends on recording the contract and on state forfeiture law; varies by state, the state portion governs.

### § 5.8 Home equity loan and HELOC

A home equity loan is a closed-end lien for a fixed amount, usually fixed-rate with level payments. A home equity line of credit is open-end and revolving: the borrower draws as needed during a draw period (commonly 10 years), pays interest or interest plus minimum principal, then repays over a repayment period (10 to 20 years), usually at a variable rate tied to prime plus a margin. HELOCs are outside the TRID forms but have their own TILA disclosures. Both count in combined loan-to-value (§ 8.2). Any lien behind the first in priority is a junior (second) mortgage.

## § 6 Conventional and government-backed loans

### § 6.1 Conventional loans, conforming loans and PMI

A conventional loan is not insured or guaranteed by a federal agency; the lender bears the credit risk, so underwriting is stricter. A conforming loan meets Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac purchase standards (§ 7), including the maximum loan amount set annually by the Federal Housing Finance Agency: for 2026 the baseline one-unit limit is $832,750, with high-cost-area limits up to 150% of baseline (as of 2026; verify). Loans above the limit are non-conforming "jumbo" loans held in portfolio or sold privately; loans failing other agency standards are also non-conforming.

Conventional loans with a loan-to-value ratio above 80% normally require private mortgage insurance (PMI), bought from a private insurer to reimburse the lender for part of its loss on default; it is paid monthly, as a single upfront premium, or as lender-paid insurance built into the rate. Under the Homeowners Protection Act, on a borrower-paid policy for a single-family principal residence the borrower may request cancellation when the balance reaches 80% of original value (good payment history, no junior liens), and the lender must terminate automatically when the balance is scheduled to reach 78% of original value if the loan is current, or at the midpoint of the schedule otherwise. Down payments as low as 3% exist on certain agency programs; 20% avoids PMI. Conventional loans are generally not assumable.

### § 6.2 FHA-insured loans

The Federal Housing Administration, part of HUD, does not lend. It insures loans made by FHA-approved lenders against loss, funded by borrower-paid mortgage insurance premiums, so lenders can accept smaller down payments and weaker credit. Mechanics as of 2026 (verify against the current HUD Single Family Housing Policy Handbook 4000.1): minimum required investment 3.5% of the lesser of appraised value or price for a decision credit score of 580 or above, 10% for 500–579, and the down payment may be gifted from acceptable sources; an upfront MIP of 1.75% of the base loan amount, usually financed; an annual MIP paid monthly, currently 0.55% for most 30-year loans above 95% LTV and 0.50% at or below, lasting the life of the loan when initial LTV exceeds 90% and 11 years otherwise. Loan limits are set by county each year between a floor of 65% and a ceiling of 150% of the conforming baseline. The property must be the borrower's principal residence (1–4 units) and meet HUD minimum property standards as verified by an FHA-roster appraiser. FHA loans are assumable by a creditworthy buyer with lender approval, carry no prepayment penalty, and allow seller contributions to closing costs up to 6% of the price. Standard qualifying ratios are 31% housing and 43% total, with higher ratios allowed with compensating factors or automated approval (§ 8.3).

### § 6.3 VA-guaranteed loans

The Department of Veterans Affairs guarantees loans made by private lenders to eligible veterans, service members, certain reservists and Guard members, and certain surviving spouses; the VA generally does not lend. Eligibility is shown by a Certificate of Eligibility. The guaranty protects the lender against loss up to a stated amount; "entitlement" is the portion of the loan the VA will guarantee. Basic entitlement is $36,000; since 2020 borrowers with full entitlement face no VA loan limit and may borrow with no down payment for as much as the lender approves, while borrowers with partially used entitlement are subject to county limits tied to the conforming limit and may need a down payment (as of 2026; verify against the VA Lenders Handbook, Pamphlet 26-7). Features: no monthly mortgage insurance; a one-time funding fee of roughly 1.25% to 3.3% of the loan depending on down payment and prior use (as of 2026; verify), financeable and waived for borrowers receiving VA disability compensation; no prepayment penalty; assumable by a qualified buyer, veteran or not, with approval, the seller's entitlement being restored only if the buyer is an eligible veteran who substitutes entitlement. Underwriting uses a 41% total debt-to-income guideline plus a residual-income test. The VA appraisal yields a Notice of Value and the property must meet VA minimum property requirements. Seller concessions beyond normal closing costs are capped at 4% of the loan (as of 2026; verify).

### § 6.4 USDA rural housing loans

USDA Rural Development runs two Section 502 programs. The Single Family Housing Guaranteed Loan Program guarantees loans by approved lenders to low- and moderate-income households (income generally at or below 115% of area median) buying modest homes in eligible rural areas as shown on the USDA eligibility map; it permits 100% financing with no down payment, charges an upfront guarantee fee of 1.00% of the loan and an annual fee of 0.35% of the outstanding balance paid monthly (as of 2026; verify against Handbook HB-1-3555 and the current fee notice), and requires owner occupancy. The Section 502 Direct Loan Program lends government funds directly to low- and very-low-income applicants with payment assistance. USDA loans carry no prepayment penalty.

### § 6.5 Comparing MIP, PMI and the guaranty

All three protect the lender, not the borrower, and all are paid by the borrower. PMI is private, required above 80% LTV on conventional loans, and cancellable. FHA MIP has an upfront and an annual premium that, above 90% LTV, lasts the life of the loan. The VA guaranty is funded by a one-time fee with no monthly insurance; the USDA guarantee carries both upfront and annual fees.

## § 7 The secondary mortgage market

### § 7.1 Primary versus secondary market

The primary market is where borrowers obtain loans: banks, thrifts, credit unions, mortgage bankers (who lend their own or borrowed funds and sell the loans) and mortgage brokers (who arrange loans for a fee but do not lend). The secondary market is where existing loans are bought and sold among lenders and investors. Selling loans returns cash to originators so they can lend again, moves funds between regions, and standardizes underwriting because buyers set the rules for what they will purchase. Originators often keep servicing for a fee after selling the loan; servicing-transfer notices are governed by the cached RESPA text.

### § 7.2 Fannie Mae

The Federal National Mortgage Association is a government-sponsored enterprise, a shareholder-owned corporation chartered by Congress. It buys conventional conforming loans (and some FHA and VA loans) from approved lenders, holding them or pooling them into mortgage-backed securities that it guarantees. Its Selling Guide and Desktop Underwriter system define conforming standards. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac have been in FHFA conservatorship since September 2008 (status as of 2026; verify).

### § 7.3 Freddie Mac

The Federal Home Loan Mortgage Corporation is the second GSE, chartered in 1970 originally to buy loans from savings institutions. It buys conforming loans, issues guaranteed MBS, and publishes standards through its Seller/Servicer Guide. Both GSEs use the same FHFA loan limits and the same loan application.

### § 7.4 Ginnie Mae

The Government National Mortgage Association is not a GSE but a wholly owned government corporation within HUD. It neither buys loans nor issues securities. It guarantees timely payment of principal and interest on MBS issued by approved lenders and backed by pools of FHA, VA, USDA and HUD Public and Indian Housing loans, with the full faith and credit of the United States; the GSEs' guaranties do not carry that explicit backing.

### § 7.5 Other participants

Private conduits securitize jumbo and non-conforming loans; pension funds and insurers buy MBS. The Federal Reserve influences mortgage rates indirectly through monetary policy; it does not set mortgage rates or make home loans.

## § 8 Underwriting concepts

### § 8.1 The lender's questions

Underwriting asks whether the borrower can repay (capacity: income and debt ratios), will repay (credit history and score), has a stake in the property (capital: down payment and reserves), and whether the collateral is sufficient (appraised value and condition). "The three Cs" — capacity, credit, collateral — name this framework.

### § 8.2 Loan-to-value and combined loan-to-value

LTV is the loan amount divided by the lesser of appraised value or purchase price. It measures the lender's exposure: the higher the LTV, the thinner the borrower's equity cushion. LTV determines whether mortgage insurance is required (conventional loans above 80%), affects rate and fees, and sets program eligibility. Combined LTV adds all liens (first mortgage plus second mortgage or HELOC balance) before dividing by value; lenders cap CLTV when approving second liens. Examples: REP Ref. Real Estate Math § 5.3.

### § 8.3 Debt-to-income ratios

The front-end (housing) ratio is the total proposed housing payment — PITI plus mortgage insurance and association dues — divided by gross monthly income. The back-end (total) ratio adds recurring debts (car and student loans, minimum card payments, alimony, child support) to the housing payment before dividing. Traditional conventional guidelines are 28% and 36%; FHA manual-underwriting guidelines are 31% and 43%; the VA uses a single 41% total ratio plus residual income (all as of 2026; verify; automated underwriting routinely approves higher ratios with compensating factors). The lower of the two maximum payments controls. Examples: REP Ref. Real Estate Math § 5.9.

### § 8.4 PITI

Principal, interest, taxes and insurance are the four components of the typical housing payment. Principal and interest go to the lender; the tax and insurance portions are deposited into an escrow (impound) account from which the servicer pays the tax bill and hazard premium when due. Mortgage insurance and association dues are added for qualifying. Limits on the escrow cushion are in the cached RESPA text.

### § 8.5 Credit

Lenders pull a tri-merge credit report and use scores as a risk measure; minimums and pricing tiers vary by program (FHA's 580/500 thresholds in § 6.2 are an example). Bankruptcy, foreclosure, short sale and deed in lieu impose waiting periods that differ by program. What a creditor may consider and the adverse-action notice requirement are ECOA rules; cite the cached ECOA text.

### § 8.6 Collateral: the appraisal

The lender orders an appraisal from an independent state-licensed or certified appraiser. If value comes in below the contract price, LTV is measured against the lower appraised value, so the buyer must add cash, renegotiate, or use an appraisal contingency to cancel. Appraiser-independence rules bar lenders and licensees from pressuring the appraiser.

### § 8.7 Points

A point is 1% of the loan amount, not of the price. A loan origination fee is the lender's charge for making the loan, often one point. Discount points are prepaid interest paid at closing to buy down the note rate; a rough rule of thumb is one-eighth to one-quarter of a percentage point per point, though actual pricing is the lender's. Discount points raise the lender's yield and are included in the finance charge and APR. Points may be paid by the buyer or, within program limits, the seller. Examples: REP Ref. Real Estate Math § 5.4.

### § 8.8 Interest rate versus APR

The note rate is applied to the balance to compute interest. The annual percentage rate expresses the total cost of credit — interest plus finance charges such as discount points, origination fees and mortgage insurance — as a yearly rate on the amount financed, so offers can be compared. The APR therefore equals or exceeds the note rate; a wide gap signals heavy upfront charges. The definitions of finance charge and APR and their disclosure are federal law; cite the cached TILA text. Charging interest above a legal maximum is usury; limits vary by state and the state portion governs.

## § 9 The lending process

### § 9.1 Pre-qualification versus pre-approval

Pre-qualification is an informal estimate based on unverified information and carries no commitment. Pre-approval means the lender has taken an application, pulled credit, verified income and assets, and issued a conditional commitment up to a stated amount, subject to an acceptable property and appraisal and no change in circumstances. Neither is final approval.

### § 9.2 Application and early disclosures

The borrower completes the Uniform Residential Loan Application (Form 1003) and supplies income, asset and identity documents. Receipt of six items — name, income, Social Security number, property address, estimated value and loan amount — constitutes an application and triggers the Loan Estimate and related disclosures within set deadlines; the borrower must indicate intent to proceed before most fees can be charged. The triggers and deadlines are in the cached RESPA and TILA authorities.

### § 9.3 Processing, underwriting and rate lock

The processor verifies employment, deposits, credit, title, flood-zone status and the appraisal. The underwriter applies program guidelines and issues a decision: approved, approved with conditions, suspended, or denied; conditions are cleared and the file is "clear to close," with a commitment letter stating terms and expiration. A rate lock holds the quoted rate and points for a stated period (commonly 30 to 60 days); if it expires before closing the borrower may face the market rate or an extension fee. Changed circumstances that alter locked terms trigger a revised Loan Estimate under the cached TILA rules.

### § 9.4 Closing, funding and post-closing

The lender delivers the Closing Disclosure before consummation (§ 10.4), the borrower signs the note, security instrument and disclosures, the lender funds, the deed and security instrument are recorded, and disbursements are made. In wet-funding states the lender's money is present at signing; in dry-funding states funds arrive after signed documents are reviewed (varies by state; the state portion governs). Afterwards the loan is held or sold and servicing may transfer with the notice RESPA requires.

### § 9.5 Predatory lending red flags

Predatory lending imposes unfair, deceptive or abusive terms, often on elderly, unsophisticated or protected-class borrowers. Recognized patterns:

- Equity stripping: lending against a home's equity with no regard for ability to repay, profiting from fees and eventual foreclosure rather than repayment.
- Loan flipping: repeated refinancing with no net benefit to the borrower, generating new points and fees rolled into the balance each time.
- Packing: adding unrequested or unnecessary products — single-premium credit life insurance, inflated "processing" fees — into the financed amount without clear disclosure.
- Steering: pushing a borrower who qualifies for a prime loan into a costlier subprime or high-fee product; on a prohibited basis this also violates ECOA and the Fair Housing Act.
- Bait-and-switch: quoting attractive terms, then presenting different documents at the table.
- Structural traps: large balloon payments, negative amortization, prepayment penalties paired with adjustable rates, mandatory arbitration, inflated appraisals.

The federal responses — HOEPA high-cost loan rules and the ability-to-repay rules — are part of TILA/Reg Z; cite the cached text. A licensee who sees these signs should urge the client to compare lenders and must never steer a client to a lender for anything of value (see cached RESPA § 8 text).

## § 10 Closing the transaction

### § 10.1 What closing is and who does it

Closing (settlement) is the event at which the seller delivers the deed, the buyer pays the price, the lender funds, and all charges between the parties are settled as the purchase agreement requires; the contract fixes the rights and closing performs them. The closing (settlement) agent assembles documents, collects and disburses funds, prepares the settlement statement, and arranges recording. Who serves is set by custom and law: a title or escrow company in many western and midwestern states, an attorney in many eastern and southern states, sometimes the lender or broker; several states require an attorney to conduct or supervise closings (varies by state; the state portion governs). Licensees attend, check figures against the contract, answer questions within their license, and coordinate the walk-through and keys, but may not give legal advice or draft legal instruments.

### § 10.2 Escrow closing versus face-to-face closing

In an escrow closing both parties deposit documents and funds with a neutral escrow agent under written instructions; when every condition is met the agent records and disburses, and the parties need not meet. In a face-to-face (round-table) closing the parties, their agents, the lender and the closing attorney exchange documents and funds at one sitting. Custom varies by state.

### § 10.3 Title work and title insurance

Before closing the title company searches the records and issues a title commitment listing the requirements for insuring (liens the seller must clear, shown by payoff letters) and the exceptions that will remain (easements, covenants, current taxes). Two policies may issue: a lender's policy in the loan amount, required by virtually every lender and declining as the balance is paid; and an owner's policy in the purchase price, protecting the buyer and heirs for as long as they hold title. Who pays for each varies by state and contract (§ 10.7).

### § 10.4 The Loan Estimate and the Closing Disclosure

For most closed-end consumer mortgage loans on residential property, two federal forms replaced the Good Faith Estimate, early Truth-in-Lending statement and HUD-1 for applications taken since October 2015. The Loan Estimate is a three-page form given shortly after application setting out loan terms (amount, rate, monthly principal and interest, whether any can increase, prepayment penalty and balloon features), projected payments including escrow, estimated closing costs and cash to close, and comparison figures such as APR. The Closing Disclosure is a five-page form restating that information as final figures, itemizing every closing cost by who pays it and when, reconciling cash to close against the Loan Estimate, and containing a Summaries of Transactions section with the borrower's and seller's debits and credits; the seller may receive a seller-only version. The borrower must receive it a set number of business days before consummation and certain changes restart the wait; the triggers and day counts are federal law and items on them must cite the cached TILA and RESPA text. Reverse mortgages and HELOCs remain outside the two-form regime; cash sales use whatever settlement statement the closing agent prepares.

### § 10.5 Debits and credits

A settlement statement has a buyer's side and a seller's side, each with a debit column (amounts the party owes or is charged) and a credit column (amounts the party has paid, is receiving, or is owed). The price is the seller's largest credit and the buyer's largest debit. The buyer's new loan is a buyer credit only; the seller's payoff is a seller debit only; earnest money already deposited is a buyer credit only. Commission, seller-paid costs and seller-owed prorations are seller debits; buyer-paid loan fees, prepaids and escrow reserves are buyer debits. Prorations appear on both sides as offsetting entries: a proration the seller owes is a seller debit and a buyer credit of the same amount. Buyer cash to close = buyer debits − buyer credits; seller proceeds = seller credits − seller debits. Worked statements: REP Ref. Real Estate Math § 12.

### § 10.6 Prorations

Expenses that run with the property over time — property taxes, association dues, rents collected in advance, interest on an assumed loan — are divided so each party bears the share for its period of ownership. Items the seller paid ahead are credited to the seller and debited to the buyer; items accrued but unpaid are debited to the seller and credited to the buyer. Two calendar conventions exist (365-day actual, or 360-day with 30-day months), and the closing day is charged to one party by agreement or custom; examination problems state the convention. Methods and examples: REP Ref. Real Estate Math § 11.

### § 10.7 Who pays what

The purchase agreement controls; absent agreement, local custom fills the gap and varies by state (the state portion governs). Default allocations that examination items assume unless told otherwise: the buyer pays lender fees (origination, discount points, appraisal, credit report, flood certification), the lender's title policy, recording of the deed and security instrument, prepaid interest, the first year's hazard premium and the initial escrow deposits; the seller pays the brokerage commission, payoff of existing liens and recording of their releases, deed preparation, and in many but not all states the owner's title policy and the transfer tax. Government programs limit what the seller may pay for the buyer (§ 6.2, § 6.3).

### § 10.8 Federal reporting at closing

The settlement agent (or, if none, the lender, then the seller's broker, then the buyer's broker) reports the sale to the Internal Revenue Service on Form 1099-S with gross proceeds and the seller's taxpayer identification number; a principal-residence sale is exempt when the seller certifies in writing that the gain is excludable (currently up to $250,000, or $500,000 on a joint return; verify). If the seller is a foreign person under FIRPTA (26 U.S.C. § 1445) the buyer is the withholding agent and generally must withhold 15% of the amount realized, with reduced or zero withholding for lower-priced residences the buyer will occupy and exceptions where the seller furnishes a non-foreign affidavit or a withholding certificate (as of 2026; verify). The lender reports mortgage interest and purchase points on Form 1098. Refer tax questions to a tax professional.

### § 10.9 Deed delivery, acceptance and recording

Title passes when the grantor delivers a validly executed deed to the grantee during the grantor's lifetime intending to pass title, and the grantee accepts (presumed when the conveyance is beneficial). In an escrow closing, delivery to the escrow agent becomes effective when the escrow conditions are satisfied. Recording is not required to pass title between the parties, but it gives constructive notice, protects the grantee against later purchasers and lien creditors, and fixes the priority of the buyer's title and the lender's lien; the closing agent records the deed first and the security instrument immediately after, in the county where the land lies. Recording requires acknowledgment of the grantor's signature and payment of recording fees and any transfer tax (varies by state).

### § 10.10 Final walk-through, possession and risk

The buyer's final walk-through, shortly before closing, confirms that the property is in the contract condition, agreed repairs are done, included personal property remains and the seller's belongings are gone; deficiencies are handled by a closing credit, an escrow holdback or a written agreement. Possession passes when the contract says — typically at closing and funding, or on recording in escrow states. If the seller stays after closing or the buyer moves in early, a written occupancy agreement should fix rent, insurance, utilities, maintenance and a firm move-out date. Who bears casualty risk between contract and closing varies by state (some follow the Uniform Vendor and Purchaser Risk Act); the contract usually addresses it and the state portion governs. The buyer's hazard insurance must be in force at closing because the lender requires it. At the table the buyer signs the note, the security instrument and the Closing Disclosure; the seller signs the deed, an affidavit of title, the transfer tax declaration and the 1099-S and FIRPTA certifications.
