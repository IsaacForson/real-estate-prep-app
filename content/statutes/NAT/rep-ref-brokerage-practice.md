# REP Ref. Brokerage Practice

Reference note: the operation of a real estate brokerage — scope of practice, engagement agreements, compensation, antitrust in practice, employment status, supervision, advertising, risk management, trust funds, and property management. Authored expository text (status: draft — needs licensed review). Covers Pearson VUE national outline V.A, V.B, and the non-statutory parts of V.D, plus PSI national outline VIII (property management) and X.B–X.D. Where a topic is governed by a cached federal statute (Sherman Act, TCPA, Fair Housing Act, ADA Title III, E-SIGN, RESPA), items should cite the statute for the legal rule and this note only for practice context. Where a rule varies by state, the state portion of the exam governs.

## § 1 Scope of a broker's practice

### § 1.1 What a license authorizes

A real estate license authorizes the holder, for compensation and on behalf of others, to perform the acts state law defines as brokerage: listing, selling, buying, exchanging, leasing, renting, managing, negotiating, advertising, and offering to do any of these with respect to real property. The precise list is statutory and varies by state. A **broker** may operate a brokerage and employ or sponsor licensees; a **salesperson** (or associate broker) may perform brokerage acts only under a sponsoring broker and may be compensated only by that broker. Acting for others for compensation without a license is unlawful in every state, and an unlicensed person generally cannot sue to collect a commission. Owners dealing with their own property, attorneys acting within their practice, court-appointed fiduciaries, and (in many states) resident managers performing limited leasing tasks are common exemptions; the state portion governs.

### § 1.2 The line with the unauthorized practice of law

Licensees may complete standardized, pre-printed or attorney-prepared contract forms by filling in the factual terms the parties have negotiated — names, price, dates, contingencies chosen from the form's menu — because that is incidental to the brokerage service. Licensees may **not** draft original contract language, create deeds, mortgages, or leases from scratch (except where a standard lease form is expressly permitted), give legal advice about the meaning or effect of a document, advise how to take title, or interpret how a law applies to a client's situation. Doing so is the unauthorized practice of law — a license law violation and, in most states, a crime. The safe response to a legal question is to recommend an attorney. Charging separately for document preparation raises the same issue. Exactly which additions a licensee may write varies by state; the state portion governs.

Example: A buyer asks the licensee to add a clause making the sale contingent on the buyer's partner's approval of the property "in her sole discretion." Writing that clause from scratch is drafting. The licensee should either use an approved contingency addendum that covers the situation or refer the buyer to an attorney to draft the language.

### § 1.3 Other professional boundaries

Licensees likewise should not give tax, structural, environmental, or lending advice beyond general information, and may offer a broker price opinion or comparative market analysis only where state law allows and only labeled as such, never as an appraisal. Reasonable care (REP Ref. Agency § 5.7) includes recognizing when a client needs another professional.

## § 2 Listing agreements

### § 2.1 Nature and required terms

A listing agreement is the employment contract between a seller (or landlord) and a brokerage, creating seller agency (REP Ref. Agency § 2.2). It is a personal service contract with the *broker*, not the individual salesperson; if the salesperson leaves the firm, the listing stays with the broker. Most states require listings to be written, signed by the seller, with a definite expiration date, and prohibit automatic extension clauses. Typical contents: property identification; list price and terms; the broker's compensation and when it is earned; the listing period; authorization to place a sign, use a lockbox, advertise, and cooperate with and compensate other brokers; personal property included; the seller's warranties about title and condition; and required disclosures.

### § 2.2 Open listing

A nonexclusive listing given to one or more brokers at the same time, under which the seller owes a commission only to the broker who is the procuring cause (§ 4.3) of the sale. The seller retains the right to sell without owing anyone a commission. An open listing is a unilateral contract (see REP Ref. Contracts § 1.3): the seller promises to pay, but no broker promises to do anything. Because brokers may spend effort and earn nothing, open listings receive little marketing and are rare for residential sales.

### § 2.3 Exclusive agency listing

The seller appoints **one** broker as the exclusive agent for the term, and owes that broker a commission if the property is sold by the broker *or by any other broker*; but the seller **retains the right to sell the property personally** without paying a commission. The distinguishing feature is the seller's reserved right to find his or her own buyer.

### § 2.4 Exclusive right to sell listing

The seller appoints one broker and agrees to pay the commission if the property sells during the term **regardless of who procures the buyer — including the seller**. This is the most common residential listing because it gives the broker the greatest assurance of being paid and therefore the greatest incentive to market the property. It is bilateral: the broker promises to use diligent efforts, and the seller promises to pay.

Example: Under an exclusive agency listing, the seller sells to a coworker without the broker's involvement — no commission is owed. Under an exclusive right to sell, the same sale earns the broker the full commission. Under an open listing given to three brokers, the seller's own sale owes nothing, and a sale by Broker B owes a commission only to Broker B.

### § 2.5 Net listing

A listing in which the seller names a net amount he or she must receive and the broker keeps everything above that figure as compensation. Net listings are illegal in many states and discouraged everywhere because they create an inherent conflict between the broker's loyalty and the broker's pocket: the broker profits by convincing the seller to accept a low net figure and then selling high. Where permitted, they typically require the broker to disclose the anticipated sale price and commission before the seller signs. Candidates should recognize a net listing as the type that is "problematic" or "prohibited in many states."

### § 2.6 Protection (safety, extender) clause

A clause under which the seller owes the commission if, within a stated period after the listing expires, the property is sold to a buyer the broker introduced during the term. Most versions require the broker to deliver a list of such prospects at expiration and do not apply if the seller lists with another broker. Its purpose is to prevent a seller and buyer from waiting out the listing to avoid the commission.

### § 2.7 Termination of a listing

A listing ends by sale, expiration, mutual agreement, revocation or renunciation (with possible liability), death or incapacity of seller or broker, destruction or condemnation of the property, or bankruptcy — the rules that govern any agency (REP Ref. Agency § 4). A broker who continues to market an expired listing is acting without authority.

## § 3 Buyer representation and property management agreements

### § 3.1 Buyer representation agreement

The contract that creates buyer agency (REP Ref. Agency § 2.3). It parallels the listing: exclusive or nonexclusive; a term with a definite end; the geographic area and property type covered; the broker's duties; how the broker will be compensated (a retainer, a flat fee, a percentage of the price, or the amount offered through the listing broker, with the buyer responsible for any shortfall if the agreement so provides); how the broker handles the firm's own listings (dual or designated agency consent); and a protection clause for properties shown during the term. Under an **exclusive** buyer agreement the buyer agrees to work only with that brokerage and owes compensation if the buyer purchases any property of the covered type during the term, whether or not the broker found it. Many states now require a written buyer agreement before a licensee shows property to a buyer; the state portion governs.

### § 3.2 Property management agreement

The contract creating a general agency (REP Ref. Agency § 1.2) between a property owner and a managing broker. It should state: the property and the term; the manager's authority (to lease, set or approve rents, collect rents, hire and pay vendors, evict, and spend up to a stated amount without approval); the owner's obligations (funding a reserve, carrying insurance, disclosing known defects); the manager's compensation (commonly a percentage of gross collected rent, plus leasing fees and sometimes a fee for supervising major repairs); reporting frequency and content; how trust funds are held; and termination. Because the manager handles the owner's money and binds the owner to leases, most states require a written agreement and treat management as brokerage activity requiring a license. Details of management practice are in § 12.

## § 4 Compensation

### § 4.1 Forms of compensation

Brokerage compensation is whatever the client and broker agree to: a percentage of the sale price (most common for sales), a flat fee, an hourly fee, a percentage of collected rent (management), or a fee per lease. **Commission rates are not set by law, by any regulator, or by any trade group; every rate is negotiable between the broker and the client.** Any statement that a rate is "standard," "customary," or "what everyone charges" is both untrue and an antitrust warning sign (§ 5). Compensation belongs to the broker; a salesperson receives a share only from his or her own broker under their employment or independent contractor agreement.

### § 4.2 When a commission is earned versus paid

Under the traditional rule, a listing broker has *earned* a commission when the broker produces a **ready, willing, and able buyer** on terms the seller accepts — that is, when a binding contract is signed — even if the sale later fails because of the seller's default. In practice, most listing agreements make the commission *payable* at closing out of the seller's proceeds, and many provide that no commission is owed if the transaction fails for reasons other than the seller's fault. The listing agreement controls; where it is silent, state law governs. On a buyer's default the broker may, depending on the agreement, share in forfeited earnest money.

### § 4.3 Procuring cause

When more than one broker claims a commission for the same sale, the dispute is decided by identifying the **procuring cause**: the broker whose efforts began an unbroken chain of events that led to the sale. Merely introducing the buyer to the property is not conclusive; a broker who shows the property and then abandons the buyer may lose to a second broker who negotiates and writes the accepted offer. Factors include who first showed the property, whether there was a break in continuity, whether the buyer abandoned the first broker, and whether a written buyer agreement existed. Open listings and cooperative-compensation disputes are where procuring cause matters most; between the seller and the brokers, the seller under an exclusive listing owes one commission to the listing broker and the brokers sort out the split.

### § 4.4 Cooperative compensation and referral fees

A listing broker who offers to share the commission with the broker who brings the buyer (a cooperating broker) makes an offer that becomes binding when the cooperating broker procures the buyer on the offered terms. Alternatively, a buyer's broker may be paid directly by the buyer under the buyer agreement. **Referral fees** between licensed brokers are lawful and must flow broker to broker; paying anything of value to an *unlicensed* person for referring business is prohibited in every state, and kickbacks for referrals of settlement services in a federally related mortgage transaction are separately prohibited by the cached RESPA statute.

### § 4.5 Computing a commission

Commission = sale price × rate.

Example: A home sells for $425,000 with a 5.5% commission.
$425,000 × 0.055 = **$23,375** total commission.

To find the price from a commission: price = commission ÷ rate. If a broker received $18,600 at 6%, the sale price was $18,600 ÷ 0.06 = **$310,000**.

To find the rate: rate = commission ÷ price. A $12,000 commission on a $400,000 sale is $12,000 ÷ $400,000 = 0.03 = **3%**.

### § 4.6 Splitting a commission — worked examples

Splits happen in layers: first between the listing brokerage and the cooperating (buyer's) brokerage; then within each brokerage between the broker and the salesperson.

**Example A — two brokerages, even split, 70/30 internal splits.**
Sale price $360,000; total commission 6% = $360,000 × 0.06 = $21,600.
Listing brokerage and buyer's brokerage split 50/50: each receives $10,800.
The buyer's salesperson is on a 70/30 split with her broker (70% to the salesperson):
$10,800 × 0.70 = **$7,560** to the salesperson; $10,800 × 0.30 = $3,240 retained by the broker.

**Example B — uneven co-op split.**
Sale price $500,000; total commission 5%. The listing broker offers 2.5% of the price to cooperating brokers.
Total: $500,000 × 0.05 = $25,000. Cooperating broker: $500,000 × 0.025 = $12,500. Listing broker keeps $25,000 − $12,500 = $12,500.
The listing salesperson is on a 60/40 split (60% to salesperson): $12,500 × 0.60 = **$7,500**.

**Example C — working backward to price.**
A salesperson on a 55% split received $6,187.50. Her broker had received half of a 6% total commission. What was the sale price?
Brokerage share = $6,187.50 ÷ 0.55 = $11,250. Total commission = $11,250 × 2 = $22,500. Price = $22,500 ÷ 0.06 = **$375,000**.

**Example D — net to seller (a common exam pattern).**
A seller wants to net $282,000 after paying a 6% commission and $3,000 in other closing costs. What list price achieves this?
The seller keeps 100% − 6% = 94% of the price, less $3,000. So 0.94 × price − $3,000 = $282,000 → 0.94 × price = $285,000 → price = $285,000 ÷ 0.94 = **$303,191.49** (round up to $303,200 in practice). Check: $303,191.49 × 0.06 = $18,191.49; $303,191.49 − $18,191.49 − $3,000 = $282,000.

### § 4.7 Compensation to salespersons

A salesperson may accept compensation for licensed activity only from his or her sponsoring broker — never directly from a client, another broker, a lender, or a vendor. A "bonus" offered by a seller or builder to the selling salesperson must be paid through the broker. Salespersons also may not pay other salespersons directly; splits between team members are run through the broker's books.

## § 5 Antitrust in practice

### § 5.1 The law and the practice

The cached Sherman Act is the governing federal statute; items on the legal rule should cite it directly. This section describes how the four classic violations arise in a brokerage setting so that scenario items can be grounded. State antitrust statutes generally parallel the federal act; the state portion governs any state-specific rule.

### § 5.2 Price fixing

An agreement among competitors to set, raise, or stabilize the price of their services. In brokerage this means any agreement among brokers from *different* firms about commission rates, minimum fees, or the amount offered to cooperating brokers. The agreement need not be written or formal; an understanding reached over coffee, or parallel conduct following a discussion, can suffice. **A broker setting its own firm's rates and directing its salespersons to charge them is lawful**; agreeing with another firm is not. Telling a client "the going rate in this town is 6%" invites an inference that rates are set collectively; the correct statement is that the firm's fee is negotiable and is whatever the firm has chosen to charge.

Example: At an association meeting two managing brokers agree that neither will accept a listing below 5%. That is per se price fixing. If one of those brokers had simply announced her own firm's policy without any agreement, it would not be.

### § 5.3 Group boycott

An agreement among competitors to refuse to deal with another competitor or supplier — for instance, two or more firms agreeing not to show the listings of a discount broker, not to cooperate with a new firm, or to pressure a multiple listing service to exclude a broker. Refusing individually to do business with someone is generally lawful; agreeing with a competitor to do so is a group boycott.

### § 5.4 Market allocation

An agreement among competitors to divide customers, territories, or types of property — "you take everything north of the highway and I'll take the south," or "you handle condominiums and we'll handle single-family." Each firm gains a local monopoly, and competition is eliminated within each territory. A single firm assigning territories to its *own* salespersons is not market allocation; the violation requires agreement between competitors.

### § 5.5 Tie-in arrangements

Conditioning the sale of one product or service on the buyer's purchase of a second, separate one, where the seller has enough market power to force the purchase — for example, a developer who will sell lots only to buyers who agree to list the finished homes with the developer's brokerage, or a brokerage that will take a listing only if the seller uses its affiliated mortgage or title company. Merely offering affiliated services is lawful with appropriate disclosure (and, for settlement services, subject to the cached RESPA statute); requiring them is a tie-in.

### § 5.6 Practice safeguards

Brokers should set fees independently; never discuss fees, splits, or dealings with particular competitors when meeting other firms; document in every listing that compensation is negotiable; avoid words like "standard" or "customary"; leave any conversation in which competitors begin discussing rates; and train licensees that antitrust liability is personal and criminal as well as civil.

## § 6 Independent contractor versus employee

### § 6.1 Why the distinction matters

Most salespersons are engaged by their brokers as **independent contractors** rather than employees. The classification affects federal income tax withholding, Social Security and Medicare taxes, unemployment insurance, workers' compensation, overtime, and benefits. It does **not** affect the broker's duty under state license law to supervise the salesperson (§ 7): a broker cannot avoid supervisory responsibility by calling a licensee an independent contractor. Nor does it change agency: the salesperson is the broker's agent for license-law and client purposes regardless of tax status.

### § 6.2 The general common-law test

Ordinarily the IRS and courts decide employee status by the degree of control the payer has over how, when, and where work is done (behavioral control, financial control, and the parties' relationship). A worker with set hours, mandatory meetings, reimbursed expenses, and a salary looks like an employee; one who sets his or her own hours, pays his or her own expenses, and is paid only on results looks like an independent contractor.

### § 6.3 The federal statutory safe harbor for licensed real estate agents

Federal tax law contains a special rule treating a licensed real estate agent as a **statutory non-employee** (independent contractor) for federal income and employment tax purposes if three conditions are all met:

1. the individual is a **licensed real estate agent**;
2. **substantially all** of the individual's compensation for real estate services is **directly related to sales or other output** (commissions), rather than to the number of hours worked; and
3. the services are performed under a **written contract** providing that the individual **will not be treated as an employee** for federal tax purposes.

When all three are present, the broker does not withhold income tax or pay the employer share of employment taxes, and the agent is responsible for self-employment tax and quarterly estimated payments. The broker reports commissions on the annual information return for nonemployee compensation. Because the safe harbor is federal, a state may still classify the same agent as an employee for state unemployment, workers' compensation, or wage-law purposes; the state portion governs. Brokers who wish to preserve independent contractor status should avoid conduct inconsistent with it — setting mandatory hours, paying salaries or hourly wages, or reimbursing expenses — beyond what license law supervision requires.

Example: Broker Lee's firm has a written independent contractor agreement with each licensee stating they are not employees for federal tax purposes, pays them only commission splits, and requires them to hold a license. The three conditions are met; Lee does not withhold taxes. Lee may still require them to attend training on trust-account rules and to submit contracts for review, because supervision is a license law duty independent of tax status.

## § 7 Broker supervision

### § 7.1 The supervisory duty

State license laws make the broker (or a designated managing broker) responsible for supervising the brokerage activities of every licensee and every unlicensed person working in the firm. Supervision typically requires: written office policies; review of contracts, listings, and advertising; oversight of trust accounts; training on license law, fair housing, and agency; availability to answer questions; and timely handling of complaints. A broker may be disciplined for a salesperson's violation the broker should have prevented through reasonable supervision, and is civilly liable to clients and third parties for licensees' acts within the scope of the license (REP Ref. Agency § 9.3). Some states impose numerical limits on how many licensees a branch manager may supervise, or require a managing broker at each branch office. The state portion governs.

### § 7.2 Unlicensed assistants

An unlicensed assistant may perform clerical and administrative work that does not require a license: answering phones, scheduling appointments, preparing marketing materials and assembling files under a licensee's direction, ordering inspections at a licensee's direction, and (in some states) hosting open houses without discussing price or terms. An unlicensed assistant may **not** show property (in most states), discuss or negotiate price or terms, interpret contracts, solicit listings or buyers, hold themselves out as a licensee, or be paid a commission or any fee that varies with sales. The broker is responsible for the assistant's conduct. Permitted tasks vary considerably by state; the state portion governs.

### § 7.3 Teams

A team of licensees within a brokerage remains under the broker's supervision; the team leader is not a substitute for the broker unless licensed as one and the state permits delegation. Team advertising must identify the brokerage (§ 8.3), and team compensation flows through the broker (§ 4.7).

## § 8 Advertising

### § 8.1 Truthfulness

All advertising must be truthful and not misleading. Statements of fact about a property (square footage, lot size, age, condition, school district) must be accurate; puffery of opinion is permitted but should not shade into factual claims (REP Ref. Agency § 6.4). A licensee may not advertise property without the owner's authorization, may not advertise a listing as available after it is under contract without saying so, and may not advertise another broker's listing without permission. **Bait-and-switch** advertising — promoting a property or terms the licensee does not intend or is unable to deliver — is deceptive.

### § 8.2 Fair housing in advertising

The cached Fair Housing Act prohibits advertising that indicates a preference, limitation, or discrimination based on a protected class; items on the rule should cite that statute. In practice, licensees describe the property, not the ideal occupant: "two bedrooms, near parks" rather than "great for young families"; "walk-in shower" rather than "ideal for the disabled"; "quiet building" rather than "adult building" (unless a housing-for-older-persons exemption applies). Human models in advertising should reflect the population generally. Publishers and licensees are both exposed; the equal housing opportunity logo or slogan is customary in advertising.

### § 8.3 Disclosure of the brokerage and licensee status

Most states require that every advertisement placed by a licensee — print, sign, website, social media post, email — identify the **brokerage** by its licensed name, often in a specified size relative to the licensee's or team's name, so the public knows whom the licensee represents and who is accountable. A licensee advertising his or her own property for sale must generally disclose licensee status. **Blind ads** that omit the brokerage and imply the advertiser is a private owner are prohibited. Team names must not suggest the team is an independent brokerage; many states prohibit team names containing words like "realty," "real estate," or "brokerage" without the sponsoring firm's name. Requirements vary by state and are enforced through license discipline; the state portion governs.

### § 8.4 Online advertising and social media

The same rules apply online. The brokerage name must appear on each page, profile, or post where it is required (a link to a page with the disclosure is often insufficient); listing data must be kept current when a property goes under contract or sells; photographs and descriptions must belong to or be licensed to the advertiser; testimonials must be genuine; and automated calling or texting is subject to the cached TCPA. Licensees should keep copies of online advertising for the record-retention period (§ 10).

### § 8.5 Do-Not-Call compliance in practice

The legal mechanics are in the cached TCPA (47 U.S.C. § 227) and the FTC's Telemarketing Sales Rule. In practice a brokerage that solicits by phone should: scrub prospecting lists against the National Do Not Call Registry before calling; maintain and honor an internal do-not-call list; observe permitted calling hours; identify the caller and the firm; not use auto-dialers or pre-recorded messages to cell phones without prior express consent; and train licensees who prospect expired listings or for-sale-by-owner sellers, since those calls are solicitations. An established business relationship creates a limited exception for a limited time; the cached statute and FTC rule state the current periods. Text messages are treated as calls for many purposes.

## § 9 Fraud, misrepresentation, negligence, and E&O insurance

### § 9.1 The three levels of fault

- **Fraud (intentional misrepresentation)**: a knowingly false statement (or deliberate concealment) of a material fact, made to induce reliance, on which the other party relies to his or her detriment. Remedies include rescission, compensatory and punitive damages, license revocation, and criminal prosecution.
- **Negligent misrepresentation**: a false statement made without reasonable grounds for believing it true — the licensee should have checked. Remedies include rescission and compensatory damages, plus discipline.
- **Negligence**: a failure to exercise the care a reasonable licensee would exercise, causing loss — missing a deadline, failing to recommend an inspection, misfiling a deposit — even with no false statement at all.

Because latent-defect and misrepresentation claims are the most common source of licensee liability, the practice defenses are documentation (written disclosures, inspection recommendations in writing, records of what was said and when), verification of facts before repeating them, attribution of unverified information to its source, and consistent use of seller disclosure forms.

### § 9.2 Errors and omissions insurance

**E&O insurance** is professional liability coverage that pays defense costs and damages for claims arising from a licensee's negligent acts, errors, or omissions in performing licensed services. Some states require every licensee to carry it; elsewhere it is voluntary. E&O policies universally **exclude** intentional wrongdoing such as fraud, and commonly exclude claims involving the licensee's own property, environmental and discrimination claims, and mishandling of trust funds; they do not cover license discipline. Most policies are "claims-made" — the policy in force when the claim is *made* responds — so continuous coverage matters. A deductible and per-claim and aggregate limits apply. E&O is one part of risk management, not a substitute for it.

## § 10 Trust accounts and record keeping

### § 10.1 Trust (escrow) accounts

Money a brokerage receives that belongs to others — earnest money, tenant security deposits, rents collected for owners, advance fees — is **trust money** and must be held in a designated trust or escrow account separate from the firm's operating funds. Common requirements (specifics vary by state; the state portion governs): the account is in a federally insured depository, titled as a trust account, with the broker as signatory; deposits are made within a short statutory period after receipt or contract acceptance; the broker keeps a journal of receipts and disbursements and a separate ledger for each transaction or owner; the account is reconciled monthly to the bank statement and the ledgers; and funds are disbursed only as the parties' agreement or a court directs. Many states allow a small, capped broker deposit to cover bank fees.

### § 10.2 Commingling and conversion

**Commingling** is mixing trust money with the broker's own — depositing earnest money to the operating account, or leaving earned commissions in the trust account past the permitted time. **Conversion** is using trust money for any unauthorized purpose. Both are grounds for discipline; conversion is theft. The commission portion of a closing disbursement becomes the broker's money only when it is earned and disbursed to the broker under the closing statement; a broker who "advances" himself the commission from the trust account before closing has converted funds. Disputed earnest money is held until the parties agree in writing, a court rules, or a state statutory procedure resolves it (REP Ref. Contracts § 5.2).

### § 10.3 Record keeping

State license laws require brokers to retain transaction records — listing and buyer agreements, offers (including rejected ones, in many states), contracts, disclosures, closing statements, trust account records, and advertising — for a stated period, commonly three to five years, and to make them available to the regulator on request. Records may usually be kept electronically if they are complete, legible, and retrievable. Good record keeping is also the licensee's primary defense in a misrepresentation or negligence claim (§ 9.1).

## § 11 Risk management, confidentiality, and due diligence

### § 11.1 The risk management framework

Risk management means identifying the ways a brokerage can be harmed and choosing, for each, one of four responses: **avoid** the activity (decline net listings; do not draft contract language); **control** the risk through procedures and training (disclosure protocols, contract review, trust account reconciliation); **transfer** it through insurance and contract terms (E&O coverage, recommended inspections, "as is" clauses where lawful); or **retain** it knowingly (small risks not worth insuring). Written office policies, training, and documentation are the everyday form of control.

### § 11.2 Confidentiality and personal information

Beyond the agency duty of confidentiality (REP Ref. Agency § 5.5), brokerages hold sensitive personal and financial information — loan applications, tax forms, bank statements, keys and alarm codes. Practical duties include collecting only what is needed, securing files, limiting access, securely destroying records after the retention period, warning clients about wire fraud and transmitting wiring instructions securely, and never disclosing a client's personal information without consent or legal compulsion. Federal financial privacy rules may apply to brokerages handling certain consumer financial information; the state portion governs state privacy laws.

### § 11.3 Due diligence

Due diligence is the investigation a reasonable licensee performs before making a representation or advising a client: measuring or obtaining the recorded square footage rather than repeating the seller's estimate; reviewing the seller's disclosure with the seller; checking the public record for liens, zoning, and flood status (the cached flood insurance statute governs the purchase requirement); confirming that a buyer has a pre-approval before writing an offer; and recommending professional inspections. A licensee is not an inspector or a title examiner, but must not ignore red flags — stains, cracks, odors, unpermitted additions — and should disclose them and recommend expert evaluation.

## § 12 Property management fundamentals

### § 12.1 The manager's role

A property manager is the owner's general agent (REP Ref. Agency § 1.2) charged with achieving the owner's objectives — usually maximizing net income while preserving value — through leasing, rent collection, maintenance, tenant relations, financial reporting, and regulatory compliance. Because the manager leases and collects rents for others for compensation, property management is brokerage activity requiring a broker's license in most states, with limited exemptions for on-site employees of the owner. The manager owes the owner the full set of fiduciary duties (REP Ref. Agency § 5), including loyalty (no undisclosed vendor kickbacks), accounting (properly ledgered trust funds), and reasonable care.

### § 12.2 The management agreement

See § 3.2. Two provisions deserve emphasis: the **scope of authority** (spending limits, leasing parameters, authority to evict), because the owner is bound by the manager's acts within it; and the **compensation structure**, because a fee based on collected rent rewards occupancy and collections but not expense control, which is why reporting and budgeting obligations are specified separately.

### § 12.3 Budgeting and financial reporting

The manager prepares an **operating budget** projecting income and expenses for the coming year and reports actual results against it. Key terms: **potential gross income** (all units rented at market rent for the full period); **vacancy and collection loss**; **effective gross income** (potential gross income minus vacancy and collection loss, plus other income such as laundry and parking); **operating expenses** (fixed, such as taxes and insurance; variable, such as utilities and repairs; and **reserves for replacement** of short-lived components); and **net operating income** (effective gross income minus operating expenses, before debt service and income taxes). A **capital budget** plans major improvements funded outside operations.

Example: A 20-unit building rents at $1,200 per unit per month. Potential gross income = 20 × $1,200 × 12 = $288,000. Vacancy and collection loss of 6% = $17,280. Other income $4,000. Effective gross income = $288,000 − $17,280 + $4,000 = $274,720. Operating expenses $118,000. Net operating income = $274,720 − $118,000 = **$156,720**. A management fee of 6% of collected rent ≈ 0.06 × ($288,000 − $17,280) = $16,243 and is included in operating expenses.

### § 12.4 Setting rents and marketing

Rents are set by market survey of comparable units adjusted for amenities, location, and condition, balanced against the owner's occupancy goals — a rent slightly below market may produce higher effective income through lower vacancy and turnover. Marketing must comply with fair housing law (§ 8.2). Concessions are effective rent reductions and should be reported to the owner as such.

### § 12.5 Tenant screening and fair housing in leasing

Screening criteria — income ratios, credit, rental history, criminal history where lawful — must be written, applied uniformly to every applicant, and related to the tenant's ability to pay and to care for the property. The cached Fair Housing Act prohibits refusing to rent, imposing different terms, or steering based on protected class; items on the rule should cite that statute. In leasing practice this means: the same application, deposit, and rules for everyone; no "adults only" or "no children" policies (except where a housing-for-older-persons exemption applies); reasonable, uniformly applied occupancy standards; reasonable accommodations for persons with disabilities (for example, an assistance animal despite a no-pets rule) and permission for reasonable modifications at the tenant's expense in most private housing. Blanket criminal-history bans have drawn federal enforcement attention; state and local laws add protected classes; the state portion governs.

### § 12.6 Leases and tenant relations

The manager prepares leases on approved forms (see REP Ref. Contracts § 12 for lease types and leasehold estates), enforces them consistently, responds promptly to maintenance requests, documents communications, and handles renewals and move-outs on a schedule. Retaining good tenants is generally cheaper than turnover.

### § 12.7 Maintenance categories

- **Preventive maintenance**: scheduled servicing to avoid breakdowns (HVAC filter changes, gutter cleaning, roof inspections). Reduces long-run cost.
- **Corrective (routine) maintenance**: repairing things that break — a leaking faucet, a failed water heater.
- **Emergency maintenance**: repairs that cannot wait because of danger or ongoing damage — a burst pipe, no heat in winter, a gas smell.
- **Cosmetic or make-ready maintenance**: painting and cleaning between tenants.
- **Construction / capital improvements**: renovations and replacements that add value or extend life, funded from reserves or the capital budget rather than operating expenses.

A manager should have written procedures for each category, including after-hours emergency contacts and spending authority.

### § 12.8 Security deposits generally

A security deposit is tenant money held to secure performance of the lease — unpaid rent, damage beyond normal wear and tear, and cleaning. State law commonly limits the amount (often one or two months' rent), requires it to be held in a trust account (sometimes with interest to the tenant), and requires the landlord to return the deposit or an itemized statement of deductions within a stated number of days after the tenant vacates, with penalties for bad-faith retention. "Normal wear and tear" — minor scuffs, faded paint, carpet worn by ordinary use — may not be charged against a deposit. Nonrefundable fees are treated differently and are restricted in some states. Every one of these rules varies by state; the state portion governs.

### § 12.9 Risk management in property management

Managers protect owners by carrying and verifying insurance (property, liability, loss of rents; vendor certificates of insurance); inspecting for hazards (lighting, walkways, pools, railings, smoke and carbon monoxide detectors); complying with lead-based paint disclosure for pre-1978 housing (the cached lead paint statute governs); responding to habitability complaints promptly; and following the state's eviction procedure exactly rather than using self-help — lockouts and utility shutoffs are unlawful in virtually every state.

### § 12.10 Landlord and tenant rights and obligations (general)

Tenants have the right to possession and quiet enjoyment for the term, to habitable premises (under an implied warranty of habitability recognized in most states for residential leases), to advance notice before the landlord enters except in emergencies, and to return of the deposit as § 12.8 describes. Tenants must pay rent when due, avoid damage beyond ordinary wear, and give required notice before leaving. Landlords must deliver possession, maintain the premises and common areas, make required disclosures, and use legal process to recover possession. Retaliatory eviction is prohibited in most states. Details vary by state; the state portion governs.

## § 13 Common confusions summarized

### § 13.1 Quick distinctions

- *Exclusive agency vs. exclusive right to sell*: under exclusive agency the seller may sell personally without commission; under exclusive right to sell the broker is paid regardless of who finds the buyer.
- *Price fixing vs. a firm's own policy*: agreement among competing firms is illegal; one firm setting its own rates is not.
- *Independent contractor for tax vs. supervision*: tax status never relieves the broker of the duty to supervise.
- *Commingling vs. conversion*: mixing funds, versus spending them.
