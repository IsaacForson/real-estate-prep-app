# REP Ref. Property Condition & Environmental

Authored reference note for the national portion of real estate licensing examinations. Coverage: the non-statutory portions of Pearson VUE domain VI (VI.A, VI.B) and PSI domain VII (VII.A–VII.C): condition disclosure, inspections and red flags, environmental hazards, site assessments, and licensee liability. The mechanics of the federal lead-based paint disclosure rule, CERCLA liability, and the flood insurance purchase requirement are in the cached statutes (42 U.S.C. §§ 4851–4856, 42 U.S.C. §§ 9601–9628, 42 U.S.C. § 4012a) and are cross-referenced rather than restated. Status: draft, needs licensed review. Where a rule varies by state, the state portion of the examination governs.

## § 1 Property condition disclosure fundamentals

### § 1.1 Material fact

A material fact is any fact a reasonable person would consider important in deciding whether to buy, sell, or lease a property, or on what terms. Material facts include the physical condition of the property (a leaking roof, a failed septic system), legal matters affecting it (an unrecorded easement, a pending special assessment, a zoning violation), and matters affecting the transaction (a buyer's inability to obtain financing). Materiality is judged by effect on a reasonable person's decision, not by the size of the repair bill: a $400 repair for a recurring sewer backup is material because it reveals an ongoing problem.

### § 1.2 Latent versus patent defects

A patent defect is visible or discoverable through ordinary, reasonable inspection: a cracked window, a sagging gutter. A latent defect is hidden and would not be found by a reasonably diligent inspection: a foundation crack behind finished basement walls, a buried heating-oil tank, a roof that leaks only in wind-driven rain. The general rule in most states is that a seller (and a licensee) must disclose known latent material defects but may rely on the buyer's own inspection for patent ones. A defect latent to a buyer may be patent to a trained inspector, which is one reason licensees recommend professional inspections.

### § 1.3 Caveat emptor and the modern disclosure duty

The historical rule of caveat emptor ("let the buyer beware") placed the whole burden of discovery on the buyer. Most states now require a residential seller to disclose known material defects, and a majority use a statutory seller's property condition disclosure form that the seller completes and signs and the buyer acknowledges before or at contract. The form is the seller's statement, not the licensee's, and typically covers roof, structure, water intrusion, plumbing, electrical, heating and cooling, pests, environmental conditions, and boundary disputes. Sellers answer to actual knowledge; the form is not a warranty and does not replace an inspection. Whether the form is mandatory, which transfers are exempt (commonly new construction, foreclosure and estate sales, transfers between relatives), the delivery deadline, and any rescission right vary by state; the state portion governs. A seller who answers falsely faces rescission and damages regardless of "as-is" language.

### § 1.4 "As-is" clauses

An "as-is" clause means the seller conveys the property in its present condition and will not make or pay for repairs. It shifts the risk of unknown defects to the buyer and limits post-inspection repair demands. In most states it does not relieve the seller of the duty to disclose known material latent defects, does not excuse active concealment, and protects neither seller nor licensee against fraud. Buyers purchasing "as-is" should still inspect; sellers should still disclose. A licensee should never tell a buyer that "as-is" means the seller has nothing to disclose.

### § 1.5 Stigmatized property

A stigmatized property is one buyers may shun for psychological reasons unrelated to physical condition: a death or violent crime on the premises, a reputation for being haunted, a prior occupant's illness. Most states provide by statute that such circumstances are not material facts requiring disclosure, though many require a truthful answer if a buyer asks directly. Two cases are governed by other law. The Fair Housing Act treats a prior occupant's HIV or AIDS status as disability information; a licensee must not disclose it. The presence of registered sex offenders nearby is handled under state versions of "Megan's Law," which commonly direct licensees to refer buyers to the public registry rather than research and disclose it. Both vary by state; the state portion governs.

### § 1.6 The licensee's duties

A licensee's disclosure duty runs to all parties, not only the client. The licensee must (a) disclose to any party the material facts about the property that the licensee actually knows; (b) not misrepresent any fact by affirmative statement, half-truth, or concealment; and (c) not repeat as fact something the licensee has reason to doubt. A seller's instruction to keep a defect confidential does not override the duty; loyalty to the client never authorizes deceiving a third party. Whether a licensee must also conduct a visual inspection and disclose what a reasonably competent inspection would reveal is a state-law question; some states impose that duty on the listing licensee for residential property and others do not. The state portion governs. A licensee need not disclose facts protected by fair housing law (§ 1.5) or client confidences that are not material facts about the property, such as the seller's motivation or bottom-line price.

### § 1.7 Puffing versus misrepresentation

Puffing is opinion or exaggeration a reasonable buyer recognizes as sales talk ("a wonderful family neighborhood," "the best view on the street"). It is not actionable because no reasonable person relies on it as fact. A statement becomes a misrepresentation when it asserts a fact that can be true or false ("the roof was replaced last year," "the basement has never flooded") and the fact is untrue. The line is crossed most often when a licensee answers a specific question with a guess instead of "I don't know; let's find out."

### § 1.8 Worked example

A seller tells the listing licensee, "The basement took on two inches of water in last spring's storm, but I fixed the grading and it hasn't happened since. Don't mention it." The water intrusion is a material fact, and it is latent because the basement is dry and freshly painted. The licensee must explain that it must be disclosed on the seller's form and to any buyer, that concealment exposes the seller to rescission and damages and the licensee to discipline, and that the licensee will not participate. If the seller refuses, the licensee should withdraw from the listing. Painting the basement to hide staining would convert nondisclosure into active concealment, a form of fraud (§ 5.2).

## § 2 Home inspections and red flags

### § 2.1 What a home inspection is

A home inspection is a visual, non-invasive examination of the readily accessible systems and components of a home, performed by a trained inspector for a fee and reported in writing: roof, visible structure, exterior and drainage, electrical, plumbing, heating and cooling, interior, insulation and ventilation, and built-in appliances. The inspector operates normal controls but does not dismantle equipment, move furniture, or open walls. A home inspection is not a code-compliance inspection, an appraisal, or a warranty, and it is not a pest, radon, mold, lead, asbestos, septic, well-water, or structural-engineering evaluation; each is a separate specialist service the inspector may recommend. Inspector licensing exists in many states and not others; the state portion governs.

### § 2.2 The inspection contingency

Purchase agreements commonly give the buyer a stated period (often 7 to 14 days) after acceptance to inspect and then accept the property, request repairs or a credit, or terminate and recover the earnest money. Whether silence equals acceptance and whether the buyer may terminate for any result or only for material defects depend on the form and vary by state. Once the contingency expires or is waived, the buyer generally takes the property subject to defects the inspection revealed or could have revealed.

### § 2.3 Red flags a licensee should recognize

A licensee is not an inspector but should recognize indicators that call for expert evaluation. Water: stains on ceilings or at the base of walls, efflorescence (white mineral deposits) on masonry, musty odor, a constantly running sump pump, fresh paint on one isolated area, dehumidifiers in a finished basement. Structure: horizontal foundation cracks or cracks wider than about a quarter inch, stair-step cracks in block, sticking doors and windows, sloping floors, gaps between chimney and house. Electrical: knob-and-tube or aluminum branch wiring, scorch marks at outlets. Plumbing: galvanized steel or polybutylene supply pipe, slow drains, a corroded water heater. Environmental indicators (§ 3): a fill or vent pipe in the yard or basement wall (buried oil tank), old pipe insulation or 9-by-9-inch floor tiles (asbestos), chalking paint in a pre-1978 home (lead), lush strips or odors over a drainfield (septic failure). Site: standing water, grading toward the house, large trees over the sewer line. None is proof of a defect; each is a reason to recommend an expert.

### § 2.4 Wood-destroying organisms

Termites, carpenter ants, powder-post beetles, and wood-decay fungi are grouped as wood-destroying organisms (WDO). Evidence includes mud tubes on foundation walls, hollow or blistered wood, discarded wings, and frass (sawdust-like droppings). A WDO inspection is separate from a home inspection, is often required by lenders (customarily seller-paid on VA loans; varies by region), and is reported on a standardized form in many states. Treatment and repair are negotiated in the contract.

### § 2.5 The licensee's role in inspections

Recommend a professional inspection to every buyer in writing; provide several qualified inspectors rather than steering to one; never discourage an inspection; encourage the buyer to attend; and leave interpretation to the inspector and other experts. A licensee who says "that crack is nothing to worry about" has offered an expert opinion the licensee is not qualified to give. If a seller refuses to permit inspections, that fact itself should be disclosed.

## § 3 Environmental hazards

### § 3.1 Asbestos

Asbestos is a group of naturally occurring fibrous minerals valued for fire resistance and insulation and used in building products through the 1970s: pipe and boiler insulation, sprayed acoustic ("popcorn") ceilings, 9-inch vinyl floor tiles, ceiling tiles, roofing and siding shingles, joint compound, and vermiculite attic insulation. Buildings built before about 1980 are most likely to contain it. Inhaled fibers cause asbestosis (lung scarring), lung cancer, and mesothelioma, appearing decades after exposure. The hazard arises when the material is friable (crumbles under hand pressure) or is disturbed; intact, undisturbed asbestos is generally a lower risk than a badly done removal. Management options are encapsulation (sealing with a coating or wrap), enclosure (an airtight barrier), and abatement (removal) by trained and, in most states, licensed contractors with disposal at an approved facility. There is no general federal requirement that a residential seller test for asbestos, but known asbestos is a material fact.

### § 3.2 Lead-based paint

The Consumer Product Safety Commission banned lead-based paint for residential use in 1978; housing built before 1978 is presumed to contain it, and the older the house the more likely. For regulatory purposes lead-based paint contains lead at or above 1.0 milligram per square centimeter or 0.5 percent by weight. The hazard is lead dust and chips from deteriorating paint or from friction, impact, and renovation, plus lead-contaminated soil around the foundation. Children under age six are most at risk because lead damages the developing nervous system (learning disabilities, reduced IQ, behavioral problems); pregnant women are also at elevated risk. The disclosure rule for sales and leases of pre-1978 "target housing" (disclosure form, EPA pamphlet, 10-day inspection opportunity for buyers, 3-year record retention) is in the cached statute at 42 U.S.C. § 4852d and is not restated. An inspection determines whether lead-based paint is present; a risk assessment identifies hazards; interim controls (repair, repainting, specialized cleaning) reduce hazards temporarily; abatement (removal, enclosure, encapsulation, component replacement) is permanent and must be done by certified firms. Under the EPA Renovation, Repair, and Painting rule (effective 2010), contractors paid to disturb painted surfaces in pre-1978 housing must be certified and use lead-safe practices. Lead in drinking water from lead pipes, solder, and brass fixtures is a separate issue.

### § 3.3 Radon

Radon is a colorless, odorless, radioactive gas from the natural decay of uranium in soil and rock. It enters through cracks in slabs and foundation walls, floor drains, sump pits, crawl spaces, and gaps around pipes, and can be released from well water; it accumulates in basements and first floors. It is the second leading cause of lung cancer after smoking and the leading cause among nonsmokers. It is measured in picocuries per liter (pCi/L). The EPA action level is 4 pCi/L: at or above it, fix the home; between 2 and 4, EPA recommends considering a fix. Average indoor level is about 1.3 pCi/L and outdoor about 0.4. Radon can occur in any home in any state; EPA's county map groups counties into Zone 1 (predicted average indoor level above 4 pCi/L), Zone 2 (2 to 4), and Zone 3 (below 2), but the map is a screening tool, never a substitute for testing the house. Tests are short-term (2 to 90 days) or long-term (more than 90 days); transactions usually use a 48-hour to 7-day test under closed-house conditions. Mitigation is generally active soil (sub-slab) depressurization: a pipe through the slab into the gravel beneath, with a continuously running fan venting soil gas above the roofline; sealing cracks alone is not an adequate fix. Many states certify testers and mitigators and some require radon disclosure at sale; the state portion governs.

### § 3.4 Mold

Molds grow wherever moisture, a food source (wood, drywall paper, dust), and moderate temperature coexist. Spores are everywhere; the building problem is uncontrolled moisture from leaks, flooding, condensation, and poor ventilation. Health effects are mainly allergic reactions, asthma aggravation, and respiratory irritation, with greater sensitivity in children, the elderly, and the immunocompromised. There is no federal exposure standard for mold and no federal disclosure requirement; a few states have mold-specific laws, and known mold or water intrusion is a material fact everywhere. EPA guidance: fix the water problem first, because cleaning without addressing moisture means the mold returns. EPA suggests an area under about 10 square feet (roughly 3 feet by 3 feet) can usually be cleaned by the occupant, while larger areas, sewage-related mold, or mold in HVAC systems call for a professional. Porous materials that cannot be dried (carpet, ceiling tile, drywall) are usually discarded. "Black mold" is one genus among many; color does not determine hazard.

### § 3.5 Formaldehyde and urea-formaldehyde foam insulation

Formaldehyde is a pungent colorless gas used in adhesives and resins. It off-gasses from pressed-wood products (particleboard, medium-density fiberboard, hardwood plywood paneling); manufactured homes historically had higher levels, and federal manufactured-housing standards now limit emissions. Urea-formaldehyde foam insulation (UFFI) was pumped into wall cavities of existing homes in the 1970s as an energy retrofit; improperly mixed or cured foam released high levels of formaldehyde. The Consumer Product Safety Commission banned UFFI in 1982, a federal appeals court vacated the ban in 1983, and the product fell out of use anyway. Emissions decline substantially with time, so decades-old UFFI typically emits little today, but it remains a question on some state forms. Health effects are eye, nose, and throat irritation, respiratory symptoms, and at high long-term exposure an elevated cancer risk. Remedies are ventilation, removing or sealing the source, and controlling heat and humidity.

### § 3.6 Underground storage tanks

An underground storage tank (UST) is a tank and connected piping with at least 10 percent of combined volume below ground. Federal regulation under Subtitle I of the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act, administered by EPA and delegated to states, covers tanks holding petroleum or certain hazardous substances, chiefly at gas stations and industrial sites, and requires registration, leak detection, corrosion protection, financial responsibility, and proper closure. The federal exemptions most relevant to residential practice are tanks storing heating oil for use on the premises and farm or residential tanks of 1,100 gallons or less storing motor fuel for noncommercial use. Many states regulate residential heating-oil tanks anyway, and exemption from regulation is not exemption from liability: a leaking tank contaminates soil and groundwater, cleanup can cost tens of thousands of dollars, and the owner pays. Indicators are fill and vent pipes at the ground or foundation, an oil furnace or evidence of a former one, oil odors, and dead vegetation. Proper closure is removal or, where impractical, in-place closure by emptying, cleaning, and filling the tank with inert material, in either case with soil testing and paperwork kept for future disclosure. A known tank, whether in use, abandoned, or closed, is a material fact.

### § 3.7 Groundwater, wells, and septic systems

Properties outside municipal service areas rely on private wells and on-site sewage disposal. The federal Safe Drinking Water Act regulates public water systems, not private wells, so well owners test their own water; recommended annual tests include total coliform bacteria, nitrates, pH, and total dissolved solids, with other contaminants (arsenic, radon, volatile organic compounds) based on local conditions. Well problems include low yield (gallons per minute) and contamination from nearby septic systems, agriculture, or leaking tanks. A conventional septic system is a septic tank, where solids settle and partially digest, and a drainfield (leach field), where effluent percolates through soil. Sizing a new system requires a percolation test of soil absorption and, in most places, a health-department permit. Signs of failure are slow drains, sewage odors, wet or unusually lush areas over the drainfield, and backups; causes include neglect (tanks should be pumped roughly every 3 to 5 years), overloading, and root intrusion. Many jurisdictions require a septic inspection or well test at transfer, and a system that cannot be repaired on the lot may make a property effectively unbuildable; requirements vary by state and locality. Groundwater contamination from landfills, dry cleaners, and gas stations can migrate under neighboring properties, which is why a Phase I assessment (§ 4.2) reviews surrounding land uses.

### § 3.8 Electromagnetic fields

Electromagnetic fields (EMF) are produced by anything carrying electric current: transmission lines, substations, transformers, household wiring. Field strength drops rapidly with distance. Decades of research have not established a consistent causal link between residential power-frequency EMF and disease, and there is no federal exposure standard or disclosure requirement. The value effect of transmission-line proximity is a matter of perception, view, and noise rather than proven harm. A licensee should answer questions honestly, avoid asserting either that EMF is dangerous or that it is proven safe, and refer buyers to the utility or public health sources.

### § 3.9 Brownfields and Superfund sites

A brownfield is property whose reuse is complicated by the presence or potential presence of a hazardous substance, pollutant, or contaminant: former factories, gas stations, dry cleaners, rail yards, typically with moderate contamination. The EPA Brownfields Program and state voluntary cleanup programs provide grants and liability protections to encourage redevelopment; the 2002 CERCLA amendments creating the bona fide prospective purchaser defense (§ 4.1) were part of this policy. Superfund sites are the most seriously contaminated properties, placed by EPA on the National Priorities List and cleaned up under CERCLA by responsible parties or by EPA, which then recovers costs. A greenfield is undeveloped land with no contamination history. Known proximity to either kind of site is a material fact and can affect financing and insurance.

### § 3.10 Wetlands

Wetlands are areas saturated by surface or groundwater often enough to support vegetation adapted to wet soil (marshes, swamps, bogs), identified by three indicators: wetland hydrology, hydric soils, and hydrophytic plants. Under Section 404 of the Clean Water Act, discharging dredged or fill material into waters of the United States, including jurisdictional wetlands, requires a permit from the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers with EPA oversight; individual permits involve public notice and often mitigation (creating or restoring wetlands elsewhere), while general permits cover minor activities. Supreme Court decisions, most recently in 2023, have narrowed federal jurisdiction to wetlands with a continuous surface connection to traditional navigable waters, but many states and localities regulate wetlands and buffers beyond the federal definition. A wetland delineation (a field survey by a qualified professional, often confirmed by the Corps) determines how much of a parcel is buildable; a licensee marketing land as buildable without knowing whether it contains wetlands takes a serious risk. Known or mapped wetlands are material facts.

### § 3.11 Endangered species

The Endangered Species Act of 1973, administered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (land and freshwater species) and the National Marine Fisheries Service (marine species), lists species as endangered or threatened and may designate critical habitat. It prohibits the "take" of a listed species, including significant habitat modification that actually kills or injures wildlife by impairing breeding, feeding, or sheltering. A landowner who wants to develop such habitat may need an incidental take permit supported by a habitat conservation plan. The presence of a listed species or critical habitat can delay, reduce, or prevent development and is a material fact for land buyers. Many states have their own endangered species laws; the state portion governs.

### § 3.12 Flood zones and FEMA flood maps

The National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP), created in 1968 and administered by the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA), makes federally backed flood insurance available in communities that adopt and enforce floodplain management ordinances. FEMA publishes Flood Insurance Rate Maps (FIRMs), viewable through its online map service center. The Special Flood Hazard Area (SFHA) is land with at least a 1 percent annual chance of flooding, commonly called the 100-year floodplain; the name misleads, because a property in the SFHA has roughly a 26 percent chance of flooding during a 30-year mortgage. SFHA zones begin with A (inland flooding: A, AE, AH, AO, A99) or V (coastal areas with wave hazard: V, VE). Zone X (formerly B and C) lies outside the SFHA: shaded X is the 0.2 percent annual chance (500-year) area and unshaded X is minimal risk. Zone D is undetermined. The base flood elevation (BFE) is the computed height of the 1 percent flood, and a surveyor's elevation certificate documents a building's lowest floor relative to it. An owner who believes a structure was mapped into the SFHA in error may apply for a Letter of Map Amendment (LOMA). Standard homeowners insurance does not cover flood; flood coverage is a separate NFIP or private policy, and NFIP policies generally have a 30-day waiting period except when purchased for a loan closing. NFIP residential limits are $250,000 for the building and $100,000 for contents. The mandatory purchase requirement for loans from federally regulated or insured lenders on buildings in an SFHA is in the cached statute at 42 U.S.C. § 4012a. Whether a seller must disclose flood zone status, prior flooding, or flood claims varies by state; a property's flood zone and any known flooding history are material facts everywhere, and licensees should direct buyers to the FEMA map and an insurance quote early, because premiums affect affordability and loan qualification.

### § 3.13 Earthquake and other natural hazard zones

Some states require sellers to disclose whether a property lies within a designated natural hazard zone. The most developed example is California's statutory natural hazard disclosure statement, covering earthquake fault zones and seismic hazard zones, very high fire hazard severity zones, state fire responsibility areas, dam inundation areas, and flood hazard areas, based on official maps. Other regional hazards include hurricane wind zones, sinkholes in limestone regions, wildfire, landslides, expansive clay soils that crack foundations, and subsidence over former mines. Older buildings in seismic areas may need retrofitting (foundation bolting, cripple-wall bracing), and earthquake insurance, like flood insurance, is excluded from standard homeowners policies. Which hazards must be disclosed, on what form, and from which maps vary by state; the state portion governs. A licensee who knows a property is in a mapped hazard zone knows a material fact.

## § 4 Environmental site assessments and the innocent landowner concept

### § 4.1 Why buyers investigate: CERCLA liability in plain terms

The Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA, the "Superfund" law, cached at 42 U.S.C. §§ 9601–9628) makes current owners and operators of a contaminated site, owners and operators at the time of disposal, generators who arranged for disposal, and transporters liable for cleanup costs. Liability is strict (no fault or negligence required), joint and several (any one party can be made to pay the whole cost and must seek contribution from the others), and retroactive (it reaches disposal before the 1980 enactment). A buyer who did nothing to cause contamination can therefore become liable simply by taking title. Congress created three landowner defenses, each requiring the buyer to have performed "all appropriate inquiries" (AAI) into the property's prior ownership and uses before acquiring it and to meet continuing obligations afterward (cooperating with regulators, complying with land-use restrictions, taking reasonable steps to stop ongoing releases). The innocent landowner defense protects a buyer who conducted AAI and did not know and had no reason to know of contamination. The bona fide prospective purchaser defense, added by the 2002 Brownfields amendments, protects a buyer who conducted AAI and knowingly bought contaminated property, provided all disposal occurred before purchase and the continuing obligations are met; this is what makes brownfield redevelopment possible. The contiguous property owner defense protects a neighbor whose land is contaminated by migration from an adjacent site. EPA's AAI regulation (40 C.F.R. Part 312) specifies what an inquiry must include; a compliant Phase I assessment (§ 4.2) satisfies it. A buyer who skips the inquiry has no defense.

### § 4.2 Phase I environmental site assessment

A Phase I ESA is a non-intrusive investigation by an environmental professional to identify recognized environmental conditions: the presence or likely presence of hazardous substances or petroleum products from a past or present release, or a material threat of one. Its components are (1) a records review of historical sources (aerial photographs, fire insurance maps, city directories, prior deeds) and regulatory databases (Superfund sites, storage tanks, hazardous waste generators, spills, landfills) for the subject and surrounding properties; (2) a site reconnaissance looking for stains, drums, tanks, fill pipes, stressed vegetation, and odors; (3) interviews with the owner, occupants, and local officials, plus a user questionnaire completed by the buyer; and (4) a signed written report with findings, opinions, and conclusions. A Phase I involves no sampling. Under the AAI rule the interviews, lien search, records review, site reconnaissance, and professional's declaration must be performed or updated within 180 days before acquisition, and the whole report is stale after one year. Commercial and industrial lenders almost always require a Phase I; residential transactions rarely involve one unless the property has a suspicious history.

### § 4.3 Phase II environmental site assessment

When a Phase I identifies recognized environmental conditions, a Phase II investigates them by sampling and laboratory analysis: soil borings, groundwater monitoring wells, soil-gas probes, surface water and sediment samples, and, where relevant, building-material samples for asbestos or lead. The goal is to confirm whether contamination is present, identify the substances, and estimate extent and concentration relative to regulatory cleanup standards. A Phase II is intrusive, much more expensive than a Phase I, and often iterative as sampling expands to define a plume. Its results drive the decision to proceed, renegotiate, require seller remediation, or walk away.

### § 4.4 Phase III: remediation

Phase III is remediation: designing and carrying out a cleanup under oversight of the state environmental agency or EPA and documenting that standards have been met. Techniques include excavation and off-site disposal of soil, treatment of groundwater, soil vapor extraction, bioremediation, capping, and institutional controls such as recorded deed restrictions limiting future use to nonresidential purposes or prohibiting wells. Monitoring may continue for years. Completion is usually documented by a state "no further action" letter or certificate of completion, which lenders and future buyers rely on and which should be preserved with the property records. Who pays for remediation is negotiated in light of the parties' relative CERCLA exposure.

### § 4.5 Worked scenario

An investor contracts to buy a vacant commercial lot that a Phase I reveals was a gasoline station from 1955 to 1988. The records review shows registered tanks but no closure report, and the site walk finds a capped fill pipe near the sidewalk: recognized environmental conditions. A Phase II finds petroleum hydrocarbons above the state cleanup standard near the former tank pit and a small groundwater plume that has not left the site. The parties renegotiate: the seller reduces the price by the engineer's cleanup estimate; the buyer closes, removes the tanks and contaminated soil under a state voluntary cleanup program, and obtains a no further action letter with a deed restriction against residential use. Because the buyer performed all appropriate inquiries within 180 days of closing and meets the continuing obligations, it qualifies as a bona fide prospective purchaser and is protected from CERCLA liability for the pre-purchase contamination even though it knew of it when it bought. Had the buyer relied on the seller's assurance that "the tanks were pulled years ago" and skipped the assessments, it would have taken title with full strict liability and no defense.

## § 5 The licensee's role and liability

### § 5.1 What the licensee should and should not do

A real estate licensee is neither an environmental engineer, home inspector, appraiser, surveyor, nor attorney, and the license does not qualify the holder to render opinions in those fields. The licensee's role is to (a) disclose material facts the licensee knows, to all parties, in writing where possible; (b) review the seller's disclosure form for inconsistencies and follow up on red flags; (c) recommend appropriate professional inspections and tests, and document the recommendation; (d) refer parties to qualified experts and official sources rather than answering technical questions personally; (e) never minimize a condition ("that's normal settling," "radon isn't a problem around here"); and (f) attribute information to its source ("the seller states the roof is 8 years old") rather than adopting it as the licensee's own. When in doubt: "I don't know, and here is who can tell you."

### § 5.2 The spectrum of liability: puffing, innocent, negligent, and fraudulent misrepresentation

Puffing (§ 1.7) is non-actionable opinion. Innocent misrepresentation is a false statement of material fact the speaker honestly and reasonably believed true; it may allow rescission, though damages are usually unavailable. Negligent misrepresentation is a false statement made without reasonable grounds for believing it true, or a failure to disclose what the licensee should have discovered through the care a reasonably competent licensee would exercise; there is no intent to deceive, only a failure of due care, and the injured party may recover actual damages. Fraud (intentional misrepresentation, deceit) requires (1) a false representation of material fact, or active concealment, or nondisclosure where there is a duty to disclose; (2) knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for the truth (scienter); (3) intent that the other party rely; (4) justifiable reliance; and (5) resulting damage. Fraud exposes the wrongdoer to rescission, compensatory damages, punitive damages in many states, license revocation or suspension, and potential criminal prosecution. Active concealment, such as painting over water stains, paneling over a foundation crack, or removing a fill pipe to hide a tank, is fraud even though no words were spoken. Negligent misrepresentation is the category that most often ensnares well-meaning licensees because it requires only carelessness, not bad intent.

### § 5.3 Reliance and verification

A licensee may generally pass along information from a reliable source (the seller's form, public records, a survey) with attribution, but may not repeat information the licensee has reason to doubt or that contradicts what the licensee observed. Quoting square footage, lot size, or acreage as fact without a source, when the figure proves wrong, is a misrepresentation regardless of intent. Whether the standard of care requires an affirmative visual inspection is a state-law question (§ 1.6). Buyers also owe reasonable diligence; a buyer who was told of a defect, or who waived a recommended inspection, will struggle to prove justifiable reliance.

### § 5.4 Remedies and consequences

An injured buyer may seek rescission (unwinding the sale), damages (usually price paid less actual value, or cost of repair), or, in some states, statutory remedies with multiplied damages and attorney fees. Separately, the state regulator may discipline the licensee (reprimand, fine, suspension, revocation) for misrepresentation, concealment, or failure to disclose known material facts, regardless of any court outcome. The broker is generally responsible for affiliated licensees' acts. Errors and omissions insurance covers negligence but excludes intentional fraud. Limitation periods vary by state and often run from discovery of the defect.

### § 5.5 Risk management practices

Written disclosure of every known material fact before the buyer is bound. Written recommendation of inspections and specialist tests, with a signed acknowledgment if the buyer declines. Source attribution for measurements and system ages. Never answering condition or hazard questions from guesswork. Documenting conversations about defects in the file. Directing buyers to the FEMA flood map, the EPA radon map, and public registries. Reporting any seller instruction to conceal to the broker. Maintaining errors and omissions coverage.

### § 5.6 Worked scenarios

Scenario A. A buyer asks the listing licensee whether the neighborhood has ever flooded. The licensee, who has never checked, answers, "No, this area is high and dry." The property is in Zone AE and flooded twice in the past decade. The licensee had no reasonable basis for the statement: negligent misrepresentation, and fraud if the licensee had seen the map or knew of the flooding. The correct response was to direct the buyer to the FEMA map and the seller's disclosure and recommend a flood insurance quote.

Scenario B. A seller's form states "no known water intrusion." During a showing the buyer's licensee notices efflorescence on the basement block wall and a running dehumidifier. The licensee need not diagnose the cause, but these are red flags a reasonably competent licensee would recognize; the licensee should point them out and recommend an inspection. Saying nothing, or reassuring the buyer that "all basements are a little damp," risks negligent misrepresentation.

Scenario C. A licensee lists a pre-1978 home. The seller has never tested for lead and has no reports. The licensee ensures the buyer receives the federal disclosure form, the EPA pamphlet, and the 10-day testing opportunity as the cached statute requires, and marks the form to show the seller has no knowledge and no records. The licensee does not say "the paint is fine, it's all been covered," because that is an opinion outside the licensee's expertise and painting over lead paint does not eliminate the hazard.

Scenario D. A buyer's licensee learns that a resident died in the house of natural causes last year. In most states this is not a material fact requiring disclosure, and if the death were AIDS-related, disclosing the cause would violate fair housing law. If the buyer asks directly, the licensee must answer truthfully or decline to answer, never lie; the state rule governs.

## § 6 Quick reference

### § 6.1 Numbers to know

- Lead-based paint: residential use banned 1978; threshold 1.0 mg/cm² or 0.5 percent by weight; children under 6 most at risk; RRP rule effective 2010; disclosure rule gives buyers a 10-day testing opportunity.
- Radon: EPA action level 4 pCi/L; consider fixing between 2 and 4; average indoor 1.3, outdoor 0.4; Zone 1 counties predicted above 4; short-term test 2 to 90 days; standard fix is active soil (sub-slab) depressurization.
- Mold: no federal exposure standard; under about 10 square feet can usually be handled by the occupant; fix moisture first.
- UFFI: CPSC ban 1982, vacated 1983; emissions decline with age.
- USTs: 10 percent or more of volume underground; federal exemptions for on-premises heating oil and for farm or residential motor-fuel tanks of 1,100 gallons or less.
- Flood: NFIP created 1968; SFHA is the 1 percent annual chance area (zones A and V); shaded X is 0.2 percent; about 26 percent chance of flooding over a 30-year mortgage in the SFHA; NFIP residential limits $250,000 building and $100,000 contents; 30-day waiting period except at loan closing.
- CERCLA: enacted 1980; liability strict, joint and several, retroactive; defenses require all appropriate inquiries; Phase I components updated within 180 days and the whole report within 1 year of acquisition; bona fide prospective purchaser defense added 2002.
- Septic: pump roughly every 3 to 5 years; percolation test sizes a new drainfield.
