# REP Ref. Property & Land Use

Authored reference note. Status: draft — needs licensed review. Original expository text written for the REP item pipeline; items cite it by section (for example `REP Ref. Property & Land Use § 3.3`). Where a rule differs among states, this note says so; the state portion of the exam governs.

## § 1 Real property, personal property, and fixtures

### § 1.1 Land, real estate, and real property

Land is the surface of the earth, everything beneath it to the center of the earth, the airspace above it, and things attached by nature such as trees and water. Real estate is land plus permanent human-made attachments (improvements): buildings, fences, wells, paving. Real property is real estate plus the legal rights of ownership, often described as a bundle of rights: to possess, to control and use, to enjoy without interference, to exclude others, and to dispose (sell, lease, give, mortgage, or will). Each right can be separated and transferred alone; a lease, an easement, and a mortgage each transfer part of the bundle while the owner keeps the rest.

### § 1.2 Personal property

Personal property (chattels, personalty) is everything that is not real property: movable things such as furniture, vehicles, and equipment, and intangible rights such as a promissory note. Real property is conveyed by deed; personal property is conveyed by bill of sale or by delivery. A purchase contract should list any personal property included in the sale, because the deed does not transfer it.

Example. A seller agrees to leave the refrigerator and patio furniture. The deed transfers the house and land; a bill of sale or an itemized contract clause transfers the refrigerator and furniture.

### § 1.3 Fixtures

A fixture is an item that began as personal property and became real property by being attached to land or a building with the intent that it remain. A fixture passes with the real estate unless the contract excludes it. Intent of the person who attached the item is the controlling question, inferred from these tests taken together:

- Method of attachment. Bolted, wired, plumbed, cemented, or built in, so that removal would damage the property, points toward fixture.
- Adaptation. An item custom-fitted to the property (built-in shelving, a furnace sized to the house, storm windows made for specific openings) points toward fixture.
- Agreement. A written agreement between buyer and seller, or landlord and tenant, controls between them regardless of the other tests.
- Relationship of the parties. Ambiguity is resolved against the party who attached the item: in favor of the buyer over the seller and the tenant over the landlord, except for trade fixtures (§ 1.4).

Example. A homeowner screws a television bracket into wall studs and hangs a television on it. Absent an agreement, the bracket is generally a fixture; the television that lifts off is personal property. A careful contract states what stays.

### § 1.4 Trade fixtures

A trade fixture is an item a commercial tenant attaches to leased premises to carry on a business: display shelving, kitchen hoods, a barber's chairs. Although attached, trade fixtures remain the tenant's personal property. The tenant may remove them before the lease ends and must repair the damage removal causes. Trade fixtures left behind after the lease ends generally become the landlord's property by accession. The confusion to avoid: fixtures installed by an owner belong to the real estate; trade fixtures installed by a business tenant belong to the tenant.

### § 1.5 Crops and emblements

Perennial plants that grow without annual cultivation (trees, shrubs, native grasses) are real property until severed. Annual crops produced by cultivation are treated as personal property of the person who planted them and are called emblements. Under the doctrine of emblements, a tenant whose tenancy ends through no fault of the tenant at an unpredictable time may re-enter to harvest crops the tenant planted. A farmland contract should state who owns crops in the ground at closing.

### § 1.6 Severance, annexation, and manufactured homes

Real property becomes personal property by severance (cutting timber, mining ore, detaching a fixture). Personal property becomes real property by annexation (installing a furnace, planting an orchard). A manufactured home is personal property while it carries a vehicle-type title; it may become real property when permanently affixed to a foundation on land the owner holds and a state conversion or title-surrender procedure is completed. Procedure varies by state.

### § 1.7 Appurtenances and severable rights

An appurtenance is a right that belongs to and passes with the land even if the deed does not name it, such as an appurtenant easement (§ 7.2). Several rights in the bundle can be severed and sold or leased separately. Subsurface (mineral) rights may be sold apart from the surface, and the mineral owner ordinarily has an implied right of reasonable surface access unless the deed says otherwise. Air rights, the right to use the space above the surface subject to navigable-airspace rules, can be sold or leased so that a building can rise above a rail yard. Water rights follow one of two systems: under riparian (streams) and littoral (lakes, seas) doctrines, an owner whose land touches the water has a right to reasonable use; under prior appropriation, used in many western states, water rights are obtained by permit apart from land ownership and the earliest user has priority. Which applies varies by state.

Example. A farmer conveys "the surface estate only," reserving all oil, gas, and minerals. When the surface owner later sells, the mineral estate does not pass, because the farmer severed it and the reservation appears in the chain of title.

## § 2 Characteristics of land

### § 2.1 Physical characteristics

Land has three physical characteristics that never change. Immobility: land cannot be moved, so real estate markets are local and the law of the place where the land sits governs it. Indestructibility: land is durable; improvements wear out but the land remains, which is why land is not depreciated while buildings are. Uniqueness (non-homogeneity): no two parcels are identical because no two occupy the same location. Uniqueness is the legal basis for specific performance in real estate contracts: money damages are considered inadequate because the buyer cannot buy an identical substitute.

### § 2.2 Economic characteristics

Land has four economic characteristics. Scarcity: the supply of land in a given location is finite, so scarcity in a desirable area pushes value up. Improvements (modification): building on or near a parcel changes the value and use of that parcel and its neighbors, as a new interchange or a sewage plant can for decades. Permanence of investment (fixity): capital placed in land and improvements is long-lived and cannot be withdrawn quickly. Area preference (situs): people prefer some locations for convenience, prestige, schools, or climate; situs is often called the most important economic characteristic because two identical houses in different locations sell for different prices.

### § 2.3 Applying the characteristics

Example. A seller sues a defaulting buyer for specific performance of a contract on a lakefront lot. The remedy rests on uniqueness; if the buyer argues an inland lot is just as good, the answer is situs.

## § 3 Legal descriptions and surveys

### § 3.1 Purpose of a legal description

A legal description identifies a parcel so precisely that it can be located on the ground and distinguished from every other parcel. A street address is not a legal description: addresses change, repeat across towns, and do not define boundaries. Deeds, mortgages, long leases, and other recorded documents use one of three systems: metes and bounds (§ 3.2), the rectangular (government) survey (§ 3.3), or lot and block by reference to a recorded plat (§ 3.4). Descriptions often combine systems, for example metes and bounds beginning at a section corner.

### § 3.2 Metes and bounds

Metes are measured distances and directions; bounds are boundaries formed by monuments and adjoining lands. A metes-and-bounds description starts at a point of beginning (POB) tied to a monument (an iron pin, a stone, a stream, a section corner), travels around the parcel in courses each stated as a bearing and a distance (for example "North 45 degrees 30 minutes East, 210.5 feet"), and must return to the point of beginning so the figure closes. It is the oldest system, used throughout the original eastern states and wherever parcels are irregular.

Monuments may be natural (a tree, a river) or artificial (a pin, a post, a road). When elements of a description conflict, the usual rule of construction ranks natural monuments first, then artificial monuments, then adjoining boundaries, then courses, then distances, and stated area last, because what a surveyor could see on the ground is more reliable than numbers copied in an office.

Example. "Beginning at an iron pin at the northeast corner of Mill Road and County Route 9; thence North 10 degrees East 300 feet to a pin; thence South 80 degrees East 200 feet to a pin; thence South 10 degrees West 300 feet to a pin on the north line of Mill Road; thence along said line North 80 degrees West 200 feet to the point of beginning, containing 1.38 acres more or less." The parcel is a 300-by-200-foot rectangle: 60,000 square feet / 43,560 = 1.38 acres.

### § 3.3 Rectangular (government) survey system

The rectangular survey, also called the government survey or Public Land Survey System (PLSS), was authorized by the Land Ordinance of 1785 to divide public lands west of the original states. It is not used in the original Atlantic states and a few others, notably Texas and Hawaii. The grid:

- A principal meridian is a north-south line and a base line is an east-west line crossing it; every description is referenced to a named principal meridian.
- Township lines run east-west every 6 miles north and south of the base line; the strips between them are tiers, numbered Township 1 North, 2 North, and so on (or South).
- Range lines run north-south every 6 miles east and west of the principal meridian; the strips between them are ranges, numbered Range 1 East, 2 East, and so on (or West).
- Where a tier and a range cross is a township: 6 miles by 6 miles, 36 square miles, 36 sections.
- A section is 1 mile square: 1 square mile, 640 acres, 5,280 feet per side.
- Sections are numbered 1 through 36 in a serpentine pattern: Section 1 in the northeast corner, west across the top row to Section 6, down to Section 7 beneath Section 6, east to Section 12, and so on, ending with Section 36 in the southeast corner. Section 16 was historically reserved for schools.
- Because meridians converge toward the pole, standard parallels (correction lines) are run every 24 miles north and south of the base line and guide meridians every 24 miles east and west of the principal meridian, where range lines are re-measured. Within a township the excess or shortage is placed in the sections along the north and west edges, so those may not be exactly 640 acres. Irregular pieces along water or grid edges are numbered government lots.

Sections are divided into aliquot parts. A quarter section (the NE 1/4) is 160 acres and 2,640 feet per side; a half section is 320 acres; a quarter-quarter (the NW 1/4 of the SE 1/4) is 40 acres and 1,320 feet per side; a quarter of that is 10 acres and 660 feet per side.

Reading a description. It is written from the smallest part to the largest, then section, township, range, and meridian: "the SW 1/4 of the NE 1/4 of Section 14, Township 3 North, Range 2 West of the Sixth Principal Meridian." Locate it by reading backward: find the meridian and base line, count three tiers north and two ranges west, find Section 14, take its northeast quarter, then the southwest quarter of that. Acreage is 640 divided by the product of the fraction denominators: 640 / (4 x 4) = 40 acres. The word "of" means keep dividing; the word "and" joins separate pieces whose acreages are added.

Example. "The N 1/2 of the SE 1/4 and the SE 1/4 of the SW 1/4 of Section 9." First piece 640 / (2 x 4) = 80 acres; second 640 / (4 x 4) = 40 acres; total 120 acres.

### § 3.4 Lot and block (recorded plat)

When land is subdivided, a licensed surveyor prepares a plat: a map showing each lot's boundaries and dimensions, the blocks into which lots are grouped, streets, easements, and monuments. After local approval the plat is recorded in the county's public records, typically in a plat book at a page. From then on a lot is described by reference to the plat, which supplies the detailed measurements. A complete description names the lot number, block number, subdivision name, plat book and page (or instrument number), and the county and state of recording.

Example. "Lot 7, Block 3, Sunrise Estates, according to the plat recorded in Plat Book 12, Page 34, public records of Adams County, State X." Anyone can pull that plat and find Lot 7's surveyed boundaries.

### § 3.5 Surveys, monuments, datum, and benchmarks

A survey is the licensed surveyor's measurement and marking of boundaries, presented in a drawing that shows lines, corners, improvements, easements, and encroachments. A boundary survey locates corners and lines; a survey ordered for a lender or title insurer also locates buildings, fences, and driveways so that encroachments and setback violations can be seen. A survey, not a title search, reveals a neighbor's fence one foot over the line (§ 7.6). Monuments are the physical objects set or found at corners.

Vertical measurement requires a reference plane called a datum. Elevations were historically referred to mean sea level; the current national reference is the North American Vertical Datum of 1988 (NAVD 88), which replaced the 1929 datum, and many cities keep a local datum. A benchmark is a permanent marker (often a metal disk set in concrete or rock) whose elevation above the datum has been precisely established; surveyors measure from benchmarks. Datum and benchmarks are used to describe air lots and condominium units (the space between two elevations within stated horizontal boundaries) and for flood-zone determinations.

### § 3.6 Land and structure measurement basics

Conventional units, stated exactly:

- 1 mile = 5,280 feet = 1,760 yards = 320 rods = 80 chains.
- 1 rod = 16.5 feet. 1 chain = 66 feet = 4 rods = 100 links.
- 1 acre = 43,560 square feet = 4,840 square yards = 160 square rods = 10 square chains.
- 1 square mile = 640 acres = 1 section. 1 township = 36 sections = 36 square miles = 23,040 acres.
- 1 square yard = 9 square feet. 1 square foot = 144 square inches.
- 1 hectare is approximately 2.471 acres.

Area uses ordinary geometry: rectangle, length times width; triangle, one-half base times height; trapezoid, average of the parallel sides times the distance between them. Irregular lots are split into rectangles and triangles and the pieces added. Frontage is the lot's length along the street; depth runs from front line to rear line. Land may be priced per front foot, per square foot, or per acre; convert to one basis before comparing.

Structures are measured differently. Living area of a house is conventionally taken from exterior dimensions of finished, heated, above-grade space; garages, porches, and basements are reported separately. Practice varies, so a listing should state the source of its square footage.

Example. A lot is 125 feet of frontage by 250 feet deep: 31,250 square feet, or 31,250 / 43,560 = 0.717 acre. At $2.50 per square foot the indicated value is $78,125; at $600 per front foot it is 125 x $600 = $75,000.

## § 4 Government powers over private land

### § 4.1 Overview

Private ownership is always subject to four government powers, remembered by the letters P-E-T-E: police power, eminent domain, taxation, and escheat. The pair most often confused: police power regulates use and pays nothing; eminent domain takes title or an interest and must pay.

### § 4.2 Police power

Police power is the state's inherent authority, delegated to counties and cities, to enact and enforce laws protecting public health, safety, morals, and general welfare. Zoning, building codes, subdivision regulations, environmental rules, fire codes, and rent controls where they exist are exercises of it. Regulation under police power is not a taking and requires no compensation so long as it is reasonable, applies uniformly to similar properties, and leaves the owner an economically viable use. A regulation that strips essentially all use may be challenged as a regulatory taking.

### § 4.3 Eminent domain and condemnation

Eminent domain is the government's power to take private property for public use upon payment of just compensation. The Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution ends: "nor shall private property be taken for public use, without just compensation," and state constitutions contain parallel clauses. Condemnation is the procedure by which the power is exercised. The power is delegated to utilities, railroads, and redevelopment agencies under state law.

The taking may be of a whole parcel or a partial interest (a road strip, a power-line easement). Just compensation is ordinarily fair market value of what is taken plus, for a partial taking, any loss in value to the remainder (severance damages). The owner may contest whether the use is public and how much is owed but generally cannot stop a legitimate taking. An owner harmed without a formal proceeding, for example land flooded by a public project, may sue in inverse condemnation to compel payment.

Example. A county widens a road and needs a 15-foot strip along a commercial lot. It files a condemnation action, deposits its appraised value, and takes the strip. The owner disputes the figure and a jury awards more, including lost parking on the remainder. This is eminent domain, not police power, because title to the strip passed to the county.

### § 4.4 Taxation

The general real property tax is ad valorem, based on assessed value. The assessor sets an assessed value (often a stated percentage of market value, in some states adjusted by an equalization factor), exemptions are subtracted where the owner qualifies (homestead, senior, veteran, and similar exemptions vary by state), and taxing bodies apply a rate expressed per hundred, per thousand, or in mills; one mill is one-tenth of a cent, that is, $1 of tax per $1,000 of assessed value. A special assessment is a separate charge levied only on properties that benefit from a specific improvement (a sidewalk, sewer line, or street lighting), commonly apportioned by front footage or benefit. Both become liens on the property (§ 8.2). Property of governments and qualifying religious, educational, and charitable organizations is commonly exempt; details vary by state.

Example. A home assessed at $250,000 with a combined rate of 22 mills owes 250,000 x 0.022 = $5,500. With a $25,000 homestead exemption the tax is 225,000 x 0.022 = $4,950.

### § 4.5 Escheat

Escheat is the reversion of property to the state when an owner dies intestate (without a valid will) and no heirs can be found, or when property is legally abandoned. It exists so that land is never without an owner and operates only after the statutory search for heirs fails. Procedures vary by state.

## § 5 Public land-use controls

### § 5.1 Comprehensive plan

A comprehensive (master, general) plan is a local government's long-range policy statement on how land should be used and where growth and public facilities should go. It is not itself a regulation; zoning and subdivision ordinances implement it, and zoning changes are commonly required to be consistent with it.

### § 5.2 Zoning ordinances

Zoning divides a jurisdiction into districts and prescribes permitted uses and physical rules for each. Use classifications are typically residential (often split by density), commercial, industrial, agricultural, and mixed-use or planned-development districts. Bulk regulations include minimum lot size, setbacks (minimum distance of a building from front, side, and rear lot lines), height limits, maximum lot coverage, floor-area ratio, parking requirements, and buffer zones that separate incompatible districts. Under cumulative zoning a district permits its own uses plus all less intensive uses; under exclusive zoning only listed uses are allowed. Zoning is police power, so no compensation is owed for its ordinary effect on value. Reclassifying one small parcel for its owner's benefit, inconsistently with the plan and surroundings, is spot zoning and generally invalid.

### § 5.3 Nonconforming use

When a new or amended ordinance makes an existing lawful use or structure noncompliant, the existing use is a legal nonconforming use ("grandfathered") and is commonly allowed to continue. The privilege is not a license to grow: ordinances typically forbid enlarging or intensifying the use, terminate it if abandoned for a stated period, and limit rebuilding after destruction. Whether it survives a sale, and the rebuild and abandonment rules, vary by state and ordinance.

Example. A corner grocery operated thirty years before its block was rezoned single-family. It may continue as a nonconforming use, but the owner may not add a second story of retail, and if it closes for the ordinance's abandonment period the right to reopen is lost.

### § 5.4 Variance

A variance permits deviation from a specific zoning requirement because strict application would impose unnecessary hardship unique to the property, not created by the owner, and the deviation would not harm the public or neighborhood. Variances are granted by a zoning board of appeals (board of adjustment) after notice and hearing. An area (dimensional) variance relaxes a physical rule such as a setback or height; a use variance permits a use not otherwise allowed and is harder to obtain, and some jurisdictions do not grant them. The hardship must come from the land itself (odd shape, steep slope, wetland), not from the owner's finances or wishes.

Example. A triangular lot cannot hold any house that meets the 25-foot rear setback. The owner seeks an area variance for a 12-foot setback; because the hardship arises from the lot's shape and predates the owner, the board grants it with conditions.

### § 5.5 Special-use (conditional-use) permit

A special-use permit (conditional-use permit, special exception) authorizes a use the ordinance identifies as appropriate in a district only when stated conditions are met: houses of worship, schools, day-care centers, hospitals, and cemeteries in residential districts are common examples. No hardship is required; the applicant shows that the ordinance's conditions (traffic, parking, screening, hours) will be satisfied. The distinction: a variance excuses compliance with a rule; a special-use permit confirms compliance with the conditions attached to a listed use.

### § 5.6 Rezoning and amendments

An owner who wants a use that neither a variance nor a special-use permit can provide must seek an amendment to the zoning map (rezoning) or text, adopted by the legislative body after hearings. Downzoning reclassifies land to a less intensive use; upzoning goes the other way. A moratorium temporarily suspends development approvals while rules are studied.

### § 5.7 Building codes, permits, and certificates of occupancy

Building codes set minimum construction standards for structure, fire safety, electrical, plumbing, mechanical systems, energy, and accessibility. Construction and substantial alteration require a building permit before work begins; inspectors review the work in stages; when the finished structure passes final inspection the authority issues a certificate of occupancy stating it may lawfully be occupied for its intended use. Unpermitted work is a common resale disclosure issue because the buyer may inherit the duty to bring it into compliance.

### § 5.8 Subdivision regulations

Subdivision regulations govern dividing a tract into lots. The developer submits a preliminary plat, meets requirements for streets, curbs, drainage, water and sewer, utility easements, lot sizes, and open space, may pay impact fees or dedicate land for parks or schools, and records the approved final plat (§ 3.4). Streets on the plat are commonly dedicated to the public.

### § 5.9 Environmental and other regulations

Environmental regulations (wetlands, flood plains, hazardous substances, lead-based paint, septic systems, coastal zones) are further exercises of police power. The federal statutes are cached separately in the pipeline and cited directly.

## § 6 Private land-use controls

### § 6.1 Deed restrictions and restrictive covenants

A private party can limit use of land by a restriction in the deed (a deed restriction) or by recording covenants that bind every lot in a subdivision (restrictive covenants). Typical restrictions limit use to single-family residences, set minimum house sizes, control architecture, fences, colors, and parking, or prohibit businesses. A restriction "runs with the land," binding later owners, because it appears in the recorded chain of title and every purchaser has constructive notice of it. Enforcement is private, by the grantor, the association, or another owner, usually through an injunction; the government does not enforce private restrictions. A restriction that violates public policy or statute, such as one excluding owners by race, religion, or another protected class, is void and unenforceable even if it still appears in an old document.

### § 6.2 Declarations, CC&Rs, and association rules

In subdivisions, condominiums, and other common-interest communities the developer records a declaration of covenants, conditions, and restrictions (CC&Rs) before the first sale. It creates the owners' association, obligates every owner to pay assessments, defines common areas and their maintenance, and states use restrictions. Bylaws govern how the association operates; the board adopts rules on day-to-day matters such as pool hours and parking. Unpaid assessments typically become a lien the association can foreclose; its priority relative to a first mortgage, and the resale-disclosure documents a buyer must receive, vary by state.

### § 6.3 Private restrictions and zoning together

Zoning and private restrictions operate independently and both must be satisfied; in practice the more restrictive controls, because meeting the looser rule does not excuse violating the stricter one. Zoning permission for a duplex does not override a covenant limiting the lot to one house; a covenant allowing a home business does not override a zoning ban.

Example. A covenant sets a minimum house size of 2,400 square feet while zoning requires 1,200. An owner who builds 1,500 square feet complies with zoning but breaches the covenant, and a neighbor can sue to enforce it.

### § 6.4 Termination of private restrictions

Private restrictions end when a stated term expires, when the benefited owners release them in a recorded instrument, when benefited and burdened parcels come under one owner (merger), when a court finds them abandoned through widespread violation or refuses enforcement because the plaintiff waited too long (laches), or when neighborhood conditions have changed so completely that the restriction no longer serves its purpose. Some states extinguish old restrictions not re-recorded within a set period; varies by state.

## § 7 Easements, licenses, and encroachments

### § 7.1 Encumbrances generally

An encumbrance is any right or interest held by someone other than the owner that reduces value or restricts use but does not prevent transfer of title. Encumbrances are either liens, financial claims secured by the property (§ 8), or non-financial encumbrances affecting use or title: easements, private restrictions, encroachments, licenses. Property is conveyed subject to its encumbrances unless they are removed before closing.

### § 7.2 Easements: appurtenant and in gross

An easement is a nonpossessory right to use another's land for a specific purpose. The holder does not own or possess the land; the owner keeps title and may use the land in any way that does not interfere with the easement.

An appurtenant easement benefits a parcel. The benefited parcel is the dominant tenement; the burdened parcel is the servient tenement. They are usually adjacent but need not be. An appurtenant easement runs with the land: it passes automatically with the dominant tenement even if the deed is silent, and it burdens the servient tenement in the hands of every later owner with notice.

An easement in gross benefits a person or entity rather than a parcel; there is a servient tenement but no dominant tenement. Utility easements for power, pipelines, cable, and sewer are the everyday example. Commercial easements in gross are generally assignable and long-lasting; personal easements in gross are generally not assignable and end at the holder's death.

Example. Lot A can reach the road only across Lot B, and Lot B's owner grants Lot A a recorded right-of-way. Lot A is dominant, Lot B servient, and Lot A's buyer receives the right-of-way as an appurtenance. The electric company's line across Lot B is an easement in gross, held by the company rather than by any parcel.

### § 7.3 How easements are created

- Express grant or reservation. The servient owner grants the easement in a deed or separate instrument, or a grantor selling part of a tract reserves an easement over the part sold. An easement is an interest in land, so the Statute of Frauds requires a writing, and recording protects it against later purchasers.
- Implication from prior use. When an owner divides a tract and one part was openly and continuously used to serve the other in a reasonably necessary way (a driveway, a drain line), a court may find the parties intended the use to continue though the deed is silent.
- Necessity. When a division leaves one part landlocked, the law implies an easement of access over the part that keeps road access. It arises from the common ownership at division, requires strict necessity rather than convenience, and ends when the necessity ends.
- Prescription. Open and notorious use of another's land, continuous for the statutory period and without permission (adverse, under claim of right), can ripen into a prescriptive easement. The period varies by state. Unlike adverse possession, the use need not be exclusive, and the result is a right of use rather than title. An owner defeats a forming prescriptive right by granting written permission, which turns the use into a license, or by interrupting it.
- Condemnation. Governments and utilities acquire easements by eminent domain (§ 4.3) and pay for them.
- Dedication. An owner may dedicate an easement or land to public use, as a developer dedicates plat streets.
- Party walls. When one wall straddles a boundary, each owner owns the half on that side and holds an easement of support in the other half, and both share maintenance; agreements are commonly recorded.

### § 7.4 How easements end

- Merger. When one person acquires both tenements the easement is extinguished, because no one holds an easement over their own land; it does not revive if the parcels are later separated.
- Release. The holder releases the right to the servient owner in a written, recorded instrument, often a quitclaim deed.
- Expiration. An easement for a stated term or purpose ends when the term runs or the purpose can no longer be served.
- End of necessity. An easement by necessity ends when the dominant tenement gains other legal access.
- Abandonment. Nonuse alone is not enough; abandonment requires nonuse plus acts showing intent to give up the right, such as building a permanent structure across the way.
- Prescription against the easement. If the servient owner openly blocks the easement for the statutory period without objection, it is extinguished.
- Destruction of the servient estate, such as a building that contained a stairway easement, ends easements that depended on it.

### § 7.5 Licenses

A license is personal permission to use another's land for a particular purpose. It is not an interest in land: it may be oral, is revocable at will, cannot be assigned, and ends on the death of either party or a sale of the property. An event ticket, permission to hunt this season, and permission to park in a neighbor's driveway are licenses. The exam distinction: an easement is a durable interest that (if appurtenant) runs with the land; a license is a revocable personal privilege. Permission also defeats a prescriptive claim because the use is no longer adverse.

### § 7.6 Encroachments

An encroachment is a physical intrusion of a building, fence, driveway, eave, or other improvement onto a neighboring parcel or into a setback or easement. It is found by a survey or inspection, not a title search, because it rarely appears in recorded documents. An encroachment can make title unmarketable, hinder financing, and lead to a demand for removal or damages; if it continues openly for the statutory period it may ripen into a prescriptive easement or, with exclusive possession, into title by adverse possession. Sellers must generally disclose known encroachments; buyers should obtain a current survey.

Example. A buyer's new survey shows the neighbor's garage 18 inches across the line. The encroachment is a defect the buyer can require the seller to resolve, for instance by an agreement or easement with the neighbor, before closing.

### § 7.7 Profit

A profit (profit à prendre) is a right to enter another's land and remove something from it, such as timber, gravel, or minerals; it is created and ended like an easement.

## § 8 Liens (summary)

### § 8.1 Definition and classifications

A lien is a charge against property securing payment of a debt or obligation; if unpaid, the lienholder can force a sale and be paid from the proceeds. Liens are classified two ways. General versus specific: a general lien attaches to all of a debtor's property, real and personal (judgment liens, income tax liens, estate and inheritance tax liens); a specific lien attaches to one parcel (mortgage, deed of trust, property tax and special assessment liens, mechanic's liens, association assessment liens). Voluntary versus involuntary: a voluntary lien is created by the owner's agreement (mortgage, deed of trust); an involuntary lien arises without consent by statute (property taxes, mechanic's liens, tax liens) or by court action (judgments, attachments).

### § 8.2 Common liens in brief

Mortgage and deed of trust: specific, voluntary. Property tax and special assessment liens: specific, involuntary, statutory, arising each year without filing. Mechanic's (construction) lien: specific, involuntary, statutory, for contractors, subcontractors, laborers, and suppliers who improve real property and are unpaid; deadlines and priority rules vary by state. Judgment lien: general, involuntary, arising when a money judgment is recorded or docketed in a county. Federal tax lien: general, involuntary, arising on assessment and filed notice. Lis pendens is not a lien but a recorded notice that litigation affecting title is pending.

### § 8.3 Priority of liens

Priority is the order in which lienholders are paid from a foreclosure sale. The general rule is first in time, first in right: liens rank by recording date (or, for some statutory liens, the date they arose), and a junior lien is paid only after every senior lien is paid in full. Property tax and special assessment liens are the major exception: by statute they generally rank ahead of all other liens regardless of date, including an earlier-recorded mortgage. Mechanic's lien priority is set by state statute and may relate back to when work began. Parties can rearrange priority by a recorded subordination agreement. A fuller treatment of liens as they affect title and closing appears in REP Ref. Ownership, Title & Transfer.

Example. A property carries a 2019 first mortgage, a 2022 home-equity line, a 2024 mechanic's lien, and unpaid property taxes. Foreclosure proceeds go first to the taxes, then the 2019 mortgage, then the 2022 line, then the mechanic's lien (unless state law gives it an earlier effective date), and any surplus to the owner.
