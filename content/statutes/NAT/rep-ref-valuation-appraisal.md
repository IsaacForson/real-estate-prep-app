# REP Ref. Valuation & Appraisal

Authored reference note for the national portion of real estate licensing examinations. Coverage: Pearson VUE domain III (III.A–III.C) and PSI domain III (III.A–III.C): value concepts and principles, the appraisal process and federal oversight, the three approaches, multipliers, assessed value, and CMA, BPO, and AVM. Status: draft, needs licensed review. Where a rule varies by state, the state portion of the examination governs.

## § 1 Value, price, and cost

### § 1.1 Three different quantities

Value, price, and cost are separate numbers expressed in the same unit. Value is the present worth of the future benefits ownership is expected to deliver; it is an opinion, always stated as of a specific date. Price is the amount actually paid in a completed transaction; it is a historical fact. Cost is the amount spent to create or acquire the improvements (materials, labor, overhead, profit); it describes production rather than exchange.

### § 1.2 Why the three diverge

Price departs from value when a party is under pressure, poorly informed, related to the other party, or receives unusual financing or concessions; a discounted sale from parent to child is not evidence of market value. Cost departs from value because the market does not reimburse spending it does not want: a $60,000 indoor pool in a neighborhood of modest homes may add far less than $60,000 to value (see contribution, § 4.4). An appraiser estimates value, a closing statement records price, and a builder's invoice records cost.

### § 1.3 Worked example

A homeowner paid $410,000 for a house two years ago (price), then spent $45,000 on a kitchen remodel (cost). An appraiser today estimates market value at $448,000 (value). The remodel contributed 448,000 − 410,000 = $38,000 to value, not the $45,000 it cost.

## § 2 Characteristics of value (DUST)

### § 2.1 The four essential characteristics

For anything to have economic value it must exhibit four characteristics, remembered as DUST: Demand, Utility, Scarcity, and Transferability. If any one is absent, the item has no market value regardless of how desirable it is.

### § 2.2 Demand

Demand is desire coupled with the ability to pay. Desire alone is not demand; people who want a lakefront estate but cannot finance one create no effective demand. Population growth, job growth, and credit availability increase effective demand.

### § 2.3 Utility

Utility is the ability to satisfy a need or want: shelter, income, prestige. A landlocked parcel without legal access, or land zoned so that no practical use is allowed, has little utility and therefore little value.

### § 2.4 Scarcity

Scarcity means supply is limited relative to demand. Air is useful and in demand but not scarce, so it has no market value; oceanfront lots in a built-out resort town are scarce, which supports high values.

### § 2.5 Transferability

Transferability means the owner can convey the bundle of rights to another. Property with an unmarketable title, or held under a restriction preventing any sale, has impaired value because the rights cannot be moved to a willing buyer.

### § 2.6 Worked example

A developer's parcel is near a growing employment center (demand), zoned for multifamily housing (utility), one of three undeveloped tracts in the corridor (scarcity), and held in fee simple with clear title (transferability). If a title search revealed an unresolvable ownership dispute, transferability would be impaired and value would fall even though nothing else changed.

## § 3 Types of value

### § 3.1 Market value

Market value is the type most appraisals seek. The federal banking agencies' appraisal regulations define it, in substance, as the most probable price a property should bring in a competitive and open market under all conditions requisite to a fair sale, the buyer and seller each acting prudently and knowledgeably, and assuming the price is not affected by undue stimulus. Five conditions are implicit: (1) buyer and seller are typically motivated; (2) both are well informed or well advised and acting in their own best interests; (3) a reasonable time is allowed for exposure in the open market; (4) payment is in cash or its equivalent; and (5) the price represents normal consideration unaffected by special or creative financing or sales concessions granted by anyone associated with the sale. "Most probable price," not "highest price," is the phrase candidates confuse.

### § 3.2 Other types of value

Investment value is the worth to a particular investor given that investor's requirements and tax position; it may be above or below market value. Value in use is the worth for a specific use to a specific user. Insurable value is the cost to replace the destructible improvements, excluding land. Assessed value is set by a taxing authority (§ 3.3). Liquidation value assumes a forced sale with less than normal exposure time. Going-concern value includes an operating business with its real estate.

### § 3.3 Assessed value and mill rates

Assessed value is determined by a local assessor and is often a percentage of market value called the assessment ratio (100 percent in some jurisdictions, far less in others; varies by state). Some states apply an equalization factor to bring assessments in different districts to a uniform level. Tax equals assessed value, less exemptions such as a homestead exemption, multiplied by the tax rate. Rates are commonly expressed in mills: one mill is one-thousandth of a dollar ($0.001), or $1 of tax per $1,000 of assessed value, so 25 mills equals $25 per $1,000, or 2.5 percent. Some jurisdictions state rates in dollars per $100 of value. Assessed value is not evidence of market value; a property can be under- or over-assessed for years.

### § 3.4 Worked example (property tax)

A home has a market value of $320,000 and the county assesses at 40 percent: assessed value = 320,000 × 0.40 = $128,000. A $25,000 homestead exemption leaves taxable value of 128,000 − 25,000 = $103,000. The combined rate is 32 mills (0.032). Annual tax = 103,000 × 0.032 = $3,296. Stated as $3.20 per $100, the calculation is (103,000 ÷ 100) × 3.20 = 1,030 × 3.20 = $3,296, the same result.

## § 4 Economic principles of value

### § 4.1 Highest and best use

Highest and best use is the reasonably probable use of vacant land or an improved property that is legally permissible, physically possible, financially feasible, and maximally productive. The four tests are applied in that order: (1) legally permissible under zoning, private restrictions, and environmental law; (2) physically possible given size, shape, soil, topography, and access; (3) financially feasible, producing a positive return; (4) maximally productive, yielding the highest land value among feasible uses. A use that fails an earlier test is not carried forward. Appraisers analyze highest and best use both "as though vacant" and "as improved"; an existing building whose contribution exceeds the cost of demolition and redevelopment remains the highest and best use as improved. Highest and best use changes over time, which is why a farm at a growing city's edge may be valued for subdivision potential rather than crop yield.

### § 4.2 Substitution

A prudent buyer will pay no more for a property than the cost of acquiring an equally desirable substitute. Substitution underlies the sales comparison approach (comparable sales set the ceiling), the cost approach (a buyer will not pay more than the cost to build an equivalent), and the income approach (an investor will not pay more than the price of another investment yielding the same return).

### § 4.3 Conformity

Value is maximized when a property is reasonably similar in size, style, age, and quality to surrounding properties. Zoning and subdivision covenants enforce conformity, and the principle explains why an oversized or unusually styled home may not achieve value proportional to its cost.

### § 4.4 Contribution

The value of any component is measured by how much it adds to (or subtracts from) the value of the whole, not by what it cost. A $30,000 pool that adds $12,000 to sale price contributes $12,000. Contribution is what a sales comparison adjustment measures. The related principle of increasing and decreasing returns holds that added investment raises value only up to a point, after which each added dollar returns less than a dollar.

### § 4.5 Anticipation

Value is created by the expectation of future benefits: income, appreciation, or amenities. Anticipation is the foundation of the income approach and explains why news of a planned transit station raises nearby values before construction begins.

### § 4.6 Competition

Excess profits attract competitors. When a property type earns unusually high returns, new supply is built until returns fall to a normal level; a lone successful drive-through at an intersection is soon joined by others, and each earns less.

### § 4.7 Change

No physical, economic, social, or governmental condition remains constant, so value is always estimated as of a stated effective date. Neighborhoods are commonly described as moving through growth, stability, decline, and revitalization.

### § 4.8 Supply and demand

Prices rise when demand outpaces supply and fall when supply outpaces demand. Real estate supply responds slowly because construction takes time and land is fixed in location, so short-term price movements can be sharp. A plant closing reduces demand; a new interchange can increase demand and, eventually, supply.

### § 4.9 Regression and progression

Regression: the value of a superior property is pulled down by inferior properties around it. Progression: the value of an inferior property is pulled up by superior neighbors. The largest house on the block suffers regression; the smallest house on a block of large houses enjoys progression. Both follow from conformity.

### § 4.10 Plottage and assemblage

Assemblage is the act of combining adjacent parcels under one ownership. Plottage is the increase in total value when the combined parcel is worth more than the sum of its parts, usually because the larger tract supports a more productive use. Assemblage is the process; plottage is the value increment. Two adjoining lots worth $100,000 each are assembled into one commercial site worth $260,000; plottage value is 260,000 − (100,000 + 100,000) = $60,000.

## § 5 The appraisal process

### § 5.1 What an appraisal is

An appraisal is an opinion of value developed by a qualified, impartial appraiser, supported by data and analysis, stated as of a specific effective date, and communicated in a report. It is not a determination or guarantee of price. The appraiser's fee may not depend on the value concluded. The effective date may be the inspection date, a past date (retrospective appraisal, common in estate and litigation work), or a future date (prospective appraisal, common for proposed construction); the report date is when the report is signed and may differ.

### § 5.2 Steps in the process

(1) Identify the problem: the client and other intended users, the intended use, the type and definition of value, the effective date, the relevant property characteristics, and the property rights appraised (fee simple, leased fee, leasehold, or a partial interest). (2) Determine the scope of work needed for credible results. (3) Collect and analyze data: general data on region, city, and neighborhood; specific data on the site and improvements; comparable data on sales, rents, costs, and capitalization rates. (4) Analyze highest and best use, as vacant and as improved. (5) Estimate land (site) value, usually by sales comparison. (6) Apply the applicable approaches: sales comparison, cost, income. (7) Reconcile the indications into a final opinion of value. (8) Report the opinion in the form the assignment requires. Steps 1 and 2 together are often called defining the assignment.

### § 5.3 Property rights appraised

The same physical property has different values depending on the rights appraised. Fee simple value assumes no leases. Leased fee value is the lessor's interest, reflecting contract rents and the reversion at lease end; if contract rent is below market, leased fee value is below fee simple value. Leasehold value is the tenant's interest and is positive when contract rent is below market rent.

### § 5.4 Worked example

A lender orders an appraisal of a rented house for a refinance. Step 1: client is the lender, intended use is lending, value type is market value, the interest is the leased fee (a one-year lease is in place), and the effective date is the inspection date. Step 4: continued residential use is the highest and best use as improved. Step 5: the lot is valued at $90,000 from three lot sales. Step 6: sales comparison and cost indications are developed; income gets little weight because houses are not bought for income. Step 7: reconciled to $355,000. Step 8: reported on the lender's residential form.

## § 6 Federal oversight of appraisal

### § 6.1 The FIRREA framework

Faulty appraisals contributed to the savings-and-loan failures of the 1980s, and Congress responded in Title XI of the Financial Institutions Reform, Recovery, and Enforcement Act of 1989 (FIRREA), 12 U.S.C. §§ 3331–3356. Title XI created a two-layer system. Federally, it established the Appraisal Subcommittee (ASC) of the Federal Financial Institutions Examination Council (FFIEC) to oversee state programs and maintain a National Registry of appraisers eligible for federally related transactions, and it recognized a private nonprofit, The Appraisal Foundation, whose Appraiser Qualifications Board (AQB) sets minimum education, experience, and examination criteria and whose Appraisal Standards Board (ASB) writes USPAP. Each state licenses, certifies, and disciplines appraisers under its own law, which must meet or exceed the AQB minimums. FIRREA did not create a federal appraiser license; it required the states to create them.

### § 6.2 Appraiser credential levels

The AQB criteria recognize four principal classifications, adopted by the states with local variation: trainee appraiser (works under a supervisor); licensed residential appraiser (non-complex one-to-four-unit residential properties with a transaction value below $1,000,000, and complex ones below $400,000); certified residential appraiser (one-to-four-unit residential properties of any value or complexity); and certified general appraiser (all real property, including commercial, industrial, and agricultural). Titles and the exact scope of the licensed level vary by state; the state portion governs.

### § 6.3 Federally related transactions and when a licensed appraiser is required

A federally related transaction (FRT) is a real-estate-related financial transaction that a federal financial institution regulatory agency engages in, contracts for, or regulates and that requires an appraiser under the agencies' rules. For an FRT the lender must obtain an appraisal from a state-licensed or state-certified appraiser. The agencies' regulations exempt transactions at or below value thresholds: residential real estate transactions of $400,000 or less (raised from $250,000 in 2019) and commercial real estate transactions of $500,000 or less (raised from $250,000 in 2018). Below the threshold the institution must still obtain a written "evaluation," which need not be done by a licensed appraiser. A state-certified (not merely licensed) appraiser is required for FRTs of $1,000,000 or more, for nonresidential FRTs above the commercial threshold, and for complex residential FRTs above the residential threshold. FHA, VA, Fannie Mae, and Freddie Mac loans follow those agencies' own appraisal requirements.

### § 6.4 Appraisal independence

The Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 added appraisal independence requirements to the Truth in Lending Act (15 U.S.C. § 1639e). In general terms, no one with an interest in a consumer credit transaction secured by a principal dwelling may coerce, bribe, intimidate, or otherwise attempt to influence an appraiser's opinion of value, mischaracterize the value, or withhold a fee because of the value reported. Lenders must pay fee appraisers customary and reasonable compensation and must report appraisers who materially violate applicable standards to the state licensing agency. Parties may still ask an appraiser to consider additional information, to explain the conclusion, or to correct factual errors. For licensees the rule is: a licensee may give the appraiser comparable sales and point out property features, but may not name the number needed, threaten to steer future business, or condition payment on hitting the contract price. Separately, the ECOA valuation rule (Regulation B) requires a creditor to give an applicant a copy of any appraisal or written valuation developed for a first-lien loan secured by a dwelling promptly upon completion or three business days before consummation, whichever is earlier, and to notify the applicant of that right within three business days of application.

### § 6.5 USPAP in general terms

USPAP is the set of generally recognized ethical and performance standards for appraisers, written by the ASB, updated periodically, adopted by every state, and required by federal agencies in FRTs. It addresses ethics (impartiality, confidentiality, no undisclosed fees), competency (have or acquire the knowledge the assignment needs, or decline it), scope of work, record keeping, and the development and reporting of appraisals. USPAP is copyrighted and is not reproduced here; items should test the concepts, not its wording.

### § 6.6 Worked example

A buyer contracts to purchase a home for $436,000 with a conventional loan from a federally regulated bank. Because the value exceeds $400,000, the residential exemption does not apply and the bank must obtain an appraisal from a state-licensed or state-certified appraiser. The listing agent emails the appraiser three recent sales and a list of the seller's upgrades; that is permitted. The loan officer emails, "We need at least $436,000 or this deal dies"; that is a prohibited attempt to influence value. If the appraisal comes in at $425,000, the bank must give the borrower a copy under Regulation B, and the parties may renegotiate, increase the down payment, or invoke an appraisal contingency if the contract has one.

## § 7 The sales comparison approach

### § 7.1 Concept

The sales comparison (market data) approach estimates value by comparing the subject to similar properties that recently sold, adjusting the comparables' prices for differences, and reconciling the adjusted prices into a value indication. It rests on substitution and is the most reliable approach for houses, condominiums, and vacant land when adequate sales exist.

### § 7.2 Selecting comparables

Comparables should be arm's-length sales (unrelated parties, typical motivation, normal marketing), recent, in the same or a competing neighborhood, and physically similar. Lenders' residential forms call for at least three closed sales; listings and pending sales are support only. Foreclosure and short sales are distressed and require careful analysis or exclusion.

### § 7.3 Adjustment logic

Adjustments are always made to the comparable, never to the subject, because the question is what each comparable would have sold for had it been identical to the subject. If a comparable has a feature the subject lacks (comparable superior), its price is adjusted downward; if it lacks a feature the subject has (comparable inferior), its price is adjusted upward. Memory aid: Comparable Better, Subtract; Comparable Inferior, Add. Elements of comparison are analyzed in a customary order: property rights conveyed; financing terms (seller-paid points or below-market seller financing inflate price); conditions of sale (motivation, related parties); market conditions (the "time" adjustment); location; and physical characteristics (size, age, condition, quality, amenities). The transactional adjustments come first, often as percentages, then the property adjustments. Adjustment amounts come from the market, most commonly by paired-sales analysis: two sales that differ in only one feature reveal the market's price for that feature. Contribution, not cost, is the measure (§ 4.4).

### § 7.4 Worked example

Subject: 1,800 square feet, three bedrooms, two baths, two-car garage, no pool; market flat. Market-derived adjustments: living area $60 per square foot; half bath $3,000; garage bay $8,000; pool $15,000.

Comparable A sold for $352,000: 1,900 square feet, otherwise identical. It is larger by 100 square feet (superior): subtract 100 × 60 = $6,000. Adjusted price 352,000 − 6,000 = $346,000.

Comparable B sold for $338,000: 1,800 square feet, one and one-half baths, one-car garage, no pool. The comparable is inferior on baths and garage: add 3,000 + 8,000 = $11,000. Adjusted price 338,000 + 11,000 = $349,000.

Comparable C sold for $371,000: identical to the subject except it has a pool (superior): subtract $15,000. Adjusted price 371,000 − 15,000 = $356,000.

The adjusted range is $346,000 to $356,000. Comparable A required the smallest adjustment, so the appraiser weights it most heavily and concludes $348,000. Reconciliation is judgment, not averaging (§ 10.1); the arithmetic average of $350,333 does not bind the appraiser.

### § 7.5 Strengths and weaknesses

The approach is direct, market-based, and easily understood, but it depends on an active market of similar sales. It is weak for special-purpose properties, new or unique construction, and thin rural markets, where the cost approach often takes over.

## § 8 The cost approach

### § 8.1 Formula

Cost approach value = cost new of the improvements − accrued depreciation + land (site) value. Land is valued separately, usually by sales comparison, and is never depreciated. The approach rests on substitution: a buyer will not pay more for an existing property than it would cost to buy a similar site and build an equivalent structure without undue delay.

### § 8.2 Reproduction cost versus replacement cost

Reproduction cost is the cost to build an exact duplicate with the same materials, design, and workmanship, including outdated features. Replacement cost is the cost to build a structure of equivalent utility using current materials, design, and standards. Replacement cost is usually lower and automatically eliminates most functional obsolescence, so it is the more common basis; reproduction cost is used for historic properties and when functional obsolescence must be measured explicitly. "Exact duplicate" means reproduction; "equivalent utility" means replacement.

### § 8.3 Methods of estimating cost new

From least to most detailed: the index method adjusts known original cost by a construction cost index; the square-foot (comparative-unit) method multiplies building area by a per-square-foot cost for similar structures and is the usual residential method; the unit-in-place method prices each installed component; and the quantity survey method itemizes every unit of material and labor as a contractor would bid, and is the most accurate and most costly.

### § 8.4 Accrued depreciation: three causes

Accrued depreciation is the total loss in value from all causes as of the effective date, measured against cost new; it is an appraisal concept distinct from the income-tax depreciation deduction. Physical deterioration is wear, decay, and damage: a worn roof, a cracked foundation. Functional obsolescence is a loss from a flaw in design or utility within the property: one bathroom in a four-bedroom house, a floor plan requiring passage through a bedroom to reach the bath, inadequate wiring, or a superadequacy (overimprovement) such as a commercial kitchen in a modest home. External (economic or locational) obsolescence is a loss caused by something outside the property boundaries: a new freeway next door, an oversupplied market, a neighboring landfill. Physical and functional items are curable when the cost to cure is equal to or less than the value added, and incurable otherwise. External obsolescence is always incurable from the owner's standpoint because the owner cannot fix a cause located off the property.

### § 8.5 The straight-line (economic age-life) method

The simplest estimate of total accrued depreciation assumes improvements lose value evenly over their total economic life. Actual (chronological) age is years since construction. Effective age is the age the building appears to be given its condition and utility; it is less than actual age for a well-maintained or remodeled building and greater for a neglected one. Total economic life is the period over which the improvements contribute to value; remaining economic life = total economic life − effective age. Accrued depreciation = (effective age ÷ total economic life) × cost new; equivalently, annual depreciation = cost new ÷ total economic life. The method is convenient but does not separate the three causes or curable from incurable items.

### § 8.6 Worked example

A house has replacement cost new of $280,000. It is 20 years old but well maintained and updated, so the appraiser assigns an effective age of 15 years and a total economic life of 60 years. Land value is $95,000.

Step 1: annual depreciation rate = 1 ÷ 60 = 0.016667, about 1.67 percent per year.
Step 2: accrued depreciation percentage = 15 ÷ 60 = 0.25, or 25 percent.
Step 3: accrued depreciation = 280,000 × 0.25 = $70,000. (Check: 280,000 ÷ 60 = $4,666.67 per year × 15 years = $70,000.)
Step 4: depreciated cost of improvements = 280,000 − 70,000 = $210,000.
Step 5: value indication = 210,000 + 95,000 = $305,000. Remaining economic life = 60 − 15 = 45 years.

Using the actual age of 20 years would give 20 ÷ 60 = 33.33 percent, or $93,333, and an indication of $281,667; effective age, not actual age, is the correct input.

### § 8.7 When the cost approach is most useful

New or nearly new construction (little depreciation to estimate); special-purpose properties with few sales (schools, churches, government buildings); and insurance, where insurable value is the replacement cost of the destructible improvements excluding land. It is least reliable for older buildings, where estimating accrued depreciation becomes subjective.

## § 9 The income approach

### § 9.1 Concept

The income (income capitalization) approach converts expected future income into a present value estimate; it rests on anticipation (§ 4.5). Direct capitalization applies one year's net operating income and a capitalization rate. Yield capitalization (discounted cash flow) projects several years of cash flows plus a reversion and discounts them; it is used for complex investment properties and is not detailed here.

### § 9.2 Reconstructing the operating statement

The appraiser builds a stabilized annual operating statement. Potential gross income (PGI) is total rent at full occupancy at market rents. Subtract vacancy and collection loss (a percentage of PGI) and add other income (laundry, parking, fees) to reach effective gross income (EGI). Subtract operating expenses to reach net operating income (NOI). Operating expenses include fixed expenses (property taxes, hazard insurance), variable expenses (utilities, management, maintenance, repairs, payroll), and reserves for replacement (an annual set-aside for roofs, HVAC, appliances). Four items are not operating expenses and are excluded before computing NOI: mortgage debt service, income-tax depreciation, the owner's income taxes, and capital improvements. NOI is therefore income before financing and taxes, which makes it comparable across properties with different owners and loans.

### § 9.3 The IRV relationship

Income, rate, and value are linked by I = R × V, so V = I ÷ R (the appraiser's formula) and R = I ÷ V (how a rate is extracted from a sale). I is annual NOI, R is the overall capitalization rate as a decimal, and V is value. Because value equals income divided by rate, value moves inversely with the rate: a higher cap rate means a lower value for the same income. Investors demand higher rates, and therefore pay less, for riskier income, older buildings, weaker tenants, or less liquid markets.

### § 9.4 Deriving the capitalization rate

The most defensible rate comes from comparable sales: divide each comparable's NOI at sale by its sale price. A building that sold for $1,500,000 with NOI of $120,000 indicates R = 120,000 ÷ 1,500,000 = 0.08, or 8 percent. Several indications are reconciled to a rate for the subject. Rates can also be built from mortgage and equity components (band of investment) or taken from surveys, but market extraction is primary.

### § 9.5 Worked example (direct capitalization)

A 12-unit apartment building rents each unit for $1,250 per month. Comparables show 5 percent vacancy and collection loss and a 7.5 percent cap rate.

PGI = 12 × 1,250 × 12 = $180,000.
Vacancy and collection loss = 180,000 × 0.05 = $9,000.
Other income (coin laundry) = $2,400.
EGI = 180,000 − 9,000 + 2,400 = $173,400.
Operating expenses: taxes $21,000; insurance $6,500; management (6 percent of EGI) $10,404; utilities and maintenance $28,000; reserves $6,000; total $71,904. (The owner's $84,000 annual mortgage payment is excluded.)
NOI = 173,400 − 71,904 = $101,496.
Value = NOI ÷ R = 101,496 ÷ 0.075 = $1,353,280.

At an 8.5 percent rate the value would be 101,496 ÷ 0.085 = $1,194,071, illustrating the inverse relationship. An investor who paid $1,200,000 for NOI of $96,000 bought at 96,000 ÷ 1,200,000 = 0.08, or 8 percent.

### § 9.6 Gross rent multiplier and gross income multiplier

A multiplier relates price to gross income without deducting expenses. The gross rent multiplier (GRM), conventionally used for one-to-four-unit residential rentals, is sale price ÷ gross monthly rent. The gross income multiplier (GIM), used for larger commercial or multifamily properties, is sale price ÷ gross annual income. (Conventions vary; an item must state which.) Once a multiplier is extracted from comparable sales, value = subject's gross rent × multiplier. Multipliers are quick but crude because they ignore vacancy, expenses, and financing.

### § 9.7 Worked example (GRM and GIM)

Three duplexes sold: (a) $312,000 with monthly rent $2,400, GRM = 312,000 ÷ 2,400 = 130; (b) $297,000 with rent $2,250, GRM = 297,000 ÷ 2,250 = 132; (c) $335,000 with rent $2,600, GRM = 335,000 ÷ 2,600 = 128.8. The appraiser concludes a GRM of 130. The subject rents for $2,500 per month, so indicated value = 2,500 × 130 = $325,000.

A comparable retail strip sold for $2,100,000 with annual gross income of $210,000: GIM = 2,100,000 ÷ 210,000 = 10.0. The subject's annual gross income is $186,000, so indicated value = 186,000 × 10.0 = $1,860,000.

### § 9.8 Distinguishing the GRM from the cap rate

The GRM uses gross rent and is a multiplier (value = rent × GRM; a larger GRM means a higher value). The cap rate uses net operating income and is a rate (value = NOI ÷ R; a larger rate means a lower value). The GRM is usually applied to monthly rent for small residential properties; the cap rate is always applied to annual NOI.

## § 10 Reconciliation and reporting

### § 10.1 Reconciliation

Reconciliation (correlation) is the final step in which the appraiser weighs the indications from the applicable approaches and arrives at a single opinion of value. It is not averaging. The appraiser considers the quantity and quality of data behind each approach, how well each fits the property type and purpose, and consistency among the indications, then places the most weight on the most reliable approach. The same reasoning applies within an approach to the individual comparables (§ 7.4).

### § 10.2 Which approach dominates

Houses and condominiums: sales comparison, with cost as support. Vacant land: sales comparison (nothing to depreciate, usually no income). New construction and special-purpose properties: cost. Apartment, office, retail, and industrial investment properties: income, with sales comparison used to extract cap rates and confirm the result. Insurance: cost (improvements only).

### § 10.3 Reporting

A written appraisal is communicated as either an appraisal report or a restricted appraisal report; the latter is allowed only when the client is the sole intended user. Residential lenders typically require a standardized form such as the Uniform Residential Appraisal Report used by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. A report states the effective date, value type, property rights appraised, approaches used and omitted (with reasons), the reconciliation, the appraiser's certification, and assumptions and limiting conditions.

## § 11 CMA, BPO, AVM, and appraisal compared

### § 11.1 Appraisal

An appraisal is developed by a state-licensed or state-certified appraiser in compliance with USPAP, is independent of the transaction, and is required for federally related transactions above the exemption thresholds (§ 6.3). In most states only an appraiser may call a valuation an "appraisal."

### § 11.2 Competitive (comparative) market analysis

A CMA is prepared by a real estate licensee, usually without charge, to help a seller choose a listing price or a buyer decide what to offer. It uses the logic of the sales comparison approach but also studies active (competing) listings, pending sales, and expired listings, which an appraiser gives little weight: sold properties show what buyers paid, active listings show the competition, and expired listings show prices the market rejected. A CMA usually covers sales in the past three to six months in the same neighborhood and adjusts with the same "adjust the comparable" rules (§ 7.3). A CMA is not an appraisal and must not be represented as one; many states require a written disclaimer, and whether a licensee may charge for a CMA varies by state. The seller decides the list price; the licensee owes an honest, supportable opinion. Deliberately overstating value to win a listing is a misrepresentation and a disciplinary matter.

### § 11.3 Broker price opinion

A BPO is a written opinion of probable selling price prepared by a broker or salesperson, typically for a lender or servicer evaluating a loan modification, short sale, or foreclosure disposition, as a drive-by (exterior) or interior report listing recent sales and active listings. Federal law (12 U.S.C. § 3355, added by Dodd-Frank) provides that a BPO may not be used as the primary basis for determining value when originating a residential mortgage secured by the consumer's principal dwelling. Whether a licensee may prepare a BPO for a fee, and with what disclosures, varies by state; some states reserve paid BPOs to licensed appraisers. The state portion governs.

### § 11.4 Automated valuation model

An AVM is a computer program that estimates value by applying statistical models (regression, repeat-sales indexes, machine learning) to public records, MLS data, and prior sales, with no physical inspection. Lenders use AVMs for pre-qualification, lending below the appraisal threshold, quality control, and portfolio monitoring; consumers see them as online estimates. AVMs are fast and cheap but cannot see condition or interior updates, and their reliability depends on data density. Federal regulators have adopted quality-control standards for AVMs used in mortgage lending. An AVM is not an appraisal.

### § 11.5 Comparison table

| Feature | Appraisal | CMA | BPO | AVM |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Who prepares | Licensed or certified appraiser | Real estate licensee | Real estate licensee (where permitted) | Computer model |
| Typical purpose | Lending, litigation, estate, tax | Listing or offer pricing | Lender or servicer disposition and servicing | Screening, QC, low-value lending, consumer estimates |
| Standard | USPAP and state law | State license law; not an appraisal | State law; federal limit on use for origination | Regulatory model-quality standards |
| Inspection | Usually interior and exterior | Exterior or walk-through | Exterior or interior per order | None |
| Data used | Closed sales, cost, income | Sold, active, pending, expired listings | Sold and active listings | Public records, MLS, indexes |
| Fee | Yes | Usually none | Modest fee where allowed | Low or none |

### § 11.6 Worked example

A licensee's CMA finds three sales in the past four months at adjusted prices of $412,000, $418,000, and $425,000, two active competing listings at $429,900 and $434,500, and an expired listing that sat 140 days at $449,000. The licensee recommends a list range of $419,000 to $425,000, notes that the expired listing shows resistance above about $440,000, and labels the document a CMA that is not an appraisal. The seller lists at $424,900. The buyer's lender later obtains an appraisal at $422,000 based on closed sales only. Both applied the same market logic, but only the appraisal satisfies the lender's regulatory requirement.

## § 12 Quick reference of formulas

### § 12.1 Formulas and directions

- Tax = (assessed value − exemptions) × mill rate ÷ 1,000; one mill = $0.001 = $1 per $1,000.
- Plottage value = value of assembled parcel − sum of values of separate parcels.
- Cost approach value = cost new − accrued depreciation + land value.
- Accrued depreciation (age-life) = (effective age ÷ total economic life) × cost new; annual depreciation = cost new ÷ total economic life; remaining economic life = total economic life − effective age.
- EGI = PGI − vacancy and collection loss + other income; NOI = EGI − operating expenses (excluding debt service, depreciation, income taxes, capital improvements).
- V = I ÷ R; I = R × V; R = I ÷ V.
- GRM = sale price ÷ gross monthly rent; value = monthly rent × GRM. GIM = sale price ÷ gross annual income; value = annual income × GIM.
- Adjustment direction: comparable superior, subtract; comparable inferior, add; never adjust the subject.
