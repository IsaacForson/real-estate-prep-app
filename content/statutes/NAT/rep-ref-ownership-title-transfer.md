# REP Ref. Ownership, Title & Transfer

Authored reference note. Status: draft — needs licensed review. Original expository text written for the REP item pipeline; items cite it by section (for example `REP Ref. Ownership, Title & Transfer § 6.3`). Where a rule differs among states, this note says so; the state portion of the exam governs.

## § 1 Freehold estates

### § 1.1 Estates and the freehold/leasehold divide

An estate is the degree, quantity, and duration of a person's interest in real property. A freehold estate is ownership of indefinite or lifetime duration; a leasehold (nonfreehold) estate (§ 2) is a right of possession for a limited time under a lease, with ownership remaining in the landlord. The freehold estates are the fee simple estates (absolute and defeasible) and the life estate.

### § 1.2 Fee simple absolute

Fee simple absolute is the largest estate the law recognizes: ownership of unlimited duration with the full bundle of rights, transferable during life, passing by will or descent at death, and subject only to government powers and existing encumbrances. Unless a deed states otherwise, a conveyance is presumed to pass fee simple absolute. "Fee simple," "fee," and "fee simple absolute" are used interchangeably in practice.

### § 1.3 Defeasible fees

A defeasible fee is a fee simple that can be lost if a stated condition is broken or a stated event occurs. Two forms are tested:

- Fee simple determinable. The estate lasts only "so long as," "while," or "during" a stated use continues, and it ends automatically when the use stops. The grantor keeps a possibility of reverter, and title returns to the grantor or the grantor's heirs without any action.
- Fee simple subject to a condition subsequent. The grant says "but if," "provided that," or "on condition that" a thing does or does not happen. When the condition is broken the estate does not end automatically; the grantor holds a right of re-entry (power of termination) and must act to retake the property, and a grantor who waits too long may lose the right.

The distinction to keep straight: durational words ("so long as") create a determinable fee that ends by itself; conditional words ("but if") create a right the grantor must exercise.

Example. A donor conveys land "to the school district so long as it is used for a public school." When the district leases the building to a store, title reverts to the donor's heirs automatically. Had the deed said "but if the land ceases to be used for a school the grantor may re-enter," the heirs would have to act to retake it.

### § 1.4 Life estates

A life estate is a freehold measured by a lifetime. In a conventional life estate the grantor conveys "to A for life"; A is the life tenant. If the measuring life is someone else ("to A for the life of B"), the estate is pur autre vie; if A dies before B, A's heirs hold it until B dies. The life tenant may possess, use, and collect income, may sell, lease, or mortgage the interest (a buyer or lender gets only what the life tenant has, which ends at the measuring death), must pay taxes and ordinary maintenance, and may not commit waste, meaning damage or neglect that injures the property for whoever takes next.

The interest that follows is a future interest. If the property returns to the grantor (or the grantor's heirs), the grantor holds a reversion. If the deed names a third party ("to A for life, then to C"), C holds a remainder and is the remainderman. Both are present, transferable interests even though possession is postponed.

Legal life estates are created by statute rather than by the owner: dower and curtesy (a surviving spouse's life interest in the deceased spouse's real property) survive in a few states, and homestead laws in many states protect a family home from certain creditors and limit one spouse's ability to convey it alone. All vary by state.

Example. A widow deeds her farm "to my son for life, remainder to my granddaughter." The son may farm and lease it but not strip the timber; at his death the granddaughter takes fee simple. Had the deed said only "to my son for life," the farm would revert to the widow or her estate.

## § 2 Leasehold estates

### § 2.1 Nature of a leasehold

A lease is both a conveyance and a contract: it transfers exclusive possession to the tenant (lessee) for a period and sets the parties' promises about rent, maintenance, and use. The landlord (lessor) keeps a reversion. Leases for more than one year generally must be in writing under the Statute of Frauds; the threshold varies by state. A recorded long-term lease is an encumbrance on title.

### § 2.2 The four leasehold estates

- Estate (tenancy) for years. A definite period with a fixed beginning and end, whether ten days or ninety-nine years. It ends automatically on the end date without notice, and death of either party does not end it.
- Periodic estate. Runs period to period (month to month, year to year) and renews automatically until one party gives proper notice, set by the lease or state statute. It commonly arises when a tenant for years stays on with the landlord's consent and keeps paying rent.
- Estate at will. Possession with permission for no fixed period, terminable by either party; most states require some notice. It ends at the death of either party.
- Estate (tenancy) at sufferance. A holdover tenant who stays after the lease ends without consent has possession but no right to it; the landlord may evict or, by accepting rent, create a periodic tenancy.

### § 2.3 Applying the leasehold estates

Example. A tenant's two-year lease ends June 30. The tenant stays into July without the landlord's agreement and is a tenant at sufferance. On July 15 the landlord accepts a month's rent; the tenant is now a month-to-month periodic tenant who must receive statutory notice before being required to leave.

## § 3 Liens and other encumbrances on title

### § 3.1 Liens defined and classified

A lien is a charge against property securing payment of a debt; if unpaid, the lienholder may force a sale and be paid from the proceeds. A lien is an encumbrance, not an ownership interest: the debtor keeps title and possession. Liens are general (attaching to all of a debtor's property) or specific (one parcel), and voluntary (created by the owner's agreement) or involuntary (by statute or court action). A companion summary appears in REP Ref. Property & Land Use § 8.

### § 3.2 Specific liens

- Mortgage and deed of trust liens: voluntary; the borrower pledges the property as security. Whether the lender holds a lien only (lien theory) or bare legal title (title theory) varies by state.
- Real property tax and special assessment liens: involuntary, statutory, attaching each year; they generally rank ahead of all other liens (§ 3.4).
- Mechanic's (construction) lien: involuntary, statutory, for contractors, subcontractors, laborers, and suppliers who improved the property and were not paid; deadlines and priority rules vary by state.
- Association assessment lien: for unpaid condominium or homeowners' association charges; priority varies by state.
- Vendor's lien (unpaid seller) and vendee's lien (buyer's deposit after seller default): equitable liens recognized in many states.

### § 3.3 General liens

A judgment lien arises when a money judgment is recorded or docketed and attaches to the debtor's real property in that county; it is enforced by writ of execution and sheriff's sale. Federal and state tax liens (income, estate, inheritance) bind third parties once notice is filed. Lis pendens is not a lien but a recorded notice that a lawsuit affecting title is pending; a later purchaser or lender is bound by the result.

### § 3.4 Lien priority

At a foreclosure sale liens are paid in order of priority; a junior lien receives nothing until every senior lien is satisfied. The general rule is first in time, first in right, by recording date. Exceptions: property tax and special assessment liens rank first by statute regardless of date; mechanic's liens may relate back to the start of construction under state law; and a lender may agree in a recorded subordination agreement to rank behind a later lien.

### § 3.5 Clearing liens at closing

A seller must ordinarily deliver title free of liens other than those the buyer agreed to assume. The settlement agent obtains payoff statements, pays each lienholder from the proceeds, and records the satisfaction (release) of mortgage or, for a deed of trust, a deed of reconveyance; until the release is recorded the lien still appears in the record.

Example. A seller owes $180,000 on a mortgage and has a $6,000 recorded judgment lien. At a $300,000 closing the settlement agent pays both from the seller's proceeds and records both releases, so the buyer takes title free of them.

## § 4 Forms of ownership

### § 4.1 Ownership in severalty

Ownership in severalty is ownership by one person or one entity alone; the interest is "severed" from everyone else. A corporation or LLC holding title holds in severalty even though it has many owners, because the entity is a single legal person.

### § 4.2 Tenancy in common

Tenancy in common is co-ownership in which each owner holds an undivided fractional interest in the whole. Interests may be unequal; each owner has an equal right to possess the entire property (unity of possession) and cannot claim a specific part; each may sell, mortgage, or will the share without the others' consent; and there is no survivorship, so a deceased owner's share passes to heirs or devisees. A conveyance to two or more unmarried persons is presumed in most states to create a tenancy in common. Any co-owner may sue for partition, in which a court divides the land or orders it sold and the proceeds split.

### § 4.3 Joint tenancy

Joint tenancy is co-ownership with the right of survivorship: when one joint tenant dies, that share passes automatically to the surviving joint tenants, outside probate and outside the decedent's will. Creating it traditionally requires four unities: time (interests acquired at the same moment), title (by the same instrument), interest (equal shares), and possession (equal right to the whole). Many states also require express words such as "as joint tenants with right of survivorship" because the law presumes tenancy in common. A joint tenant may convey the share during life; the buyer becomes a tenant in common with the remaining owners, who stay joint tenants among themselves. A joint tenant cannot will the share, because at death there is nothing left to pass. Some states limit or abolish joint tenancy; varies by state.

Example. Three siblings hold a cabin as joint tenants and one sells her third to a friend. The friend is a tenant in common as to one-third; the two siblings remain joint tenants as to two-thirds. When one sibling dies, the survivor owns two-thirds and the friend one-third.

### § 4.4 Tenancy by the entirety

Tenancy by the entirety is joint ownership available only to spouses in the states that recognize it. Each spouse owns the whole; there is a right of survivorship; neither can convey or encumber the property without the other's signature; and in many of those states a creditor of one spouse alone cannot reach it. Divorce converts it to a tenancy in common. Availability and creditor rules vary by state.

### § 4.5 Community property

In community property states, property acquired by either spouse during marriage through labor or income is community property, owned one-half by each, while property owned before marriage or received by gift or inheritance is separate property. Both spouses must generally join in conveying community real property. At death a spouse may will his or her half; some states offer a survivorship option. Which states and the classification rules vary by state.

### § 4.6 Trusts

A trust is an arrangement in which a trustor (settlor) transfers title to a trustee, who holds and manages the property for beneficiaries under the trust instrument. A living (inter vivos) trust is created during the trustor's life; a testamentary trust is created by will and takes effect at death. Trust real estate is conveyed by the trustee, so a buyer should confirm the trustee's authority. In a land trust, available in some states, the trustee holds legal and equitable title while the beneficiary keeps control and the beneficial interest is treated as personal property; varies by state.

### § 4.7 Business entities holding title

Entities own real estate in severalty. A corporation is a separate legal person with perpetual existence; shareholders own stock, not the real estate, and are shielded from its debts. General partners share management and are personally liable; limited partners' liability is capped at their investment. A limited liability company (LLC) combines liability protection with flexible management. A joint venture is a partnership for a single project; a real estate investment trust (REIT) pools investors' funds to hold real estate or mortgages. Who may sign a deed for an entity depends on its governing documents.

## § 5 Common-interest ownership

### § 5.1 Condominiums

A condominium owner holds fee simple title to a unit (the airspace and interior surfaces defined in the declaration) plus an undivided interest, as a tenant in common with all other unit owners, in the common elements: land, structure, roof, hallways, amenities. Limited common elements, such as an assigned parking space or balcony, are reserved for one unit but owned in common. Each unit is separately taxed and mortgaged; one owner's default does not affect the others' titles. The association created by the recorded declaration maintains the common elements and collects assessments, which become a lien if unpaid. Condominium is a form of ownership, not a building type.

### § 5.2 Cooperatives

In a cooperative, a corporation owns the building and land. A resident buys shares of the corporation and receives a proprietary lease to occupy a specific unit. The resident owns stock (personal property in most states) and a leasehold, not real estate; some states treat cooperative interests as real property for certain purposes. The corporation typically carries one blanket mortgage and one tax bill, and residents pay monthly charges covering their share; because widespread default puts the whole building at risk, boards commonly screen purchasers and may reject a sale. 
### § 5.3 Timeshares

A timeshare divides the right to use a unit into periods, typically weeks, sold to different purchasers. In a fee (interval-ownership) timeshare each purchaser receives a deeded fractional interest that can be sold or willed. In a right-to-use timeshare the purchaser holds only a contract right, license, or lease for a term of years, and title stays with the developer. Timeshare sales are regulated by state statute, often with rescission rights; varies by state.

### § 5.4 Planned unit developments

A planned unit development (PUD) is a zoning and ownership arrangement in which individual lots are owned in fee simple while streets, open space, and recreational facilities are owned by a homeowners' association of which every lot owner is a member. The association, not the lot owners as tenants in common, owns the common areas, which is the main structural difference from a condominium. PUD zoning allows mixed uses and clustered density in exchange for preserved open space.

### § 5.5 Keeping the forms straight

Condominium: fee title to the unit plus tenancy in common in the common elements. Cooperative: stock plus a proprietary lease; the corporation owns the real estate. PUD: fee title to the lot; the association owns the common areas. Timeshare: deeded fractional fee (interval ownership) or a right to use.

Example. In a high-rise condominium a buyer receives a deed to the unit and a share of the common elements; in a PUD she receives a deed to the lot while the association owns the pool and streets. In neither case does she own stock; that would be a cooperative.

## § 6 Deeds

### § 6.1 Deed versus title; requirements of a valid deed

Title is the right to ownership; a deed is the written instrument by which title is voluntarily transferred from grantor to grantee. A deed does not prove title is good; it transfers whatever the grantor has. A valid deed requires:

- A legally competent grantor (of age and sound mind), identifiable, who signs the deed.
- An identifiable grantee capable of taking title; a deed to a fictitious or deceased person is void. The grantee need not sign.
- Words of conveyance (the granting clause), such as "grants and conveys," showing present intent to transfer.
- A statement of consideration, often nominal ("ten dollars and other good and valuable consideration"); a gift deed may recite "love and affection."
- An adequate legal description (REP Ref. Property & Land Use § 3).
- Delivery by the grantor and acceptance by the grantee during the grantor's lifetime (§ 11.2).

Other common parts: the habendum clause ("to have and to hold"), which defines the estate granted and must agree with the granting clause; exceptions and reservations (easements, mineral rights, existing restrictions); and an acknowledgment, the grantor's declaration before a notary that the signature is voluntary. Acknowledgment is generally required to record a deed but not for validity between the parties. Some states require witnesses; varies by state. A deed need not be dated or recorded to be valid, though both are prudent.

### § 6.2 Covenants of title

A warranty deed contains the grantor's promises (covenants) about title. The traditional six: seisin (the grantor owns the estate conveyed), right to convey (authority to transfer it), against encumbrances (none other than those stated), quiet enjoyment (the grantee will not be disturbed by a superior claim), warranty (the grantor will defend the title and compensate for loss from a superior claim), and further assurances (the grantor will sign further documents needed to perfect title). The first three are present covenants, breached if at all at delivery; the last three are future covenants, breached only when a superior claim is later asserted.

### § 6.3 General warranty deed

A general warranty deed gives the grantee the greatest protection: the grantor makes all the covenants of title and warrants against defects arising at any time in the chain of title, before or during the grantor's ownership. Many states have statutory short-form deeds in which words such as "conveys and warrants" imply the full covenants.

### § 6.4 Special (limited) warranty deed

A special warranty deed limits the covenants to the grantor's own period of ownership: the grantor warrants only against claims "by, through, or under" the grantor, and gives no protection against defects created before the grantor took title. Fiduciaries, lenders selling foreclosed property, and corporate sellers commonly use it.

### § 6.5 Bargain and sale deed

A bargain and sale deed conveys title with an implied representation that the grantor holds title and possession, but without express warranties against encumbrances or defects. A covenant against the grantor's own acts is sometimes added, making it similar to a special warranty deed. It is used in some states in foreclosure and tax sales and by fiduciaries; the buyer relies on title insurance rather than the grantor's promise.

### § 6.6 Quitclaim deed

A quitclaim deed conveys whatever interest the grantor has, if any, with no warranties and no implication that the grantor has any interest. If the grantor owns fee simple, a quitclaim conveys it as effectively as a warranty deed; if the grantor owns nothing, the grantee receives nothing and has no recourse. Quitclaims are used to clear clouds on title (a former spouse's or heir's possible interest, a boundary dispute), to transfer between family members, and to release easements. A quitclaim does not pass title the grantor acquires later.

### § 6.7 Special-purpose deeds

Special-purpose deeds are named for the grantor's capacity or the transaction: executor's or administrator's deed (a personal representative conveying a decedent's property), guardian's deed, trustee's deed (including after a deed-of-trust foreclosure), sheriff's or referee's deed (after a judicial foreclosure or execution sale), tax deed, deed in lieu of foreclosure (borrower conveys to the lender to avoid foreclosure), gift deed, correction deed, and deed of reconveyance (trustee returns title when a deed-of-trust loan is repaid). Most carry no warranties beyond the grantor's own acts. A deed of trust, despite its name, is a financing instrument.

### § 6.8 Applying the deed types

Example. A buyer receives a general warranty deed. Two years later an heir of a 1980s owner asserts an unreleased interest. The buyer may look to the seller under the covenant of warranty even though the defect predates the seller's ownership. Under a special warranty deed the seller would owe nothing for a pre-ownership defect, and the buyer would rely on the owner's title policy.

## § 7 Voluntary alienation

### § 7.1 Transfer during life

Alienation is the transfer of title. Voluntary alienation occurs when the owner chooses to transfer by sale, gift, dedication to public use, or conveyance into trust, each accomplished by a deed delivered during the owner's lifetime.

### § 7.2 Transfer at death by will

A will is a written instrument, effective only at death, by which a testator directs how property passes. A gift of real property by will is a devise and the recipient a devisee; a gift of personal property is a bequest or legacy. A valid will generally requires a testator of legal age and sound mind, a writing, the testator's signature, and attesting witnesses; some states accept a handwritten unwitnessed (holographic) will; requirements vary by state. A codicil amends a will. A will conveys nothing during life and may be revoked at any time; title passes to devisees only through probate (§ 12.1). A surviving spouse's elective share or homestead rights may override the will; varies by state.

## § 8 Involuntary alienation

### § 8.1 Descent (intestate succession)

When an owner dies without a valid will, or the will fails to dispose of all property, title passes to heirs under the state's statute of descent and distribution, which sets the order and shares (typically spouse and children first, then parents, siblings, and more distant relatives). Heirs take through probate administration; if none can be found, the property escheats (§ 8.2).

### § 8.2 Escheat

Escheat is the reversion to the state of property of a person who dies intestate without heirs, or of property legally abandoned, so that land is never ownerless; it follows the statutory search for heirs.

### § 8.3 Eminent domain

Government and delegated entities may take private property for public use upon payment of just compensation through condemnation; title passes by court order or by a deed signed under threat of condemnation. See REP Ref. Property & Land Use § 4.3.

### § 8.4 Adverse possession

Adverse possession lets a person who occupies another's land for the statutory period acquire title. The possession must be actual (physical use as an owner would), open and notorious (visible), exclusive (not shared with the owner or the public), hostile or adverse (without permission, under a claim of right), and continuous for the period set by state statute. Some states shorten the period when the possessor has color of title (a defective deed) or has paid the property taxes. Successive possessors in privity may add their periods together (tacking). Government land generally cannot be adversely possessed. The possessor perfects title by a quiet title action whose judgment is recorded. Compare prescriptive easements (REP Ref. Property & Land Use § 7.3), which give a right of use rather than title and do not require exclusivity. Period and elements vary by state.

Example. A neighbor fences and gardens a 10-foot strip of the adjoining lot for longer than the statutory period while the record owner neither objects nor gives permission. The neighbor sues to quiet title and is awarded the strip. Had the owner granted written permission early on, the possession would not have been hostile and the claim would fail.

### § 8.5 Foreclosure

Foreclosure is the lienholder's remedy of forcing a sale to satisfy a defaulted debt. Judicial foreclosure proceeds through a lawsuit and court-ordered sale (sheriff's or referee's deed). Non-judicial foreclosure, where the security instrument contains a power of sale and state law permits, proceeds by notice and trustee's sale without a court (trustee's deed). Strict foreclosure, in which the court transfers title to the lender without a sale, survives in a few states. Before the sale the borrower may pay the debt under the equitable right of redemption; after the sale some states allow a statutory redemption period. If the price is less than the debt the lender may seek a deficiency judgment where allowed; a surplus goes to junior lienholders and then the borrower. A borrower may instead give a deed in lieu of foreclosure with the lender's consent. Methods and redemption rights vary by state.

### § 8.6 Other involuntary transfers

Partition divides or sells co-owned property at a co-owner's request; a bankruptcy trustee may sell a debtor's property; tax sales (§ 12.2) are involuntary. Natural forces also change title: accretion (gradual addition of soil by water) and reliction (land exposed when water permanently recedes) go to the adjoining owner; erosion is gradual loss; avulsion, a sudden shift of land by flood or a change of a stream's course, generally does not move the boundary.

## § 9 Recording and notice

### § 9.1 The recording system

Each county (or equivalent) maintains public records in which deeds, mortgages, liens, easements, plats, and other instruments affecting title are recorded and indexed by grantor and grantee names or by parcel. Recording is not required for a deed to be valid between the parties; a delivered, unrecorded deed passes title. Recording protects the grantee against the rest of the world by giving public notice of the interest, so a later purchaser or lender cannot claim to be unaware of it. Generally a document must be acknowledged to be recorded, and a recording fee and any transfer tax are paid.

### § 9.2 Actual, constructive, and inquiry notice

Actual notice is knowledge a person in fact has. Constructive notice is knowledge the law charges a person with, whether or not the person actually knows, because the information was available: everything properly recorded gives constructive notice, and so does open possession of the property by someone other than the record owner. Inquiry notice arises when facts would lead a reasonable person to investigate; the person is charged with what the investigation would have revealed. A buyer who neither searches the records nor inspects the property is bound by what those steps would have shown.

### § 9.3 Priority under recording acts

Recording acts decide who wins when a grantor conveys the same interest twice. Under a race statute the first to record wins regardless of knowledge. Under a notice statute a later bona fide purchaser for value without notice (actual or constructive) of the earlier interest wins even before recording. Under a race-notice statute the later purchaser wins only by both lacking notice and recording first. Which applies varies by state; in every system, prompt recording is how a buyer or lender protects a newly acquired interest.

Example. On Monday a seller deeds a lot to A, who does not record. On Wednesday the seller deeds the same lot to B, who pays value, knows nothing of A, and records Thursday. In a notice or race-notice state B owns the lot and A's remedy is against the seller. Had A recorded on Monday, B would have had constructive notice and A would prevail.

## § 10 Title evidence

### § 10.1 Chain of title and clouds

The chain of title is the sequence of recorded conveyances and encumbrances tracing ownership from the earliest record (often a government patent) to the present owner; each grantee should appear as the next grantor. A gap (a missing link, such as a deed from someone who never appears as a grantee) or a cloud (any recorded item casting doubt on title, such as an unreleased mortgage or a deed from one co-owner alone) must be cured before title is marketable. Cures include a recorded release or quitclaim from the holder of the doubtful interest, an explanatory affidavit, or a quiet title action, in which a court declares who owns the property and extinguishes adverse claims.

### § 10.2 Title search, abstract, and opinion

A title search is an examination of the public records (deeds, mortgages, liens, judgments, taxes, probate, plats) for the parcel over a period fixed by custom or statute. An abstract of title is a condensed history of every recorded instrument affecting the parcel, prepared by an abstractor and continued at each transfer. An attorney reviews the abstract or search and issues an opinion of title stating who owns the property and what encumbrances exist. These reveal only what is in the records; they give no protection against forgeries, undisclosed heirs, or recording errors, which is what title insurance covers.

### § 10.3 Torrens registration

A few states offer a Torrens system in which title is registered with a court and the resulting certificate of title is conclusive evidence of ownership and encumbrances. Varies by state.

### § 10.4 Title insurance

Title insurance is an indemnity contract in which the insurer agrees to defend the insured's title and pay losses caused by covered defects existing on the policy date, for a one-time premium paid at closing. Before closing the insurer issues a commitment (binder) listing the exceptions it will take, giving the parties a chance to cure problems.

Two kinds of policy exist. A lender's (mortgagee's) policy protects only the lender, for the loan amount, declining as the loan is paid down and ending when it is repaid; lenders routinely require it. An owner's policy protects the buyer for the purchase price for as long as the insured or the insured's heirs hold an interest; it is optional, but without it the owner alone bears the cost of defending a claim that the lender's policy covers only for the lender. Buying both together is usually cheaper than separately. Who customarily pays for the owner's policy varies by state and contract.

A standard policy typically covers defects in the recorded chain: forged or improperly delivered deeds, undisclosed heirs, recording mistakes, lack of capacity, unreleased liens the search missed. Standard exclusions and exceptions commonly include defects the insured knew of and did not disclose, defects created after the policy date, rights of parties in possession not shown by the records, unrecorded easements, boundary and encroachment matters a survey would reveal, unrecorded mechanic's liens, taxes not yet due, and government regulation such as zoning and eminent domain. Extended coverage removes some of these, typically survey and possession matters. An insurer that pays a claim is subrogated to the insured's rights against whoever caused the loss.

Example. A buyer pays $400,000 with a $320,000 loan. The lender's policy is for $320,000 and protects only the lender; the buyer also buys a $400,000 owner's policy. When a forged deed in the chain surfaces, the insurer defends the buyer's title and, if it cannot be cured, pays her loss up to $400,000. Without the owner's policy, the lender's policy would have paid only the loan balance, to the lender.

### § 10.5 Marketable title

Marketable title is title free from reasonable doubt: one a reasonably prudent, well-informed buyer would accept, not subject to undisclosed liens or encumbrances, and not exposing the buyer to litigation. It need not be perfect, only free of material defects. Purchase contracts ordinarily require the seller to deliver marketable title at closing, subject to encumbrances the contract lists; if the seller cannot cure a defect in time, the buyer may generally cancel and recover the deposit or accept the title as is. Marketability is judged at closing, so the seller may use the interval to clear clouds. Unmarketability is a title defect, not a physical one: an unreleased lien may make title unmarketable, while a leaky roof does not.

## § 11 Closing, settlement, and passage of title

### § 11.1 Roles at settlement

Closing (settlement) is the event at which the seller delivers the deed, the buyer pays the price, and documents are recorded. In some states the parties meet with attorneys; in others an escrow agent closes without a meeting. The settlement (closing) agent, whether a title company, escrow company, attorney, or lender representative, is a neutral party who follows both sides' written instructions, collects and disburses funds, prepares the settlement statement, obtains lien payoffs and releases, and records the deed and mortgage. In some states an attorney must conduct or review the closing. Licensees confirm contract conditions are met, review the settlement statement, and are paid from proceeds. Federal closing disclosures are covered in the RESPA and TILA materials.

### § 11.2 When title passes: delivery and acceptance

Title passes when the deed is delivered by the grantor and accepted by the grantee, not when it is signed and not when it is recorded. Delivery is the grantor's act, with present intent, of putting the deed beyond the grantor's control so that title passes now. Handing the deed to the grantee is the ordinary case; delivery may also be made to an escrow agent with instructions to release it when conditions are met, and title then passes when they are satisfied. Delivery must occur during the grantor's lifetime: a signed deed found in the grantor's desk after death conveys nothing. Acceptance is presumed when the conveyance benefits the grantee. Recording raises a presumption of delivery but is not itself the moment of transfer. If the grantor dies after depositing a deed in escrow, many states treat the transfer as relating back to the deposit; varies by state.

Example. A seller signs a deed on Monday and gives it to the escrow agent. The buyer's funds arrive Thursday, the agent releases the deed, and it is recorded Friday. Title passed Thursday, when the escrow conditions were met and the deed was released to the buyer.

### § 11.3 Documents and possession

Closing documents typically include the deed; an affidavit of title in which the seller swears to marital status and the absence of new liens or unrecorded interests; a bill of sale for personal property; payoff and release documents; the buyer's note and mortgage or deed of trust; and the settlement statement. Possession passes when the contract says, commonly at closing; possession and title are separate, so early or late possession calls for an occupancy agreement.

## § 12 Special transfers

### § 12.1 Probate transfers

Probate is the court process that proves a will, appoints a personal representative (an executor named in the will, or an administrator if there is none), pays the decedent's debts and taxes, and distributes the remainder to devisees or heirs. Real property is conveyed by an executor's or administrator's deed, often after court approval, usually without warranties beyond the representative's own acts. Expect court timelines and "as is" terms; procedures vary by state.

### § 12.2 Tax sales

When property taxes remain unpaid, the taxing authority enforces its lien by a tax sale. In some states a tax certificate is sold to an investor who is repaid with interest if the owner redeems within the statutory period and who may obtain a tax deed if the owner does not; in others the property itself is sold at a tax deed sale. A tax deed carries no warranties, and the title may need a quiet title action before it is insurable. Redemption periods vary by state.

### § 12.3 Short sales

A short sale is a sale by a distressed owner for less than the mortgage balance, with the lender's consent to release its lien for the reduced payoff. It is a voluntary sale, not a foreclosure; the owner conveys by ordinary deed. Lender approval is a contingency that can take weeks; the lender may or may not waive the deficiency, and forgiven debt can have tax consequences. Disclosure that the sale requires lender approval is commonly required.

### § 12.4 Real estate owned (REO) and lender sales

REO (real estate owned) is property a lender acquired at its own foreclosure sale or by deed in lieu and now holds for resale. The lender sells as an ordinary owner, usually "as is," commonly by special warranty or bargain and sale deed, with its own addenda. The buyer should verify that any redemption period has run and junior liens were extinguished, and should obtain an owner's title policy. A buyer at the foreclosure sale itself (§ 8.5) receives the officer's or trustee's deed without warranties and takes subject to senior liens and any redemption rights, so a title search before bidding is essential.
