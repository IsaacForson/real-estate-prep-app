# REP Ref. Agency

Reference note: agency relationships in real estate brokerage — how they arise and end, the duties they impose, and the disclosures they require. Authored expository text (status: draft — needs licensed review). Covers Pearson VUE national outline IV.E–IV.G and PSI national outline domain VI. Agency law is state law; where a rule varies by state, the state portion of the exam governs. This note describes the general common-law framework and the forms of representation that state statutes commonly permit or modify.

## § 1 Agency law basics

### § 1.1 Definitions

**Agency** is a relationship in which one person (the **agent**) is authorized to act on behalf of another (the **principal**) in dealings with third parties, and agrees to do so subject to the principal's control. The agent owes the principal **fiduciary** duties — duties of the highest trust and loyalty — because the agent is handling the principal's affairs. In real estate brokerage the principal is the **client** (a seller, buyer, landlord, or tenant who has engaged the brokerage), and the agent is the **broker**. Salespersons and associate brokers licensed under a broker act for the broker and, through the broker, for the broker's clients; under general agency law they are agents of the broker and sub-agents of the client. A person in the transaction who is *not* represented by the brokerage is a **customer** (or third party). The distinction between client and customer determines which set of duties applies (§ 5 versus § 6).

### § 1.2 Universal, general, and special agency

Agency is classified by the scope of authority the principal has conferred.

- A **universal agent** may do anything the principal could lawfully do. This is created by a broad power of attorney and is rare in brokerage.
- A **general agent** may act for the principal in a range of matters within a continuing business or activity and may bind the principal to contracts within that scope. A property manager who leases units, hires contractors, and collects rents under a management agreement is a general agent of the owner. A salesperson is generally treated as a general agent of the sponsoring broker.
- A **special agent** (limited agent) is authorized to perform one specific task, usually without the power to bind the principal to a contract. A listing broker is a special agent: the broker is engaged to find a ready, willing, and able buyer, but cannot sign a purchase agreement for the seller or accept an offer on the seller's behalf unless separately authorized.

Candidates often assume a listing broker can "accept" an offer for the seller. Under ordinary special agency the broker only transmits offers; acceptance is the principal's act.

### § 1.3 Fiduciary

A fiduciary is one who acts for another in a position of trust and confidence. Trustees, executors, attorneys, and agents are fiduciaries. The duties that follow from that status in brokerage are collected in § 5. The point to hold onto is that a fiduciary must put the principal's interests above the fiduciary's own and above those of third parties, within the limits of the law and honesty toward everyone.

## § 2 Forms of representation in a real estate transaction

### § 2.1 Overview and state variation

The forms below are the common vocabulary of representation. Not every state permits every form; several states have abolished subagency in practice, some prohibit dual agency, some define non-agency "transaction broker" or "facilitator" status by statute, and most have enacted statutes that replace or restate common-law duties. Wherever this note says "generally," the state portion governs.

### § 2.2 Seller agency (listing agency)

The brokerage represents the seller under a listing agreement and owes the seller fiduciary duties. The buyer, unless separately represented, is a customer who is owed honesty and fair dealing but not loyalty. A seller's agent must, for example, try to obtain the highest price and best terms for the seller, and may not reveal to a buyer that the seller will accept less than the list price without the seller's permission.

### § 2.3 Buyer agency

The brokerage represents the buyer under a buyer representation agreement and owes the buyer fiduciary duties. The seller is the customer. A buyer's agent must try to obtain the lowest price and best terms for the buyer and must not reveal, for instance, that the buyer is willing to pay more than the offered price. Buyer agency became widespread after courts and regulators recognized that most buyers wrongly believed the licensee showing them property was "their" agent when, under traditional subagency, that licensee actually represented the seller.

### § 2.4 Dual agency

A single brokerage (and, in the classic form, a single licensee) represents both the buyer and the seller in the same transaction. Because full loyalty to two parties with opposing interests on price is impossible, dual agency is permitted only with the **informed written consent of both parties** in states that allow it at all, and the dual agent's duties are necessarily limited: the agent may not advocate on price or terms for either side, and may not disclose confidential information of one party to the other (for example, the seller's bottom line or the buyer's maximum). **Undisclosed dual agency** — representing both sides without both parties' knowledge and consent — is a serious license law violation and a breach of fiduciary duty that can result in loss of commission, rescission of the transaction, and discipline. Dual agency can arise accidentally at the brokerage level when a buyer-client of one salesperson becomes interested in a property listed by another salesperson in the same firm; in-house sales are therefore where designated agency (§ 2.5) matters.

Example: A brokerage lists Sam's house and also represents Tia as a buyer-client. Tia wants to write an offer on Sam's house. Unless the state provides for designated agency, the brokerage is now a dual agent and must obtain written consent from both Sam and Tia before proceeding, and must stop advocating on price for either.

### § 2.5 Designated (appointed) agency

A statutory arrangement, available in many states, in which the broker designates one licensee in the firm to represent the seller and a different licensee to represent the buyer in the same transaction. Each designated licensee owes full fiduciary duties to his or her own client; the supervising broker is typically treated as a dual agent or as a neutral party who must protect the confidences of both. Designated agency is meant to solve the in-house dual agency problem while preserving advocacy for each side. The exact duties of the broker and the disclosure and consent required vary by state.

### § 2.6 Transaction brokerage and other non-agency roles

Some states allow a licensee to assist one or both parties **without representing either** — as a transaction broker, facilitator, or intermediary (terminology varies). A non-agent owes no fiduciary loyalty but is still bound by statutory duties that commonly include honesty, disclosure of known material facts about the property, accounting for funds, reasonable skill and care, and confidentiality of certain information. A transaction broker does not negotiate for either party. Whether this role exists, and what it is called, is a state question; the state portion governs. Candidates should recognize that "non-agency" does not mean "no duties."

### § 2.7 Subagency

A **subagent** is an agent of an agent: a licensee who, with the principal's authorization, assists the listing broker in serving the seller and thereby owes the seller fiduciary duties even though the subagent may be the only licensee the buyer is working with. Historically, a blanket offer of subagency through a multiple listing service made every cooperating licensee a subagent of the seller. This produced the misunderstanding described in § 2.3 and exposed sellers to liability for the acts of licensees they had never met. Many states have curtailed or eliminated blanket subagency; today a cooperating licensee is far more often a buyer's agent. A principal is generally liable for the acts of an authorized subagent within the scope of the agency.

### § 2.8 Single agency

A brokerage policy of representing only one side in any transaction — never acting as dual agent. If a buyer-client wants to buy the firm's listing, a single-agency firm typically refers one party to another brokerage.

## § 3 How agency is created

### § 3.1 Express agreement

Agency is most commonly created by an **express** agreement, written or oral, in which the principal authorizes the agent to act. In brokerage the written instruments are the listing agreement (seller agency), the buyer representation agreement (buyer agency), and the property management agreement (see REP Ref. Brokerage Practice for their terms). Many states require these to be in writing for the licensee to be entitled to a commission or as a matter of license law; general agency law does not always require a writing. Oral agency is legally possible but leaves the scope of authority and the compensation unprovable.

### § 3.2 Implied agency (agency by conduct)

Agency can be created by the conduct of the parties, without a written or spoken agreement, when the licensee acts as though representing a person and that person reasonably relies on the licensee's representation. A licensee who advises an unrepresented buyer on what to offer, negotiates on the buyer's behalf, and tells the buyer "I'll take care of you" may have become the buyer's implied agent — and if the licensee is also the listing agent, an undisclosed dual agent. Implied agency is the main reason licensees are trained to disclose whom they represent early and to avoid giving advice to customers that only a client should receive.

### § 3.3 Ostensible agency and estoppel

**Ostensible** (apparent) agency arises when a principal's words or conduct lead a third party reasonably to believe that someone is the principal's agent, and the third party relies on that belief. The principal is then **estopped** (barred) from denying the agency as to that third party. The key is that the appearance is created by the *principal*, not merely claimed by the supposed agent. Example: a seller tells a neighbor, "Talk to my nephew about buying the lot; he handles that for me." If the nephew negotiates terms with the neighbor, the seller may be bound even though no formal agency was created.

### § 3.4 Ratification

Agency by **ratification** arises when a person acts without authority (or beyond authority) and the principal, with knowledge of the facts, afterward accepts the benefit of the act or otherwise approves it. The ratification is retroactive: the principal is treated as though the act had been authorized from the beginning. A seller who accepts an offer procured by a licensee who had no listing has ratified the licensee's acts (though whether a commission is owed depends on the agreement and state law).

### § 3.5 Compensation does not determine agency

Who pays the licensee does not decide whom the licensee represents. A buyer's agent may be paid from the seller's proceeds, by the listing broker's cooperative offer, or by the buyer directly, and remains the buyer's agent in every case. Conversely, a licensee who is paid nothing may still be an agent (a gratuitous agent) with full fiduciary duties. Agency is created by agreement or conduct (§§ 3.1–3.4), not by the flow of money. Compensation arrangements should nevertheless be disclosed to all parties (§ 7.3).

## § 4 How agency is terminated

### § 4.1 Acts of the parties

- **Completion (performance)** of the purpose — the listed property closes; the buyer-client purchases a home.
- **Expiration** of the term stated in the agreement. Most states require listing and buyer agreements to have a definite termination date; automatic renewal clauses are prohibited in many states.
- **Mutual agreement** to end the relationship before the term or purpose is complete.
- **Revocation** by the principal or **renunciation** by the agent. Either party has the *power* to end the agency at any time because agency is a consensual, personal relationship; but neither necessarily has the *right* to do so. A party who terminates without cause before the agreed term may be liable to the other for damages — a seller who cancels an exclusive listing early to sell privately may owe the broker the commission or expenses; a broker who abandons a listing may owe the seller damages. Courts will not compel a principal to keep an agent, so the remedy is money, not specific performance.

### § 4.2 Operation of law

- **Death or incapacity** of either the principal or the agent (broker). Because the relationship is personal, the death of a sole-proprietor broker terminates the firm's listings; the death of a salesperson does not, because the salesperson is not the seller's agent in the primary sense — the broker is. Death of the seller terminates the listing (the estate may re-list). Note the contrast with contracts of sale, which usually bind heirs.
- **Destruction** or condemnation of the property, which makes the purpose impossible.
- **Bankruptcy** of the principal, when it transfers control of the property to a trustee, or of the broker.
- **Change in law** making the object of the agency illegal.
- **Loss of the broker's license**, which makes lawful performance impossible.

### § 4.3 Duties that survive termination

The duty of **confidentiality** generally survives the end of the agency: a former listing agent may not later tell a buyer what the former seller-client's bottom line was, and a former buyer's agent may not disclose the former client's finances or motivations. Duties of loyalty and obedience end with the relationship; the duty to account for money already received continues until the accounting is complete.

## § 5 Fiduciary duties owed to the client

### § 5.1 Overview — the OLD CAR mnemonic

The common-law fiduciary duties are usually listed as **O**bedience, **L**oyalty, **D**isclosure, **C**onfidentiality, **A**ccounting, and **R**easonable care and diligence. Many state statutes restate these with different labels or add specific duties (such as presenting all offers promptly); the state portion governs any statutory formulation. Each duty is discussed below with an example of compliance and breach.

### § 5.2 Obedience

The agent must follow the principal's **lawful** instructions promptly and faithfully. Instructions to break the law — to refuse to show the property to families with children (a violation of the cached Fair Housing Act), to conceal a known defect, to misstate square footage — must be refused; obedience never requires illegality, and an agent who follows an unlawful instruction is personally liable. Example: a seller instructs the listing agent not to present offers below $500,000. In most states the agent must present all offers regardless; the agent should explain the legal duty rather than obey. By contrast, a seller's instruction to show the home only between 10 a.m. and 6 p.m. is lawful and must be followed.

### § 5.3 Loyalty

The agent must act solely in the principal's best interest, placing it above the agent's own interest and above the interests of third parties. Loyalty forbids **self-dealing** — buying the client's listing through a relative or an entity without full disclosure and consent, or steering the client toward a transaction because it pays the agent more. It forbids **secret profits**, such as a referral fee or repair-vendor kickback the client does not know about. It forbids representing an adverse party without informed consent (undisclosed dual agency, § 2.4). Example: a buyer's agent knows the buyer would pay $410,000 but the buyer offers $390,000. Loyalty requires the agent to say nothing to the seller's side about the buyer's ceiling and to negotiate for the lowest price the seller will take.

### § 5.4 Disclosure (full disclosure to the principal)

The agent must tell the principal every fact the agent knows, or should know, that could affect the principal's decisions in the transaction. For a seller-client this includes all offers (in most states, all written offers until closing), the buyer's ability to perform, relationships between the agent and the buyer, the true market value if the client is under- or over-pricing, and any fact bearing on the buyer's likely willingness to pay more. For a buyer-client it includes known property defects, how long the property has been on the market, comparable sales, and facts about the seller's motivation the agent has lawfully learned. This duty runs *to the client*; the parallel but narrower duty to disclose material facts to customers is in § 6.3. Example: a listing agent learns that the buyer, whose offer is pending, has just been laid off. The agent must tell the seller.

### § 5.5 Confidentiality

The agent must not reveal information that would weaken the principal's bargaining position unless the principal authorizes it or the law requires disclosure. Protected information typically includes the seller's willingness to accept less than list price, the reason for selling (divorce, job relocation, financial distress), the buyer's maximum price or urgency, and either party's financial condition. Confidentiality does **not** cover material facts about the property's physical condition or legal status that the law requires to be disclosed to the other party — a known roof leak is not a "confidence," and a seller's instruction to hide it is unlawful (§ 5.2). The duty survives the agency (§ 4.3). Example: after the listing expires, the former seller-client lists with another firm. A buyer asks the first agent, "Would they have taken less?" The agent must decline to answer.

### § 5.6 Accounting

The agent must keep the principal's money and property separate from the agent's own, keep accurate records, and turn over or account for all funds and documents received in the course of the agency — earnest money, rents, security deposits, keys, documents. Mixing client funds with brokerage operating funds is **commingling**; spending client funds for any unauthorized purpose is **conversion** (§ 8). Example: a property manager collects rents into the firm's general account and pays office rent from it. That is commingling even if the owner is later paid in full.

### § 5.7 Reasonable care and diligence (skill and care)

The agent must perform with the competence expected of a licensed professional: know market values, know the standard forms and how to complete them, meet deadlines, verify facts within the agent's expertise before repeating them, recommend inspections and professional advice where appropriate, and follow through. An agent who fails to submit an offer before the deadline, misstates the acreage from memory, or advises a client on a tax consequence the agent is not qualified to assess may be liable for negligence. Care does not require the agent to be an inspector, appraiser, attorney, or accountant; it requires the agent to recognize when the client needs one and to say so.

## § 6 Duties owed to customers and other non-clients

### § 6.1 Honesty and fair dealing

To everyone in the transaction, represented or not, a licensee owes honesty and fair dealing. A seller's agent may not lie to a buyer, may not conceal a defect the agent knows of, and may not draft a contract term designed to trick the buyer. Fair dealing does not require advocacy for the customer — the seller's agent may still negotiate hard for the seller — but it forbids deception. Most state license laws make misrepresentation to any party a disciplinary offense regardless of agency.

### § 6.2 No misrepresentation

A **misrepresentation** is a false statement of fact on which another party relies. It may be **intentional** (fraud), **negligent** (the licensee should have known better or failed to check), or **innocent**. Even an innocent misrepresentation can give the injured party a right to rescind; negligent and intentional misrepresentation can support damages, and fraud can support punitive damages and license discipline. **Concealment** (actively hiding a fact) and, where a duty to disclose exists, **nondisclosure** are treated as misrepresentation. Example: a listing agent tells a buyer the basement "never floods" without asking the seller; the basement floods every spring. The agent may be liable for negligent misrepresentation even without intent to deceive.

### § 6.3 Disclosure of material facts

A **material fact** is one that a reasonable person would consider important in deciding whether, or on what terms, to buy, sell, or lease. Licensees generally must disclose to all parties material facts about the property that they know (and in many states, that they reasonably should know) — structural defects, water intrusion, environmental hazards, zoning violations, boundary disputes, and the like. This duty runs to the customer as well as the client and cannot be waived by the client's instruction. Whether facts *not* about the physical property (a death on the premises, a nearby registered offender, alleged hauntings, or the property's stigma) are material and must be disclosed varies by state; many states expressly exclude some of these. The state portion governs. Latent (hidden) defects known to the seller or agent that a buyer could not discover through reasonable inspection are the classic category that must be disclosed.

### § 6.4 Puffing versus misrepresentation

**Puffing** is an exaggerated, subjective statement of opinion that no reasonable person would rely on as fact: "the best view in the county," "a dream kitchen," "you'll love the neighborhood." It is not actionable. **Misrepresentation** is a false statement of *fact*: "the roof was replaced last year," "the lot is two acres," "the school district is X." The line is whether the statement is verifiable and whether a reasonable buyer would rely on it. A statement of opinion by someone with superior knowledge can become a factual representation when the buyer reasonably relies on the licensee's expertise — "this foundation is solid" from a licensee who saw the cracks is not puffing. Licensees should state facts only when they have verified them and should attribute unverified information to its source ("the seller reports…").

### § 6.5 Statutory duties to customers

Many states list in statute what a licensee owes a customer: honesty; disclosure of known material defects; accounting for money received; presenting offers promptly; and, in some states, reasonable skill and care in performing ministerial acts. A licensee performing **ministerial acts** for a customer — providing forms, scheduling showings, transmitting offers, explaining what a form says without advising what to do — does not thereby create an agency. Advising a customer what to offer, or how to negotiate, moves beyond ministerial acts and risks implied agency (§ 3.2).

## § 7 Disclosure of representation and conflicts of interest

### § 7.1 Agency disclosure

Because parties often misunderstand whom a licensee represents, most states require licensees to disclose, in writing, at a defined early point (first substantive contact, before showing property, or before an offer is written — the timing varies by state), whom they represent and what that means. The disclosure informs the customer that the licensee is *not* the customer's agent and will pass along information the customer volunteers. Some states use a mandatory state-prescribed form; some require a pamphlet describing the forms of representation available in that state; some require only a signed acknowledgment. The **agency disclosure form** is a disclosure, not a contract — signing it does not create agency; it confirms that the recipient has been informed. Agency itself is created by the agreements and conduct in § 3. Failure to disclose is a common basis for license discipline.

### § 7.2 Consent to dual or designated agency

Where dual or designated agency is permitted, the law almost always requires informed written consent of both parties, obtained before the dual role begins or as soon as it arises. The consent must explain what the parties give up (advocacy on price; disclosure of confidential information). Consent buried in a listing agreement signed months earlier may not satisfy state requirements that consent be informed and timely; the state portion governs.

### § 7.3 Conflicts of interest

A licensee must disclose any interest that could affect the licensee's judgment or the client's decision: that the licensee or a relative or business associate is the buyer or seller; that the licensee will receive compensation from more than one party or a referral fee from a service provider; that the licensee has an ownership interest in a title, mortgage, or inspection company being recommended; or that the licensee is representing another buyer for the same property. Disclosure must be made before the client acts on the matter, and the client's consent obtained where the conflict is one the client can waive. A licensee buying or selling his or her own property must disclose licensee status to the other party in most states.

### § 7.4 Disclosure of compensation

All parties should be informed, in writing, of how and by whom the licensee will be compensated, particularly where a buyer's agent is paid through the listing broker or where a licensee receives compensation from more than one source. Receiving compensation from more than one party without the knowledge and consent of all is a breach of loyalty (§ 5.3) and a license law violation in most states.

## § 8 Handling others' money: commingling and conversion

### § 8.1 Commingling

**Commingling** is mixing money belonging to others (earnest money, rents, security deposits, tenant funds) with the licensee's or brokerage's own funds — depositing a buyer's deposit into the firm's operating account, or leaving brokerage money in the trust account beyond the small amount many states allow for bank charges. Commingling is a violation even if no one loses money, because it exposes client funds to the brokerage's creditors and obscures the accounting.

### § 8.2 Conversion

**Conversion** is using money belonging to others for a purpose not authorized — paying the office payroll from earnest money, "borrowing" a security deposit. Conversion is theft in substance and typically results in license revocation and criminal exposure. Commingling is the path to conversion, which is why regulators treat commingling seriously. Trust account mechanics are covered in REP Ref. Brokerage Practice.

## § 9 Liability arising from agency

### § 9.1 Principal's liability for the agent

A principal is generally liable to third parties for the agent's acts within the scope of the agency, including misrepresentations the agent makes, and may be liable for a subagent's acts (§ 2.7). This is why sellers should care what their listing agent says about the property, and why brokers are liable for the acts of their salespersons (see REP Ref. Brokerage Practice on supervision).

### § 9.2 Agent's liability

An agent is personally liable for his or her own torts (fraud, negligent misrepresentation), for acting beyond authority, and for breach of fiduciary duty to the principal. Remedies for breach of fiduciary duty include loss or disgorgement of the commission, damages, rescission of the transaction in appropriate cases, and license discipline. An agent who breaches loyalty may forfeit the commission even when the principal suffered no measurable loss.

### § 9.3 Vicarious liability and the broker

Because the broker is the agent of the client and the salesperson acts through the broker, the broker is responsible to the client and to third parties for the salesperson's acts within the scope of the license. A salesperson cannot be the client's agent independently of the broker and, in every state, may accept compensation for brokerage activity only from his or her own broker.

## § 10 Common confusions summarized

### § 10.1 Quick distinctions

- *Client vs. customer*: the client is the principal and receives fiduciary duties; the customer receives honesty, fair dealing, and material-fact disclosure.
- *General vs. special agent*: a property manager (many acts, can bind) versus a listing broker (one task, cannot bind).
- *Dual vs. designated agency*: one representative for both sides with limited duties, versus two licensees in one firm each fully representing one side.
- *Disclosure to the client vs. disclosure to the customer*: everything that affects the client's decision, versus material facts about the property.
- *Confidentiality vs. concealment*: protecting the client's bargaining position is required; hiding a material defect is prohibited.
- *Puffing vs. misrepresentation*: opinion nobody would rely on, versus a false statement of verifiable fact.
- *Power vs. right to terminate*: either party can end an agency at will, but doing so without cause may create liability for damages.
- *Agency disclosure form vs. agency agreement*: the form informs; the agreement (or conduct) creates.
- *Who pays vs. whom you represent*: compensation source never determines agency.
- *Commingling vs. conversion*: mixing funds, versus spending them for an unauthorized purpose.
