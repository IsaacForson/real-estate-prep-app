# REP Ref. Real Estate Math

Authored reference note for the calculation domains of the national examinations: Pearson VUE VIII.A–VIII.G and PSI XI.A–XI.B. Each method gives the formula, the steps and two worked examples. Status: draft, authored 2026-09-08, needs licensed review.

## § 1 Conventions

### § 1.1 Rounding rule

Carry full precision through every intermediate step and round only the final answer. Money is rounded to the nearest cent (half-cent rounds up); percentages to two decimal places; areas to two decimals unless the problem asks for whole units. Where a legal convention requires rounding up to the next increment (transfer taxes, § 10) the note says so. Examination answer choices are usually far enough apart that a one-cent difference in method does not matter; when it might, the problem states the convention.

### § 1.2 Percent and decimal

To use a percent in arithmetic, divide by 100: 6.5% = 0.065; 32 mills = 32 ÷ 1,000 = 0.032 (§ 9.2). To express a decimal as a percent, multiply by 100: 0.0878 = 8.78%.

### § 1.3 The part-whole-rate triangle

Almost every percentage problem is one of three rearrangements of a single relationship: Part = Whole × Rate. Therefore Whole = Part ÷ Rate and Rate = Part ÷ Whole. Identify which of the three quantities the question gives and which it asks for. Commission (part) = price (whole) × rate; interest (part) = principal (whole) × rate; tax (part) = assessed value (whole) × rate; NOI (part) = value (whole) × cap rate. "Of" signals the whole; "is" or "equals" signals the part.

## § 2 Area, volume and land measurement

### § 2.1 Units and conversions

1 acre = 43,560 square feet. 1 square mile = 640 acres = 1 section. A township is 36 sections (6 miles × 6 miles). 1 mile = 5,280 feet. 1 yard = 3 feet; 1 square yard = 9 square feet; 1 cubic yard = 27 cubic feet. 1 hectare = 10,000 square meters ≈ 2.471 acres; 1 acre ≈ 0.4047 hectare. Convert all dimensions to the same unit before multiplying.

### § 2.2 Rectangles and frontage

Area = frontage (width along the street) × depth. Acres = square feet ÷ 43,560.

Example A. A lot is 80 feet wide and 125 feet deep. Area = 80 × 125 = 10,000 sq ft. Acres = 10,000 ÷ 43,560 = 0.2296 → 0.23 acre.

Example B. A rectangular parcel of 2.5 acres has 330 feet of frontage; find the depth. Square feet = 2.5 × 43,560 = 108,900. Depth = 108,900 ÷ 330 = 330 feet.

### § 2.3 Triangles, trapezoids and irregular lots

Triangle: area = ½ × base × height, where the height is measured perpendicular to the base. Trapezoid (two parallel sides): area = (side 1 + side 2) ÷ 2 × depth. An irregular lot is split into rectangles and triangles whose areas are summed.

Example A. A trapezoidal lot has 100 feet on the street, 140 feet at the rear, and a depth of 200 feet. Average width = (100 + 140) ÷ 2 = 120 feet. Area = 120 × 200 = 24,000 sq ft. Acres = 24,000 ÷ 43,560 = 0.5510 → 0.55 acre.

Example B. An L-shaped lot consists of a 100 × 150 foot rectangle with an attached 50 × 60 foot rectangle. Areas: 100 × 150 = 15,000; 50 × 60 = 3,000. Total = 18,000 sq ft. A triangular corner of base 120 feet and height 90 feet would add ½ × 120 × 90 = 5,400 sq ft.

### § 2.4 Volume

Volume = length × width × height, in cubic units. Convert inches to feet (divide by 12) before multiplying; divide cubic feet by 27 for cubic yards.

Example A. A warehouse 60 feet by 100 feet with 14-foot ceilings encloses 60 × 100 × 14 = 84,000 cubic feet.

Example B. A concrete driveway 12 feet wide, 40 feet long and 4 inches thick. Thickness = 4 ÷ 12 = 0.33333 feet. Volume = 12 × 40 × 0.33333 = 160 cubic feet. Cubic yards = 160 ÷ 27 = 5.926 → 5.93 cubic yards.

### § 2.5 Cost per square foot and price per front foot

Price per square foot = price ÷ area; price = area × price per square foot. Price per front foot = price ÷ frontage. Price per acre = price per square foot × 43,560.

Example A. An 1,850 sq ft house sold for $296,000. Price per square foot = 296,000 ÷ 1,850 = $160.00. A similar 2,400 sq ft house at the same rate would be 2,400 × 160 = $384,000.

Example B. Land sells for $3.50 per square foot. Per acre: 3.50 × 43,560 = $152,460. A 0.75-acre parcel: 0.75 × 43,560 = 32,670 sq ft × 3.50 = $114,345. A lot with 75 feet of frontage that sold for $187,500 brought 187,500 ÷ 75 = $2,500 per front foot.

### § 2.6 Government survey fractions

A section contains 640 acres. To find the acreage of a fractional description, multiply the denominators of every fraction in the description and divide 640 by the product. Descriptions joined by "and" are separate parcels whose acreages are added.

Example A. "The S½ of the NW¼ of the SE¼ of Section 8." Denominators 2 × 4 × 4 = 32. Acreage = 640 ÷ 32 = 20 acres.

Example B. "The NE¼ of the NE¼ and the S½ of the SE¼ of Section 20." First parcel: 640 ÷ (4 × 4) = 40 acres. Second parcel: 640 ÷ (2 × 4) = 80 acres. Total = 120 acres.

### § 2.7 Hectares

Example A. 10 hectares × 2.471 = 24.71 acres. Example B. 100 acres × 0.4047 = 40.47 hectares.

## § 3 Percentage problems

### § 3.1 Finding the part, the whole or the rate

Example A (part). 6.5% of $240,000 = 240,000 × 0.065 = $15,600.

Example B (whole). A 4% commission came to $11,200. Price = 11,200 ÷ 0.04 = $280,000. (Rate: $18,000 is what percent of $300,000? 18,000 ÷ 300,000 = 0.06 = 6%.)

### § 3.2 Percent change

Percent change = (new − old) ÷ old. The denominator is always the original value.

Example A. Bought for $320,000, now worth $352,000. (352,000 − 320,000) ÷ 320,000 = 32,000 ÷ 320,000 = 0.10 = 10% increase.

Example B. Value fell from $400,000 to $370,000. (370,000 − 400,000) ÷ 400,000 = −30,000 ÷ 400,000 = −0.075 = 7.5% decrease.

### § 3.3 Working backward from a changed amount

If a final amount is the original plus a percentage of the original, original = final ÷ (1 + rate). If it is the original minus a percentage, original = final ÷ (1 − rate). Never multiply the final amount by the rate.

Example A. A property sold for $324,000, which was 8% more than the owner paid. Paid = 324,000 ÷ 1.08 = $300,000. (Check: 300,000 × 0.08 = 24,000; 300,000 + 24,000 = 324,000.)

Example B. Sold at a 5% loss for $237,500. Paid = 237,500 ÷ 0.95 = $250,000.

## § 4 Commission

### § 4.1 Total commission and co-op split

Commission = sale price × rate. When two brokerages cooperate, the total is split as the listing agreement or MLS offer provides, often but not always 50/50.

Example A. $415,000 × 5.5% = 415,000 × 0.055 = $22,825. Split 50/50: 22,825 ÷ 2 = $11,412.50 to each side.

Example B. A 6% commission on $280,000 = $16,800, with 3.5% offered to the listing side and 2.5% to the selling side. Listing side: 280,000 × 0.035 = $9,800. Selling side: 280,000 × 0.025 = $7,000. Check: 9,800 + 7,000 = 16,800.

### § 4.2 Agent's share of the broker's share

Apply the splits in sequence: price × commission rate × brokerage's share × agent's split. The agent's percentage applies to the brokerage's share, not to the whole commission.

Example A. The selling brokerage receives 3% of a $350,000 sale and its agent is on a 60% split. Brokerage share: 350,000 × 0.03 = $10,500. Agent: 10,500 × 0.60 = $6,300. Brokerage retains 10,500 − 6,300 = $4,200.

Example B. Total commission 6% on $500,000, split 50/50 between brokerages, listing agent on a 70/30 split. 500,000 × 0.06 = 30,000; × 0.50 = 15,000; × 0.70 = $10,500 to the agent; the listing brokerage keeps $4,500.

### § 4.3 Net-to-seller pricing

When a seller wants to net a stated amount after commission, the commission is a percentage of the unknown price, so: price = (desired net + other seller costs) ÷ (1 − commission rate). Steps: add every seller cost that is a fixed dollar amount (loan payoff, closing costs) to the desired net; divide by one minus the rate.

Example A. Net $250,000, closing costs $4,200, commission 6%. Price = (250,000 + 4,200) ÷ (1 − 0.06) = 254,200 ÷ 0.94 = $270,425.53. Check: 270,425.53 × 0.06 = 16,225.53; 270,425.53 − 16,225.53 − 4,200 = $250,000.00.

Example B. Net $90,000, loan payoff $185,000, closing costs $3,500, commission 5%. Price = (90,000 + 185,000 + 3,500) ÷ 0.95 = 278,500 ÷ 0.95 = $293,157.89. Check: 293,157.89 × 0.95 = 278,500.00; less 185,000 and 3,500 leaves $90,000.

### § 4.4 Finding the rate or price from a commission

Example A. A $19,500 commission on a $325,000 sale: 19,500 ÷ 325,000 = 0.06 = 6%.

Example B. An agent received $9,450, which was 70% of the brokerage's 3% share. Brokerage share = 9,450 ÷ 0.70 = $13,500. Price = 13,500 ÷ 0.03 = $450,000.

## § 5 Loan math

### § 5.1 Simple interest

Interest = principal × annual rate × time in years. Monthly interest = principal × annual rate ÷ 12. Loan interest is computed on the outstanding balance, so a month's interest on an amortizing loan uses the balance at the start of that month.

Example A. $312,500 at 6.4%. Annual interest = 312,500 × 0.064 = $20,000. Monthly = 20,000 ÷ 12 = $1,666.67.

Example B. A three-year interest-only loan of $150,000 at 5%. Total interest = 150,000 × 0.05 × 3 = $22,500; monthly payment = 150,000 × 0.05 ÷ 12 = $625.

### § 5.2 Finding principal or rate from an interest payment

Principal = annual interest ÷ rate; rate = annual interest ÷ principal. Multiply a monthly figure by 12 first.

Example A. Monthly interest of $1,125 at 6%. Annual = 1,125 × 12 = 13,500. Principal = 13,500 ÷ 0.06 = $225,000.

Example B. Annual interest of $15,600 on $240,000. Rate = 15,600 ÷ 240,000 = 0.065 = 6.5%.

### § 5.3 Loan-to-value, down payment and CLTV

LTV = loan ÷ the lesser of price or appraised value. Loan = value × LTV. Down payment = price − loan. CLTV = (all liens) ÷ value.

Example A. Price $400,000, appraisal $390,000, loan $312,000. LTV = 312,000 ÷ 390,000 = 0.80 = 80% (the lower appraisal governs). Down payment = 400,000 − 312,000 = $88,000 — the buyer covers the appraisal gap as well as 20% of appraised value.

Example B. A 90% loan on a $275,000 purchase appraised at $275,000: loan = 275,000 × 0.90 = $247,500; down payment = $27,500. If the owner later adds a $30,000 HELOC when the first balance is $240,000 and value is $300,000, CLTV = (240,000 + 30,000) ÷ 300,000 = 0.90 = 90%.

### § 5.4 Points

One point = 1% of the loan amount, never of the price. Dollar cost = loan × points ÷ 100. Points = dollar cost ÷ loan × 100.

Example A. A buyer pays 2 discount points on an 80% loan for a $350,000 purchase. Loan = 350,000 × 0.80 = 280,000. Points = 280,000 × 0.02 = $5,600.

Example B. A borrower paid $6,375 in points on a $255,000 loan. 6,375 ÷ 255,000 = 0.025 = 2.5 points. A 1% origination fee plus 1.5 discount points on a $300,000 loan = 300,000 × 0.025 = $7,500.

### § 5.5 Payment from an amortization factor

Problems supply a factor: the monthly payment per $1,000 borrowed for a given rate and term. Payment = (loan ÷ 1,000) × factor.

Example A. $250,000 for 30 years at 6.5%; factor 6.3207. Payment = 250 × 6.3207 = $1,580.18 (rounded from 1,580.175).

Example B. $185,000 for 15 years at 5.5%; factor 8.1708. Payment = 185 × 8.1708 = $1,511.60 (rounded from 1,511.598).

### § 5.6 Interest and principal split; balance after a payment

Steps: (1) month's interest = balance × annual rate ÷ 12; (2) principal paid = payment − interest; (3) new balance = balance − principal paid. Repeat with the new balance for the next month.

Example A. $250,000 at 6.5%, payment $1,580.18. Month 1 interest = 250,000 × 0.065 ÷ 12 = 16,250 ÷ 12 = $1,354.17. Principal = 1,580.18 − 1,354.17 = $226.01. Balance = 250,000 − 226.01 = $249,773.99. Month 2 interest = 249,773.99 × 0.065 ÷ 12 = 16,235.31 ÷ 12 = $1,352.94; principal = 1,580.18 − 1,352.94 = $227.24; balance = $249,546.75.

Example B. $185,000 at 5.5%, payment $1,511.60. Interest = 185,000 × 0.055 ÷ 12 = 10,175 ÷ 12 = $847.92. Principal = 1,511.60 − 847.92 = $663.68. Balance = 185,000 − 663.68 = $184,336.32.

### § 5.7 Total interest over the life of a loan

Total interest = payment × number of payments − original principal.

Example A. 1,580.18 × 360 = 568,864.80; − 250,000 = $318,864.80.

Example B. 1,511.60 × 180 = 272,088.00; − 185,000 = $87,088.00.

### § 5.8 PITI

PITI = monthly principal and interest + annual taxes ÷ 12 + annual insurance ÷ 12 (+ monthly mortgage insurance and association dues when the problem includes them).

Example A. P&I $1,580.18; taxes $4,200 per year; insurance $1,440 per year. 4,200 ÷ 12 = 350; 1,440 ÷ 12 = 120. PITI = 1,580.18 + 350 + 120 = $2,050.18.

Example B. P&I $1,511.60; taxes $3,000 ÷ 12 = 250; insurance $900 ÷ 12 = 75; PMI $92.50. Total = 1,511.60 + 250 + 75 + 92.50 = $1,929.10.

### § 5.9 Qualifying ratios

Front-end: maximum housing payment = gross monthly income × housing ratio. Back-end: maximum total obligations = gross monthly income × total ratio; subtract other monthly debts to get the housing payment that ratio allows. The borrower qualifies for the lower of the two housing figures. To find the income needed for a given payment, divide the payment by the ratio.

Example A. Income $96,000 per year = $8,000 per month; ratios 28/36; other debts $750 per month. Front: 8,000 × 0.28 = $2,240. Back: 8,000 × 0.36 = 2,880 − 750 = $2,130. Maximum PITI = $2,130 (the lower).

Example B. Income $6,500 per month; ratios 31/43; debts $450. Front: 6,500 × 0.31 = $2,015. Back: 6,500 × 0.43 = 2,795 − 450 = $2,345. Maximum PITI = $2,015. Income needed to carry a $2,050.18 PITI at 28%: 2,050.18 ÷ 0.28 = $7,322.07 per month = $87,864.86 per year.

### § 5.10 Prepayment penalties

Penalty = balance × penalty percentage, or a stated number of months of interest on the balance.

Example A. 2% of a $214,600 balance = 214,600 × 0.02 = $4,292.

Example B. Six months' interest on $180,000 at 6%: monthly interest = 180,000 × 0.06 ÷ 12 = $900; penalty = 900 × 6 = $5,400.

### § 5.11 Escrow reserves (impounds) at closing

Monthly escrow deposit = annual tax (or premium) ÷ 12. Initial deposit at closing = monthly amount × months required. Federal rules limit the cushion a servicer may hold (see cached RESPA text); problems state the months.

Example A. Taxes $3,600 per year (300 per month) and insurance $1,200 per year (100 per month); the lender collects 3 months of each. (300 + 100) × 3 = $1,200.

Example B. Taxes $4,800 per year; 6 months required. 4,800 ÷ 12 = 400 × 6 = $2,400.

## § 6 Appreciation and depreciation

### § 6.1 Straight-line depreciation (cost approach)

Annual depreciation = improvement cost ÷ total economic life (land is never depreciated). Accrued depreciation = annual depreciation × effective age. Depreciated value = cost − accrued depreciation. Equivalent percentage method: annual rate = 100% ÷ economic life.

Example A. Reproduction cost $300,000, economic life 50 years, effective age 12. Annual = 300,000 ÷ 50 = $6,000 (2% per year). Accrued = 6,000 × 12 = $72,000. Depreciated value = 300,000 − 72,000 = $228,000.

Example B. Cost $180,000, life 40 years, age 8. Annual = 180,000 ÷ 40 = $4,500. Accrued = 4,500 × 8 = $36,000. Value = $144,000.

### § 6.2 Tax depreciation of investment property

For federal income tax the building portion of the basis (not land) is depreciated straight-line over 27.5 years for residential rental property and 39 years for nonresidential property (IRS Publication 946; the first and last years use a mid-month convention that examination problems normally ignore).

Example A. A rental house with a $275,000 building basis: 275,000 ÷ 27.5 = $10,000 per year.

Example B. An office building with a $780,000 building basis: 780,000 ÷ 39 = $20,000 per year. If land is $200,000 of a $980,000 total price, only the $780,000 building is depreciated.

### § 6.3 Appreciation

Compound (each year's percentage applies to the prior year's value): value × (1 + rate) repeated once per year. Simple (each year's percentage applies to the original value): value × (1 + rate × years). Examination problems that say "appreciated 4% per year" are usually compound; "appreciated a total of 12%" is a single multiplication.

Example A. $300,000 at 4% per year for 3 years, compound. 300,000 × 1.04 = 312,000; × 1.04 = 324,480; × 1.04 = $337,459.20. Simple: 300,000 × (1 + 0.04 × 3) = 300,000 × 1.12 = $336,000.

Example B. $250,000 at 5% for 2 years: 250,000 × 1.05 = 262,500; × 1.05 = $275,625.

### § 6.4 Depreciation by percentage per year

Straight: value × (1 − rate × years). Compound: value × (1 − rate) repeated.

Example A. $400,000 loses 3% per year for 5 years, straight: 3% × 5 = 15%; 400,000 × 0.85 = $340,000.

Example B. Same figures compounded: 0.97 × 0.97 = 0.9409; × 0.97 = 0.912673; × 0.97 = 0.88529281; × 0.97 = 0.8587340. 400,000 × 0.8587340 = $343,493.61.

### § 6.5 Working backward to the original value

Original = current ÷ (1 + total appreciation) or current ÷ (1 − total depreciation).

Example A. Now worth $345,000 after total appreciation of 15%: 345,000 ÷ 1.15 = $300,000.

Example B. Now worth $220,000 after a 12% loss: 220,000 ÷ 0.88 = $250,000.

## § 7 Investment measures

### § 7.1 From potential gross income to net operating income

Potential gross income (PGI) = all units × rent × 12 (full occupancy). Effective gross income (EGI) = PGI − vacancy and collection loss + other income. Net operating income (NOI) = EGI − operating expenses. Operating expenses include taxes, insurance, utilities, maintenance, management and reserves for replacement. They exclude debt service (mortgage payments), depreciation, income taxes and capital improvements. Unless the problem says otherwise, apply the vacancy percentage to potential rental income.

Example A. 12 units at $1,250 per month; 5% vacancy; other income $6,000; operating expenses $68,000. PGI = 12 × 1,250 × 12 = $180,000. Vacancy = 180,000 × 0.05 = 9,000. EGI = 180,000 − 9,000 + 6,000 = $177,000. NOI = 177,000 − 68,000 = $109,000.

Example B. PGI $96,000; vacancy 8%; operating expenses $35,000; annual debt service $38,400. Vacancy = 96,000 × 0.08 = 7,680. EGI = $88,320. NOI = 88,320 − 35,000 = $53,320. Debt service is not subtracted.

### § 7.2 Capitalization rate: I = R × V

Income = Rate × Value; Value = Income ÷ Rate; Rate = Income ÷ Value. Income means NOI. Value moves inversely to the rate: a higher cap rate produces a lower value for the same income.

Example A. NOI $109,000 at an 8% cap rate: value = 109,000 ÷ 0.08 = $1,362,500.

Example B. NOI $53,320 on a $666,500 price: rate = 53,320 ÷ 666,500 = 0.08 = 8%. Income required from a $900,000 property at 7.5%: 900,000 × 0.075 = $67,500.

### § 7.3 Gross rent multiplier and gross income multiplier

GRM = price ÷ monthly gross rent (used for one- to four-unit residences). GIM = price ÷ annual gross income (used for larger income property). Value = rent × multiplier. Multipliers use gross income, not NOI.

Example A. Comparable rentals show a GRM of 140; the subject rents for $2,100 per month. Value = 2,100 × 140 = $294,000.

Example B. A duplex sold for $285,000 and rents for $1,900 per month: GRM = 285,000 ÷ 1,900 = 150. A building sold for $1,200,000 with annual gross income of $150,000: GIM = 1,200,000 ÷ 150,000 = 8.0.

### § 7.4 Before-tax cash flow and cash-on-cash return

Before-tax cash flow (BTCF) = NOI − annual debt service. Cash-on-cash return = BTCF ÷ cash invested (down payment plus closing costs).

Example A. NOI $53,320; debt service $3,200 × 12 = $38,400; cash invested $170,000. BTCF = 53,320 − 38,400 = $14,920. Return = 14,920 ÷ 170,000 = 0.08776 → 8.78%.

Example B. BTCF $22,500 on $250,000 invested: 22,500 ÷ 250,000 = 0.09 = 9%.

### § 7.5 Equity

Equity = current market value − all loan balances.

Example A. Value $480,000; first mortgage $310,000; HELOC $25,000. Equity = 480,000 − 310,000 − 25,000 = $145,000.

Example B. Bought for $300,000 with 20% down ($60,000); now worth $360,000 with a $235,000 balance. Equity = 360,000 − 235,000 = $125,000.

### § 7.6 Return on investment and profit

ROI or percent profit = (amount received − total cost) ÷ total cost. Total cost includes the price paid plus capital improvements and, if the problem includes them, acquisition and selling costs.

Example A. Paid $200,000, spent $30,000 on improvements, sold for $276,000. Cost = 230,000. Profit = 276,000 − 230,000 = 46,000. ROI = 46,000 ÷ 230,000 = 0.20 = 20%.

Example B. Paid $200,000, sold for $180,000. (180,000 − 200,000) ÷ 200,000 = −0.10 = a 10% loss.

### § 7.7 Breakeven occupancy

Breakeven occupancy = (operating expenses + annual debt service) ÷ PGI. It is the occupancy at which cash flow is zero.

Example A. Expenses $35,000; debt service $38,400; PGI $96,000. (35,000 + 38,400) ÷ 96,000 = 73,400 ÷ 96,000 = 0.7646 → 76.46%.

Example B. Expenses $68,000; debt service $82,000; PGI $180,000. 150,000 ÷ 180,000 = 0.8333 → 83.33%.

## § 8 Valuation math

### § 8.1 Sales comparison adjustments

Adjust the comparable, never the subject, so that the comparable becomes like the subject. If the comparable has a feature the subject lacks (comparable superior), subtract the feature's value from the comparable's price. If the comparable lacks a feature the subject has (comparable inferior), add. Time adjustments for a rising market are added to older sales.

Example A. Comparable sold for $310,000 three months ago; it has a garage worth $15,000 that the subject lacks; the subject has a fireplace worth $3,000 that the comparable lacks; the market has risen 0.5% per month. Garage: −15,000. Fireplace: +3,000. Time: 310,000 × 0.005 × 3 = +4,650. Adjusted price = 310,000 − 15,000 + 3,000 + 4,650 = $302,650.

Example B. Comparable sold for $425,000 with one more bedroom than the subject (worth $12,000) and a smaller lot (worth $8,000 less). Bedroom: −12,000. Lot: +8,000. Adjusted = 425,000 − 12,000 + 8,000 = $421,000.

### § 8.2 Cost approach

Value = land value + (reproduction or replacement cost of improvements − accrued depreciation). Land is valued separately by sales comparison and is not depreciated.

Example A. Land $90,000; 2,200 sq ft at $150 per sq ft; effective age 10 of a 55-year life. Cost = 2,200 × 150 = $330,000. Depreciation = 330,000 × 10 ÷ 55 = $60,000. Value = 90,000 + (330,000 − 60,000) = $360,000.

Example B. Land $120,000; replacement cost $400,000; depreciation 2.5% per year for 8 years = 20%. Depreciation = 400,000 × 0.20 = $80,000. Value = 120,000 + 320,000 = $440,000.

### § 8.3 Income approach

Direct capitalization: value = NOI ÷ cap rate (§ 7.2). For small residential rentals, value = monthly rent × GRM (§ 7.3).

Example A. NOI $72,000 at 9%: 72,000 ÷ 0.09 = $800,000.

Example B. A single-family rental at $1,650 per month in a market where GRM is 160: 1,650 × 160 = $264,000.

### § 8.4 Reconciliation

The final value estimate weights the three approaches by their reliability for the property type; it is not a simple average. An item that averages three indicated values is testing a misconception.

## § 9 Property tax

### § 9.1 Assessed value

Assessed value = market value × assessment ratio (100% in some jurisdictions, a fraction in others).

Example A. Market value $300,000 × 40% = $120,000 assessed.

Example B. Market value $275,000 × 35% = $96,250 assessed.

### § 9.2 Tax rates: mills, per $100, per $1,000, percent

One mill = one-tenth of a cent = $1 of tax per $1,000 of assessed value = 0.001. Tax = assessed value × mills ÷ 1,000. Per $100: tax = assessed value ÷ 100 × rate. Per $1,000: tax = assessed value ÷ 1,000 × rate.

Example A. $120,000 assessed at 32 mills: 120,000 × 0.032 = $3,840. Monthly escrow = 3,840 ÷ 12 = $320.

Example B. $250,000 assessed at $2.40 per $100: 250,000 ÷ 100 = 2,500 × 2.40 = $6,000. $200,000 assessed at $18.50 per $1,000: 200 × 18.50 = $3,700.

### § 9.3 Exemptions

Subtract the exemption from assessed value before applying the rate.

Example A. Assessed $120,000, homestead exemption $25,000, 32 mills. (120,000 − 25,000) × 0.032 = 95,000 × 0.032 = $3,040.

Example B. Assessed $96,250, exemption $15,000, 41 mills. 81,250 × 0.041 = $3,331.25.

### § 9.4 Equalization factor

Multiply assessed value by the equalization factor, then subtract exemptions if any, then apply the rate.

Example A. $120,000 × 1.15 = $138,000 × 0.032 = $4,416.

Example B. $96,250 × 0.90 = $86,625 × 0.041 = $3,551.63 (rounded from 3,551.625).

### § 9.5 Special assessments

Special assessments for local improvements are often levied per front foot.

Example A. A sidewalk costs $48 per front foot; a 90-foot lot pays 90 × 48 = $4,320. If the city pays half, the owner pays $2,160.

Example B. A sewer assessment of $12,000 is spread over 10 annual installments with 6% interest on the unpaid balance; the first year's payment is 1,200 principal + 12,000 × 0.06 = 720 interest = $1,920.

## § 10 Transfer taxes and recording fees

### § 10.1 Method

Transfer (documentary stamp, conveyance) taxes are stated as a dollar amount per increment of consideration — per $100, per $500 or per $1,000 — often "or fraction thereof," which means round the number of increments up. Steps: divide the taxable consideration by the increment; round up if any fraction remains; multiply by the rate. Which party pays, whether an assumed loan is excluded, and whether a separate mortgage or intangible tax applies to the loan all vary by state; the state portion governs.

Example A. $0.70 per $100 or fraction thereof on $253,450. 253,450 ÷ 100 = 2,534.5 → 2,535 increments × 0.70 = $1,774.50.

Example B. $1.50 per $500 or fraction thereof on $312,300. 312,300 ÷ 500 = 624.6 → 625 × 1.50 = $937.50. If the same rate were stated as $3.00 per $1,000 with no rounding, the tax would be 312.3 × 3.00 = $936.90; the wording decides.

### § 10.2 Recording fees

Recording fees are flat charges per document or per page, not percentages.

Example A. $30 for the first page and $10 for each additional page: a 4-page deed costs 30 + 3 × 10 = $60.

Example B. Deed (4 pages) and mortgage (12 pages) at the same schedule: 60 + (30 + 11 × 10) = 60 + 140 = $200.

## § 11 Prorations

### § 11.1 Two calendar conventions

The 365-day (actual) method uses the true number of days in each month and divides annual amounts by 365 (366 in a leap year if the problem says so). The 360-day (banker's, statutory) method treats every month as 30 days and the year as 360 days. The problem states which to use; answers differ slightly, so do not mix them.

### § 11.2 The closing day

By one convention the seller owns the property through the day of closing, so the seller is charged for the closing day; by the other the buyer owns the closing day. Both exist in practice and the examination states which applies. When the problem is silent, the more common textbook assumption is that the seller is responsible for the day of closing.

### § 11.3 Items paid in arrears (property taxes)

The seller has used part of the year but the bill will arrive after closing and be paid by the buyer, so the seller owes the buyer the seller's share: debit seller, credit buyer. Steps: annual amount ÷ days in year = daily rate; count the seller's days from January 1 through the closing date (or the day before, depending on § 11.2); multiply.

Example A. Taxes $3,650 per year, closing April 15, 365-day method, seller owns the closing day. Daily = 3,650 ÷ 365 = $10.00. Seller's days: January 31 + February 28 + March 31 + April 15 = 105. Proration = 105 × 10.00 = $1,050.00, debit seller / credit buyer. Under the 360-day method: daily = 3,650 ÷ 360 = 10.13889; days = 3 × 30 + 15 = 105; proration = 105 × 10.13889 = $1,064.58.

Example B. Taxes $4,380, closing September 10, 365-day method, buyer owns the closing day. Daily = 4,380 ÷ 365 = $12.00. Seller's days: January through August = 31 + 28 + 31 + 30 + 31 + 30 + 31 + 31 = 243, plus September 1–9 = 9, total 252. Proration = 252 × 12.00 = $3,024.00, debit seller / credit buyer. Under the 360-day method: 8 × 30 + 9 = 249 days × (4,380 ÷ 360 = 12.16667) = $3,029.50.

### § 11.4 Items paid in advance (prepaid dues, prepaid taxes, insurance)

The seller has paid for time the buyer will enjoy, so the buyer owes the seller the unused share: credit seller, debit buyer. Count the buyer's days from the day after closing (or the closing day, per § 11.2) through the end of the paid period. Hazard insurance policies today are usually cancelled and refunded by the insurer rather than prorated; a problem that prorates insurance uses the same method.

Example A. Annual association dues of $1,200 were prepaid for the calendar year; closing June 30; 360-day method; seller owns the closing day. Buyer's days = 6 × 30 = 180. Daily = 1,200 ÷ 360 = 3.33333. Proration = 180 × 3.33333 = $600.00, credit seller / debit buyer.

Example B. Taxes of $2,920 were prepaid for the calendar year; closing October 20; 365-day method; seller owns the closing day. Buyer's days = October 21–31 (11) + November (30) + December (31) = 72. Daily = 2,920 ÷ 365 = $8.00. Proration = 72 × 8.00 = $576.00, credit seller / debit buyer.

### § 11.5 Rent and security deposits

Rent is paid in advance, so rent the seller collected for days after closing belongs to the buyer: debit seller, credit buyer. Tenants' security deposits are transferred in full to the buyer, who must eventually return them: debit seller, credit buyer for the whole amount (handling varies by state; the state portion governs).

Example A. Rent of $1,800 was collected on the 1st; closing on the 20th of a 30-day month; seller owns the closing day. Buyer's days = 10. Daily = 1,800 ÷ 30 = $60. Proration = 10 × 60 = $600, debit seller / credit buyer.

Example B. Four units at $1,500 each, rents collected September 1, closing September 12, actual-day method (September has 30 days), seller owns the closing day. Monthly rent = 6,000; daily = 6,000 ÷ 30 = $200; buyer's days = 18. Proration = 18 × 200 = $3,600, debit seller / credit buyer. Security deposits of $6,000 are a further debit seller / credit buyer of $6,000.

### § 11.6 Interest: assumed loans and the buyer's new loan

Mortgage interest is paid in arrears (the payment due on the 1st covers the prior month). On an assumed loan the seller owes the buyer interest from the last payment date through closing: debit seller, credit buyer. On the buyer's new loan, the lender collects prepaid (per diem) interest from the closing date through the end of the month, so the first regular payment can fall on the first of the second month; this is a buyer debit paid to the lender, not a proration between the parties.

Example A. Assumed balance $150,000 at 6%, closing on the 16th, 30-day month, seller owns the closing day. Monthly interest = 150,000 × 0.06 ÷ 12 = $750; daily = 750 ÷ 30 = $25; seller's days = 16. Proration = 16 × 25 = $400, debit seller / credit buyer.

Example B. New loan $280,000 at 6.5%, closing June 20, first payment August 1, lender charges interest from and including the closing day. Daily = 280,000 × 0.065 ÷ 365 = 18,200 ÷ 365 = $49.863. Days June 20–30 = 11. Prepaid interest = 11 × 49.863 = $548.49, debit buyer.

### § 11.7 Summary of directions

Accrued but unpaid (taxes in arrears, interest on an assumed loan, utilities billed later): debit seller, credit buyer. Prepaid by seller (dues, prepaid taxes, insurance if prorated): credit seller, debit buyer. Rent collected in advance and security deposits: debit seller, credit buyer. Prepaid interest on the buyer's new loan: debit buyer only.

## § 12 Closing statements

### § 12.1 Debit and credit logic

Debit = a charge against a party; credit = an amount in a party's favor. Single-entry items appear on one side only: the buyer's new loan and earnest money (buyer credits); the seller's loan payoff and commission (seller debits); each party's own closing costs. The purchase price and the prorations are double entries: price is a seller credit and a buyer debit; a proration owed by the seller is a seller debit and a buyer credit for the same amount. Seller's net proceeds = seller credits − seller debits. Buyer's cash to close = buyer debits − buyer credits.

### § 12.2 Seller net sheet (worked)

Price $350,000; commission 5.5%; loan payoff $212,400; owner's title policy $1,750 (seller custom in this problem); transfer tax $0.70 per $100 = 3,500 increments × 0.70 = $2,450; tax proration owed to buyer $1,050 (§ 11.3 Example A); settlement fee $600; repair credit to buyer $1,500.

Commission = 350,000 × 0.055 = $19,250.
Seller debits: 19,250 + 212,400 + 1,750 + 2,450 + 1,050 + 600 + 1,500 = $239,000.
Seller credits: $350,000.
Net proceeds = 350,000 − 239,000 = $111,000.

### § 12.3 Buyer cash to close (worked)

Same sale. Loan $280,000 (80%); earnest money $10,000 already deposited; 1% origination fee; appraisal $600; lender's title policy $900; recording $150; prepaid interest $548.49 (§ 11.6 Example B); tax escrow 3 months at 3,650 ÷ 12 = 304.1667 per month = $912.50; first-year hazard premium $1,440 plus 2 months' reserve at 120 = $240; tax proration credit $1,050; repair credit $1,500.

Origination = 280,000 × 0.01 = $2,800.
Buyer debits: 350,000 + 2,800 + 600 + 900 + 150 + 548.49 + 912.50 + 1,440 + 240 = $357,590.99.
Buyer credits: 280,000 + 10,000 + 1,050 + 1,500 = $292,550.00.
Cash to close = 357,590.99 − 292,550.00 = $65,040.99.

Check on the down payment alone: 350,000 − 280,000 = 70,000; less earnest money 10,000 = 60,000 still owed toward price; plus closing costs and prepaids of 7,590.99; less seller credits of 2,550; equals 65,040.99.

## § 13 Property management and rental math

### § 13.1 Rent per square foot

Commercial rent is quoted per square foot per year unless stated monthly. Annual rent = area × rate; monthly = annual ÷ 12. Rate = annual rent ÷ area.

Example A. 2,400 sq ft of retail space at $22 per sq ft per year: 2,400 × 22 = $52,800 per year = 52,800 ÷ 12 = $4,400 per month.

Example B. An office rents for $3,750 per month and contains 1,500 sq ft. Annual = 3,750 × 12 = 45,000; rate = 45,000 ÷ 1,500 = $30 per sq ft per year.

### § 13.2 Vacancy, occupancy and management fees

Vacancy rate = vacant units ÷ total units (or vacant rent ÷ potential rent). Occupancy = 1 − vacancy. Management fees are usually a percentage of rent actually collected (EGI).

Example A. 120 units with 9 vacant: 9 ÷ 120 = 0.075 = 7.5% vacancy; occupancy 92.5%.

Example B. Management fee of 6% on collected rents of $88,320: 88,320 × 0.06 = $5,299.20. Operating expense ratio = operating expenses ÷ EGI = 35,000 ÷ 88,320 = 0.3963 → 39.63%.

### § 13.3 Budgeting rent to reach a target

Work upward from the target: required EGI = target NOI + operating expenses; required PGI = EGI ÷ (1 − vacancy rate); rent per unit per month = PGI ÷ units ÷ 12.

Example A. Target NOI $60,000, expenses $35,000, vacancy 8%, 10 units. EGI = 95,000. PGI = 95,000 ÷ 0.92 = $103,260.87. Per unit per month = 103,260.87 ÷ 10 ÷ 12 = $860.51.

Example B. An owner wants a 9% return on a $500,000 building with $22,000 of expenses and 5% vacancy, 6 units. NOI = 500,000 × 0.09 = 45,000. EGI = 67,000. PGI = 67,000 ÷ 0.95 = $70,526.32. Per unit per month = 70,526.32 ÷ 6 ÷ 12 = $979.53.

## § 14 Profit and loss

### § 14.1 Percent profit or loss on cost

Percent = (sale price − total cost) ÷ total cost (§ 7.6).

Example A. Bought $180,000, improvements $25,000, sold $246,000. Cost 205,000; profit 41,000; 41,000 ÷ 205,000 = 0.20 = 20%.

Example B. Bought $260,000, sold $234,000: −26,000 ÷ 260,000 = −0.10 = 10% loss.

### § 14.2 Finding cost from a stated profit or loss

Cost = sale price ÷ (1 + profit rate) or ÷ (1 − loss rate) (§ 3.3).

Example A. Sold at a 15% profit for $287,500: 287,500 ÷ 1.15 = $250,000.

Example B. Sold at a 10% loss for $234,000: 234,000 ÷ 0.90 = $260,000.

## § 15 Common traps

### § 15.1 Wrong base

Points are a percentage of the loan, not the price. The agent's split is a percentage of the brokerage's share, not the whole commission. Percent change and percent profit divide by the original value or cost, never the new value. Tax exemptions reduce assessed value, not the tax.

### § 15.2 Net-to-seller shortcut

Multiplying the desired net by (1 + rate) understates the price; divide by (1 − rate) instead (§ 4.3).

### § 15.3 NOI contamination

Debt service, depreciation, income taxes and capital improvements are not operating expenses. A problem that lists a mortgage payment among the expenses is testing whether you leave it out (§ 7.1).

### § 15.4 Multipliers versus cap rates

GRM uses monthly gross rent; GIM uses annual gross income; cap rates use annual NOI. Dividing NOI by a GRM, or monthly rent by a cap rate, is a common wrong-answer pattern (§ 7.2–§ 7.3).

### § 15.5 Adjustment direction

Adjust the comparable toward the subject: comparable better, subtract; comparable inferior, add. Never adjust the subject (§ 8.1).

### § 15.6 Proration direction and day count

Arrears items are seller debit / buyer credit; prepaid items are seller credit / buyer debit. Read whether the seller or the buyer owns the closing day and whether the year is 360 or 365 days; count actual days per month under the 365-day method (§ 11).

### § 15.7 Single-entry closing items

The buyer's loan and earnest money are buyer credits with no seller entry; the seller's payoff and commission are seller debits with no buyer entry. Prepaid interest on the new loan is a buyer debit to the lender, not a credit to the seller (§ 12.1).

### § 15.8 Units

Convert inches to feet and square feet to acres before answering; a lot's area in square feet divided by 43,560 gives acres, and multiplying denominators of a government survey description gives the divisor of 640 (§ 2.6). Cubic yards are cubic feet ÷ 27, not ÷ 9.

### § 15.9 LTV base

LTV is measured against the lesser of price or appraised value; when the appraisal is low the buyer's cash requirement rises (§ 5.3).

### § 15.10 Straight-line versus compound

"X% per year of original value" is straight-line; "X% per year" applied to a growing or shrinking balance is compound; the two answers differ and both may appear as choices (§ 6.3–§ 6.4).
